〜 Jurassic

Daohugou, ~165 Ma — a forest you can walk around in, in the dark, by ear ↩ mino.mobi

Nine ensiferans sang in this forest, and we know roughly what each of them sounded like. Five called in low pure tones below 7 kHz; three sang above 10 kHz; and one — Sigmaboilus peregrinus — sang at 22.5 kHz, above the top of human hearing. That is the oldest ultrasonic communication known from any animal, about a hundred and ten million years before the first bat. Press play and walk about. Every call here is synthesised from its own instrument — the tooth-by-tooth spacing of that species' fossilised file, driving a wing resonator at its published sharpness — and then thrown across the air by ISO 9613, with the distance, the weather and the leaves all taken off it.

The gap this closes

Katydid ultrasound has usually been explained as an answer to bats. These fossils are a hundred and ten million years too early for that, and Gu et al. say so plainly: they reject the hypothesis that bats were the sole driver of ultrasound evolution in katydids, and propose instead that early mammals and non-mammalian ancestors were already listening in. Bats, in their phrase, emerged into a soundscape already busy with ultrasounds.

That is why the who is listening control matters more than it looks. Switch between an early mammal and an archosaur and watch the ultrasonic circle swell and vanish: against one ear the pitch bought no privacy at all, against the other it bought a private channel. The fossils do not settle it. The physics only tells you what each answer costs — and the Q panel shows the other half of the same defence, the one that does not depend on pitch at all.

What is published here, and what is not

The nine species on this page are the nine of Gu et al. 2026, Reconstruction of an extinct soundscape reveals ultrasonic communication in the Jurassic, PNAS 123(36):e2615107123 (open access, CC BY 4.0): twenty fossil forewings from the Jiulongshan Formation at Daohugou, seven prophalangopsids and two haglids, co-occurring at 157–165 Ma. Their carrier frequencies and quality factors are that paper's, from the FFT of each reconstructed syllable (their Fig. 4D); the wing resonances are their finite-element eigenfrequencies (Fig. 3); the tooth counts and file shapes are read off their Fig. 4B, tooth by tooth. Those are tagged published and digitised wherever they appear.

What is ours, and tagged modelled: source levels, chirp rhythm, and how many males stand on the plot. The paper predicts syllable rates with a Gaussian-process model, but reports them in its SI Appendix, which we did not have — so the rhythm you hear is plausible rather than theirs. The four audiograms are weaker still: no ear fossilises, so they are tagged hypothesis and are best read as positions in the argument you can switch between.

The propagation is not a guess. Spreading, atmospheric absorption and foliage scattering are ISO 9613-1:1993 and ISO 9613-2:1996, implemented in Rust and checked against the standard's own published table at four temperature–humidity conditions across eight octaves. That test runs in CI. If it ever fails, every radius on this page is wrong and the build goes red.

Earlier work this builds on: Gu, Engel & Ren 2012, PNAS 109:3868, which reconstructed the ~6.4 kHz song of Archaboilus musicus from a file of 107 teeth — the first extinct animal whose song was recovered from the instrument that made it, and the precedent this assemblage extends.

Sound and physics: a zero-dependency Rust kernel compiled to raw wasm32-unknown-unknown, run from an AudioWorklet — no wasm-bindgen, no allocator on the audio thread.
