table.

Procedural character sheets for tabletop RPGs — built only on systems whose rules text is openly licensed, so the actual tables can ship with the generator.

Live

/cairn Cairn Second Edition · Yochai Gal CC BY-SA 4.0
A complete character in one throw: d20 background, 3d6 attributes, 1d6 hit protection, starting gear packed into the ten inventory slots, both background tables, a bond, an omen, and every die that produced them. Roll a party of six and the youngest reads the omen aloud. Prints as a sheet.
/cairn/kit who should carry the bomb measurement
The step nobody writes down: between the roll and the dungeon, somebody bought the shield and somebody ended up with the bomb. Hand a haul round and each thing lands with whoever it saves the most — measured, by playing the fights with and without it, in the same currency the oracle reports. Cairn has no classes, so there is no taxonomy to look a specialty up in; the specialty is whatever the simulation finds. Two things make it honest rather than merely confident: anything worth less than the slot it costs is left on the floor and said so, and where the measurement genuinely cannot separate two delvers the page calls it a tie, breaks it by a stated rule, and tells you it did.
/cairn/encounter the oracle simulation
Cairn publishes no challenge rating and no XP budget, on purpose — so this doesn't look difficulty up, it plays the fight five thousand times against your actual party, under Cairn's own combat rules, and reports the wipe rate, the expected casualties and the round the first body drops. Then it searches all 84 bestiary creatures for the encounters that land in the band you asked for. Cairn has no levels either, so the party ages by delves: each expedition rolls both a scar, which raises a maximum, and loot, which has to fit in ten slots. Watch six goblins go from deadly to routine.
/cairn/arena watch it, or fight it simulation
A percentage is not a feeling. Take any encounter the oracle has weighed and watch one of those fights play out round by round — who swings, what the die said, how much the armour stopped, what overflowed into STR — or play it yourself and choose every action. Both run the same generator with the same dice; the only difference is who decides, so nothing on screen is drawn by a second implementation. Piloting adds exactly one move the simulator never makes: withdrawing. Cairn is a game about leaving, the oracle stands and fights by definition, and putting those side by side is the clearest possible statement of why its number is a floor rather than a forecast.
/cairn/items what a slot is worth measurement
Cairn has no caster class: magic is objects, and objects are capped at ten slots. So the question about an item is not what it does but what it does per slot — which the oracle can answer, by handing the party the item and re-running the same fights on the same dice. Every weapon, every piece of armour and all 46 relics, priced in the same currency as an encounter. It also finds the cliff: the load at which picking one more thing up makes a party measurably worse, because a full pack is 0 HP.
/srd5 System Reference Document 5.2.1 · roller CC BY 4.0
A whole party from one seed, 5E-compatible. Species, class and background are drawn and ability scores rolled the SRD's own way — four d6, keep the highest three — and then every other number is computed from the rules: armour class from the armour actually worn and the Dexterity actually rolled, hit points from the hit die and Constitution, every save and skill, attack and damage per weapon, spell save DC. Open the dice on any sheet to see all four of every throw, including the one that was dropped. Behind it sits the corpus: 330 stat blocks and 423 attacks recovered from a 364-page PDF under 189 assertions — which found seven swarms welded onto their neighbours, eighty attacks truncated at a line break, and one error in the document itself.
/mausritter Mausritter · Isaac Williams CC BY 4.0
Still a candidate, no longer next. Same skeleton as Cairn, different bones — mice, six-slot pack-and-paw inventory, spells as physical objects, and the best map in this whole licence survey. What it has not got is classes, which is what pushed the SRD ahead of it.

Why these systems and not that one

You cannot copyright a game's mechanics — rolling three six-sided dice, comparing a d20 to a score. You can absolutely copyright the text that expresses them: the table entries, the class write-ups, the monster prose, the names, the setting. A generator is almost entirely that second thing. A character roller worth using has to reproduce the tables, so the only honest way to build one is on rules text that has been deliberately opened.

Since the OGL crisis of January 2023 there is a lot of it. This is the shortlist, and what each licence actually asks of a site like this one:

The rule this surface keeps: if a system's own text ships in the generator, its attribution ships beside it — in the data file, in the page footer, and in a LICENSE.md next to the tables. Share-alike systems keep their share-alike: the Cairn data here is offered back under CC BY-SA 4.0.

How the generators work

  • The seed is the character. Every sheet is a pure function of a short string, so #s=oak-fen-317 rolls the same person for everyone, forever. Hand a party to a table as a URL rather than six PDFs.
  • The dice are shown. Each sheet carries the roll log that produced it — d20 → 14, 3d6 → 4+5+2. A generator you cannot audit is a generator you cannot trust at a table where the dice matter.
  • Rules, not just randomness. Inventory slots, bulk, petty items, armour caps and encumbrance are computed by the system's own rules, not printed as a list for you to total up.
  • No backend. Everything runs in the browser. No account, no storage, nothing sent anywhere. Print the sheet and the site has done its job.
  • Where a system withholds a number, compute it — and say so. Cairn gives a Warden no way to price an encounter, so the oracle simulates one; but the bands, the veterancy curve and the arithmetic are ours, they are labelled as ours, and every verdict says which creatures have abilities the model cannot see.
