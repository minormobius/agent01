Two orthonormal bases of ℂ⁶ are mutually unbiased when every vector of one has |⟨b, c⟩|² = 1/6 with every vector of the other. Measure a state from one basis in the other and all six outcomes are equally likely. These three, from the paper's Lemma (the Klappenecker–Rötteler tensor construction), are U_r = A_r ⊗ B_r:

A₀ = I₂   A₁ = ¹⁄√2 [1 1; 1 −1]   A₂ = ¹⁄√2 [1 1; i −i]
B₀ = I₃   B₁ = F = ¹⁄√3 (ω^{jk})   B₂ = DF,  D = diag(1, ω, ω)

Every entry is a 12th root of unity divided by 1, or by √6. So each of the 216 inner products (six blocks of 36, above) is computed exactly, in the ring ℤ[ζ] with ζ = e^{iπ/6}, ζ⁴ = ζ² − 1. No floating point is involved.

Fix the standard basis and the Fourier basis F₆/√6. Which vectors are unbiased to both? Newton's method from random starting phases finds them.

This is the paper's final step (Stage C) on one fixed pair. Make the vectors the vertices of two graphs, 𝒪 for orthogonal pairs and 𝒰 for unbiased pairs. A further basis is a 6-clique in 𝒪. Two further bases would be two disjoint 6-cliques with all 36 cross edges in 𝒰. The paper runs this search inside millions of small balls covering every second basis. Here the second basis is the Fourier one, the case Grassl settled in 2004 with a Gröbner basis.

The first basis is the standard one. The other k − 1 are free complex matrices, and gradient descent (Adam) minimises a misfit f that is zero exactly when all k bases are orthonormal and mutually unbiased. Try d = 5 with 4 or 6 bases, which exist, and then d = 6 with 4.

This is evidence, not proof. Butterley and Hall (2007) and Brierley and Weigert (2008) ran searches like this and saw the same stall. A search that fails proves nothing, because it might just be looking in the wrong place. The paper's contribution is to rule out every place.

The companion paper proves the Matolcsi–Ruzsa–Weiner conjecture. For every complex Hadamard matrix of order six that is not equivalent to Tao's cubic matrix T, every one of the 20 character sums vanishes:

g_H(a) = ⅙ Σ_k Π_i H(i,k)^{a_i},   a = π·(1,1,1,−1,−1,−1)

The question

In ℂᵈ at most d + 1 bases can be pairwise mutually unbiased. When d is a prime power, d + 1 is reached (Ivanović 1981; Wootters and Fields 1989). Six is the first dimension that is not a prime power, and there the best anyone has built is three. Zauner conjectured in his 1991 diploma thesis that three is the maximum. Grassl (2004) showed that the standard–Fourier pair cannot be extended to four. Jaming, Matolcsi, Móra, Szöllősi and Weiner (2009) did the same for the whole two-parameter Fourier family with certified discretisation. Numerical searches agreed. No proof covered every pair.

The 2026 claim

A paper dated 24 September 2026, released on 6 October in a collection of 722 model-written papers, claims N(6) = 3. Its proof of "not four" is computer-assisted. It covers every possible second basis by finitely many balls in phase space, bounds every completion with Taylor estimates, and inside each ball runs the graph search from the F₆ tab. Its theorem is stated "under the arithmetic and compiler conditions" of its appendix, for one documented fresh run in binary64. A companion paper proves the Fourier-vanishing conjecture with exact integer and rational certificates. By Weiner's completion theorem, it also gives an exact upper bound of five bases.

What Lean covers

The release's Lean statement for this family is narrower than the headline, and the release says so: it "does not establish the paper's upper bound of three or its computer-assisted exclusion of four arbitrary bases". It proves the Fourier vanishing and the bound of five:

theorem fourier_and_family_bound :
    (∀ H : CMatrix, IsHadamard H → ¬Equivalent H tao →
      ∀ π : Equiv.Perm Coord, g H (permuteCharge π alpha) = 0) ∧
    (∀ n : ℕ, Attainable n → n ≤ 5)

The Fourier tab uses its definitions literally: IsHadamard (|H_ij|² = 1 and H*H = 6I), g, permuteCharge, Equivalent and taoExponent. The release's formalization catalogue does not list either paper. We did not build or run the Lean, and we did not rerun the paper's search. Its documented run took 4,470 seconds of wall-clock time (about 75 minutes) on the authors' platform.

What this page checks

mub.js is the only copy of the maths, and mub.selftest.mjs holds it to 1,387 checks.

  • The three bases: all 216 inner products, exactly in ℤ[ζ₁₂], plus the paper's two sums |1 + 2ω|² = |2 + ω²|² = 3. A perturbed basis fails.
  • The F₆ pair: random-start Newton finds 48 vectors, every time and from several seeds. Every phase is a multiple of 15°, or offset from one by θ or 2θ with sin θ = (√3 − 1)/2. They form 16 bases and no two are unbiased. Every edge is decided to within 10⁻¹³, and every other overlap sits at least 0.012 away from both 0 and 1/6. That this list of 48 is complete is Grassl's theorem, which we cite. Random search alone cannot prove it.
  • The search: it reaches f = 0 where bases are known to exist (3 in ℂ², 4 in ℂ³ and ℂ⁵, 3 in ℂ⁶), stalls above 0.04 for 4 in ℂ⁶, and its gradient matches finite differences.
  • Fourier: T and T∘T are Hadamard. |6g|² = 9 exactly at all 20 charges for T, and g = 0 exactly for F₆. g vanishes numerically across the F₆⁽²⁾ family and across random Hadamard matrices, except the ones Newton lands on in T's class. Equivalence moves neither verdict.

Not checked: the claim itself. Excluding four bases for every second basis is the paper's whole computation, and it is not rerun here. Neither are the companion paper's exact moment certificates.
