Arrows only go one way, and no pair points both ways. A vertex is good when it reaches at least as many vertices in exactly two steps as in one. Every vertex reached in one step is excluded from the second count. Seymour conjectured that every such graph has a good vertex. Try to draw one where nobody is good. Sinks don't help: a vertex with no way out reaches 0 in one step, so it is good. In a Paley tournament every vertex is exactly on the line.

Every graph on n vertices. There are 3^(n choose 2) labelled oriented graphs: each pair is joined one way, the other way, or not at all. That is 14,348,907 for n = 6. The check visits all of them in your browser.

The question

Picture a network where every link runs one way: who follows whom, who beats whom. Count each person's direct links, and then the new people they reach in exactly two steps. Paul Seymour conjectured around 1990 that someone always has at least as many in the second group as in the first. It is easy to state and to test, and it held up through decades of attempts. Proofs came for tournaments, where every pair plays, and for low degrees. The best general guarantee before 2026 was about 72% of the way there.

The 2026 claim

A paper dated 23 September 2026, written by an internal OpenAI model and released on 6 October in a collection of 722, proves the conjecture in full, with no connectivity or degree assumption. It argues by a minimal counterexample. Deleting arcs shows every vertex set must expand in a strict "concave" way. Blowing each vertex up into a large transitive tournament then turns that into an inequality about pairs of vertices. The paper shows no such pair system can exist, using a pruning lemma about matchings that holds for every finite relation.

How it was checked

The theorem is formalized in Lean. The release ships the statement as a separate file, checked by its Comparator tool against Lean's three standard axioms. The whole statement fits on a screen:

structure IsOriented (r : V → V → Prop) : Prop where
  loopless : ∀ v, ¬ r v v
  asymmetric : ∀ {u v}, r u v → ¬ r v u

def secondNeighbors (r : V → V → Prop) (v : V) :=
  univ.filter (fun w =>
    w ≠ v ∧ ¬ r v w ∧ ∃ u, r v u ∧ r u w)

def GoodVertex (r : V → V → Prop) (v : V) : Prop :=
  (firstNeighbors r v).card ≤ (secondNeighbors r v).card

theorem exists_goodVertex (r : V → V → Prop)
    (hr : IsOriented r) : ∃ v, GoodVertex r v

This page's second() is that definition with bitmasks, and the selftest compares the two on random graphs. We did not run Lean ourselves, and the paper is a preprint.

What this page checks

seymour.js is the only copy of the maths, and seymour.selftest.mjs holds it to 5,214 checks:

  • The fast neighbourhoods equal the literal Lean definitions on 300 random graphs.
  • Paley tournaments (p = 3, 7, 11, 19, 23) are tight at every vertex.
  • All 3^(n choose 2) labelled oriented graphs up to n = 6 have a good vertex. That is 14,348,907 graphs at n = 6, and the sink-free count matches an independent inclusion–exclusion formula.
  • Sink-free graphs can have as few as 2 good vertices.
  • The adversary never gets below zero.

For six vertices or fewer the check is complete. Past that, only the proof speaks.
