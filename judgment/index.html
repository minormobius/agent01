The Economics and Cultivation of Judgment in the Agentic Age

As execution scales faster than understanding, sustainable leverage depends on organizational architecture and the cultivation of judgment.

Abstract

The agentic economy is decoupling productive capacity from the human capacity to understand and direct production. Individuals can ascend the value chain and assume program-level responsibilities far faster than traditional apprenticeships cultivate the judgment those responsibilities demand. As agents and human collaborators generate increasingly complex work, the scarce resource becomes not execution but the attention required to maintain orientation, recognize consequential developments, ask the right questions, and intervene effectively.

This research program investigates sustainable leverage: the ability to scale productive activity without losing meaningful agency over it. It treats taste not merely as aesthetic preference, but as a potentially cultivable capacity to recognize relevance, evaluate unfamiliar possibilities, select consequential interventions, and be productively surprised.

The inquiry draws on attention economics, organizational theory, cybernetics, distributed cognition, psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, and theological traditions of discernment and formation. It seeks to distinguish principles of coordination that generalize across cognitive substrates from those peculiar to embodied human minds.

On the tactical side, it examines agent architectures, context management, independent verification, and organizational memory as means of reducing supervisory burden without concealing consequential errors. On the developmental side, it asks whether the same agentic systems that accelerate responsibility can also accelerate the acquisition of calibrated judgment through deliberately structured experience and feedback.

The aim is both theoretical and practical: to develop a framework for understanding, measuring, and improving the joint scaling of human judgment and machine-enabled production.

Status. The central propositions are working hypotheses, not established empirical conclusions. The reading list is a first pass at a curriculum, and it is ranked by relevance to this programme. The ranking says nothing about citation impact or how strong the evidence is.
