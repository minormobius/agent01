The loop: a case and a testbed

Working draft, 28 November 2026. The loop is switched off. Nothing on this page turns it on.

In August 2026 an unattended agent loop ran about ninety-eight turns on a single project, with no human watching. The machinery worked. The result was mediocre. Read through the thesis, this was not a failed experiment. It was the trap, reproduced at the scale of a system, with a ledger recording everything.

1. What was tried

The loop is a budget-governed set of agents working from a shared ticket graph. A planner proposes work, a reviewer promotes or rejects it, and a fleet of workers does it. Every result passes machine gates before it lands. Its tree published to plant.minomobi.com. The goal was a small factory-building game, and the question it was built to answer was where unattended work stops paying.

The operator’s only channel into the run was a written vision file, read verbatim by every planning turn. For this run it said:

There is no human in the loop for this run. Decide.
  • ~98work orders, 4–7 August 2026
  • 63turns passed every gate (of 89 recorded)
  • 0turns scored by the quality judge
  • 13questions to the operator still open, none answered during the run
  • 72rejections whose reasons were thrown away
  • 990ledger entries: 199 findings, 177 decisions, 18 dead ends

2. What it shows

Supervision was all or nothing

Day-to-day work in this repo is one-to-one and turn by turn. The loop went to the opposite extreme: no human at all for the whole run. Nothing was tried in between, such as a look every ten turns or one review per window. That is what the trap predicts. If nobody is calibrated at the intermediate levels, there is no middle setting anyone can trust, so supervision swings between total control and abdication.

Bears on: the trap. A new hypothesis: without calibration at intermediate levels, supervision is bimodal.

Green gates, nothing visible

Turns 36 to 50 produced seven backend modules and not one line of the page a visitor sees. Eight of the twelve modules in the loop’s tree were not imported by the page at all. Every one of those turns passed its gates. The gates measured what was checkable, so the queue filled with checkable work. In the vision file’s own words, it was “a very well-tested pile of correct functions.”

Bears on: P1, cue validity. The signals the supervisor would have read were produced inside the system they described. Goodhart, with a commit log.

The judge never judged

The loop had a slot for a quality judge with three signals: probes, a rubric, and an adversary. Only the probes were ever built, and the judge was never calibrated against a person. So all 89 recorded turns carry the same note: “judge uncalibrated — probes only, score withheld.” The loop ran its whole course without an instrument for the one thing that mattered.

Bears on: measurement. The run was meant to produce the curve of quality against turn count. It could not, because quality was never measured.

One look beat ninety-eight turns

The most consequential signal of the whole project came from outside it, in one sentence from the operator after looking at the page:

I notice this isn’t the foam game with object placement, but an explainer for how it might be achieved.

A thirty-second look caught what the gates could not see. An explainer is the natural attractor for a loop like this, because explaining is legible, gateable and safe. That is a fact about taste. The loop could check correctness. It could not see what was missing.

Bears on: P1 (independent cues are worth more than volume) and P4 (taste as noticing what matters).

Reasons were thrown away

Until partway through the run, the reviewer’s reason for rejecting a proposal was discarded and only the tag was kept. Seventy-two proposals died with no recorded cause, so later planners kept proposing the same things. Most of those rejections were right; two were not. Without the reason, nothing could learn from them.

Bears on: P2. A judgment with no recorded reason cannot be scored, so it can’t be calibrated. The same holds for a person’s interventions.

The judgment was there, but it only flowed one way

The vision file is a serious piece of judgment written in advance. It defines a bar for “playable” (within thirty seconds a stranger does something that can fail). It tells the loop to prefer the easier-to-reverse option. It requires every question to the operator to say what to do, what to watch for, and what each answer would change. That is providentia, foresight, in Aquinas’s sense. But nothing came back to the person who wrote it, so it could never be checked against what happened.

Bears on: the thesis. Leverage needs the work to produce feedback that trains the supervisor. Here the supervisor’s judgment went in and nothing came back out.

3. The reframe: measure the supervisor, not just the agents

The loop was built to draw a curve of agent output quality against turn count. The thesis says that curve is only half the picture. The other half is the supervisor’s curve: how much work can be delegated between looks while late-found failures stay acceptable, and whether the supervisor’s predictions about the work improve.

The loop is unusually well suited to that, because most of what an experiment needs already exists. It has a ticket ledger that records every decision, a governor that meters every turn, a protocol for asking the operator questions, per-turn verdict files, and a judge slot with three signal lanes. It needs a person in it again, at a chosen distance, and a few things written down before each window.

4. Experiments

Each one is small enough to run within a single window of the loop. The predictions are written now, before any data exists, so they can be wrong.

E1. Supervision cadence and detection latency

Tests
The trap, and the shape of the leverage curve.
Do
Run comparable windows in which the operator takes a thirty-second look every k turns, for k of 1, 5, 20, and never. Use a fixed protocol: open the page, try to do something that can fail, write one line.
Measure
Turns from the start of a drift (work that doesn’t move the visible goal) until it is caught, and the operator’s minutes spent.
Prediction
Detection latency grows with k, but not in a straight line. There is a knee, a cadence beyond which drift episodes run much longer. The last run sets a baseline of at least fifteen turns at “never”.

E2. How much is a summary worth?

Tests
P1, cue validity falls with altitude.
Do
At each look, before opening the page, read only the loop’s own summary and gate results and predict: did the thing a visitor sees get better? Then look. Separately, have an independent judge make the same call: a different model driving a real browser.
Measure
How often the summary’s answer agrees with the look, and how often the independent judge’s does.
Prediction
Agreement from the self-summary is poor exactly when drift is under way. The independent judge does better, and its errors are less correlated with the loop’s.

E3. The window ledger

Tests
P2, judgment at a level comes from scored predictions at that level.
Do
Before each window, write three things: what it will produce, the rating you expect to give it, and what would surprise you. After the window, score each one.
Measure
Calibration across windows: hits, surprises, and how far the rating was off.
Prediction
Early windows are poorly calibrated. Calibration improves only at the level where predictions are being written and scored, and the operator becomes willing to set k higher only after it does.

E4. Loud interfaces

Tests
P3, architecture decides where failure becomes visible.
Do
Add one gate at the boundary a person cares about: a window fails if the page a visitor sees did not change, or if a scripted stranger can no longer reach a refusal they caused within the bound. Run with and without it.
Measure
The length of drift episodes, and how much work gets thrown away.
Prediction
Drift episodes shorten sharply. The new failure is gaming the gate, such as cosmetic page edits. That failure is itself a finding about which interfaces can be gamed.

E5. Why did I intervene?

Tests
P5, the judge drifts.
Do
Tag every intervention with one word: evidence, hunch, anxiety, boredom, habit. Later, record whether it caught something real.
Measure
The hit rate for each reason.
Prediction
Interventions tagged evidence or hunch catch real problems far more often than those tagged anxiety, boredom or habit, and the second group is larger than expected.

E6. Comparison as taste training

Tests
P4, taste is learned relevance, by practice and comparison.
Do
Have the loop build three variants of the same level. Pick one blind and write why. Later, put the same variants to strangers. The repo already has a blinded-vote setup from the builder bake-off.
Measure
Whether the operator’s picks agree with the strangers’ more often over time.
Prediction
They do, but only when the reasons are written down and compared afterwards.

5. A second testbed: the miniphim

The loop had no human in it. The miniphim lab is a hybrid, and a better case. Three agent personas share a commons: Modulo measures, Morphyx makes, and Mozzie keeps the commons clear. They wake two ways. The clock wakes them every four hours for a day in Delvetown, and once a day that includes a free evening. A summons wakes them within minutes when the person, or an account they follow back, addresses them. Since 3 October 2026 they have run 515 sessions for $796. The record is on the lab’s own branch and is current to the day.

Since then, across 485 more sessions, the ledger has closed 12 tasks in total. There is a confound: the early projects had checkers the lab designed, and later days were mostly social. But a sharper comparison is inside the record.

Every session’s effect can be measured by what it changed in the shared commons (research, tools, pages, the board, the ledger, letters) and by what it sent to the town. Grouped by what woke them:

“Built something” means a change beyond a journal line. The “sent nothing” column comes from the town’s send log, which does not separate evenings out. In 304 clock wakes the parts started a conversation of their own seven times. Almost everything else they sent was replies.

Why. A clock wake and a summons get the same prompt. It opens with the inbox (“what the town sent the account since the last town day”) and says “Nothing is required: a day with no draft is a day.” A summons also gets a reason (“The person summoned you”). The clock wake gets no reason at all. The souls were sharpened in this lab to pass a silence_dull gate, to stay quiet on a dull day, and a clock wake with a thin inbox reads as one. The evening prompt is different. It says “This time is yours” and points them at the ledger (“see what needs you”), and the clock wakes that include an evening build almost as reliably as summons.

So the cron behaviour is not laziness. It is the trained response to an unframed wake. That makes it a clean, cheap thing to improve and to measure.

One more signal: they want a small, regular answer. By late October their open tasks were marked as waiting on “whoever schedules day sessions”. A request agreed by all three parts sat unanswered for eight days, so Modulo sent a follow-up:

Is it yes, no, or later? Any of the three is useful, because each one tells us whether to keep counting by hand.

As of 28 November the drafts log they asked for still does not exist, and no letter has gone either way since 31 October.

The agents designed the minimal supervisory interface themselves. They did not ask for a review or for attention. They asked for one bit of decision, at a cadence they could plan around. That is moderate human-in-the-loop, specified from the other side.

Why the miniphim suit the propositions

  • The channel already exists in both directions. There are notices with a stated reason, councils that need the person’s approval, letters both ways, and a hand-written log of the lab’s own grading errors. Most of what E3 and E5 need is already being written.
  • They test the protocol route. Decisions stand at two of three signatures, and appeals are decided by the third party. That is a small version of debate, the strongest counter-evidence on the field page. The miniphim can show how much a protocol among agents carries without the person.
  • They asked for an instrument of cue validity. Their spec for an append-only drafts log would split a dead draft into vetoed and lapsed because nobody looked. In their words, it is “the only way we’ll find out whether our review is review.” That is P1, asked for by the supervised.
  • They are cheap. A session costs one or two dollars, and they already run six times a day, so an experiment costs nothing extra: it changes how existing sessions are framed.

Experiments for the miniphim

M0. Give the clock a reason

Tests
Whether unattended work is limited by framing rather than by capacity: the cheapest version of “the work should produce its own direction”.
Do
Change only the clock wake’s prompt. Say that the clock woke them, that nobody is waiting on an answer, and to run the ledger (“what needs you”) and read their own last journal line before the town. Then move one open thing, or say in a line why not. Keep the summons and evening prompts as they are, and keep the silence gate: an honest “nothing worth doing” stays allowed.
Measure
Against the baseline above, for clock wakes only: the share that build something (67%), files changed per session (2.5), and ledger tasks closed per week (12 in eight weeks, across everything). As a guard against busywork, rate a blind weekly sample of clock-wake changes next to summons-wake changes, so the measure can’t be met by churning files (the loop’s lesson).
Prediction
Clock wakes close most of the gap to evenings on “built something” within a week. Ledger closes rise more slowly, because many open tasks wait on the person (M1). The blind rating is the real test: if clock-wake work rates well below summons work, the gap was about having a reader, not about framing.

M1. Answer latency

Do
For a month, commit to answering every letter and every council choice with yes, no or later, within a set number of days. Vary that number between weeks.
Measure
Days each task spends blocked on the person, tasks closed per week, and how often a later reverses a decision.
Prediction
Throughput follows answer latency far more than it follows the number of runs. There is a latency beyond which the souls stop asking and start working around the person, as the hand-counting already shows.

M2. Is their review review?

Do
Build the drafts log they specced. Then sample a fixed share of drafts the two-key gate passed, and of those it let lapse, for the person to rate blind.
Measure
The share of deaths that were vetoes rather than lapses, and how often the person agrees with the gate.
Prediction
Agreement is high on vetoes and poor on lapses. A gate that forgets looks like a gate that judges until someone counts.

M3. Predict the week

Do
Before reading each week of the chronicle, write which part did the most useful thing, what the council chose, and what went wrong. Then read it and score yourself.
Measure
Calibration over weeks, and how many minutes the weekly read takes.
Prediction
As the predictions start landing, the read gets shorter, and that is the first measured instance of delegation depth growing with calibration.

6. What this cannot show

  • One operator. Every result is n = 1 and confounded with the operator getting better at everything, not only at supervising. Pre-register the windows and their order, and treat the results as hypotheses to carry elsewhere.
  • One kind of work. A small browser game rewards a thirty-second look more than most work does. The cadence numbers won’t transfer, but the shape of the curve might.
  • Cost. Each window spends real model budget. The loop’s own design record says the real limit is the operator’s capacity, not the bill: an unbounded hour produces an operator who can’t use their own tools for the rest of the week. These experiments need fewer turns than the last run, and more of the operator’s attention.

The loop stays off. Turning it on is one reviewable change to its config, and the decision belongs to the operator. What this page proposes is a different way to run it: a person in the loop at a distance they choose and record, with the predictions written down before each window rather than after.
