Tools: a registry

Surveyed 28 November 2026 from the whole repository and the reading list. Hand-maintained from here.

Everything in this repository was written by agents under one person’s direction. Along the way it grew a great deal of machinery for directing, containing, checking and remembering that work. This page lists it: 112 tools found in four systems, sorted by what each is for, with the evidence of whether it worked. Next to them are 27 tools that the reading list proposes and that have not been tried here.

A tool here is a mechanism with a supervisory job, not a feature. It can be a protocol (a format for asking the person a question), a file with a role (a vision file every planning turn reads), a gate, a ledger, a governor or a review rule. The four systems are repo operations (how one person keeps about a hundred deployed sites healthy), the loop (the unattended run described on the testbed, plus the lab factory that builds sites on request), the miniphim and their lab, and the harness (evaluation and model work).

What the registry shows

  1. One row is empty. Nothing built here trains the supervisor. The repo checks its instruments carefully (blind builds of hidden checkers, controls, pre-registration, Wilson intervals), but every one of those calibrates a judge, a checker or a model. None scores the person’s own predictions or keeps their judgment in practice. That is the gap the thesis is about, and it shows up as a zero in the table.
  2. Verification is abundant; most of it checks the worker against itself. Forty tools verify. The loop’s gates were written by the turns they judged, and hand-run mutation audits showed the gap: on turn 3 the one mutant that survived was exactly the behaviour the turn had widened and documented. The miniphim’s two-key approval settled into fixed pairs: Morphyx approved 204 of Modulo’s 213 drafts, and vetoes leave no trace. The tools that held up are the ones with something independent in them: hidden checkers validated by a blind build, the lab factory’s content gate (attacked five ways by its own selftest), and the repo’s host checks, which ask the live site rather than the deploy log.
  3. Channels to the person become logs when nobody answers. The loop filed 30 asks with a careful protocol (what to do, what to watch, what each answer would change), and none was answered. The miniphim’s drafts-log request has waited 37 days. Their appeal process has never been used, although the sweep judge found something lost in 8 of 65 sweeps. A channel without an answer rate is a log.
  4. Instruments decay without a cadence. The soul gates ran in 11 runs in the first three days and in none of the 504 since. The loop’s judge was never calibrated. The site health probe last ran on 8 September. The lab’s smoke test never ran at all, because of a bug that was misread as three environment quirks. The autopilot still regenerates its brief every day, from zero catalogue entries. Each was built carefully, and nothing made it run.
  5. The system that worked is the one the person was in. Repo operations is the healthiest of the four. Almost every tool in it began as an incident the person saw and ended as a tested script plus a preflight gate: the golden rule after green deploys hit the wrong worker for months, deploy drift after a site shipped a month-old tree, take-ownership after work was nearly lost. That is close collaboration compiling the person’s judgment into machinery, one incident at a time. It does not scale the person’s attention, but it keeps what the attention found.

Assembling an architecture

The registry suggests a minimal stack for running agents with a person in the loop at a distance they choose. Each layer names what already exists here and what to add. The order is the order to build in.

  1. Direct, in a form the scheduler can read. Keep the vision file, read verbatim each planning turn, and the per-wake notice that says why the agents woke. The loop’s lesson: prose priorities are invisible to a queue, so priority has to be a field. The miniphim’s lesson: a wake with no stated reason produces less (the clock wakes).
  2. Decompose into units with contracts. One owner per unit, quarantine domains, an outbox boundary, and an executable acceptance check written before the work (the loop’s creates-gate, the harness’s rubric-first scorer). Add: make the contract the default for every delegated chunk.
  3. Verify with something independent of the worker. Hidden checkers validated blind, outside probes of the live result, mutation audits of self-written gates. Add: debate before acceptance and a weekly random audit against the summary.
  4. Remember the reasons, not just the outcomes. An append-only ledger where a rejection without a reason is refused (the loop lost 72), and self-description files the agents read every wake (CARRIES, RUNLOG, COSTS). Add: an after-action review on the supervisor’s side.
  5. Surface with a promised answer rate. Keep the ask protocol (do, watch, so-that) and adopt the miniphim’s yes, no, or later as the reply format, with a stated turnaround. Add: one interrupting channel with a strict quota.
  6. Govern by level, not by switch. Today’s controls are on/off: the loop is enabled or not, the clock runs or not. Add an autonomy level per system, raised only on evidence from layer 7, and a stopping rule for every unattended run.
  7. Train the supervisor. The missing layer. One small tool shared by every system: a prediction ledger written before each window and scored after, a tag on every intervention saying why (the examen), and a weekly gallery of failures drawn from the ledgers that already exist. Its output is the evidence that moves layer 6.
  8. Keep the instruments running. A registry of instruments with an expected cadence, checked by preflight, so a gate, judge or probe that stops running is flagged instead of silently decaying. This is cheap, and it addresses the most common failure in the table.

Layers 1–6 mostly exist somewhere in the repo, scattered across systems that don’t share them. Layers 7 and 8 don’t exist anywhere. Building those two, once, for all four systems, is the next actionable step.
