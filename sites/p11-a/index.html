What a Chladni plate is

In 1787 the physicist Ernst Chladni scattered sand on a metal plate and drew a violin bow across its edge. The plate rang at one of its natural resonant frequencies, and the sand — instead of settling randomly — traced out clean geometric patterns. Those patterns are now named after him. They were some of the first evidence anyone could see that a vibrating surface has a fixed, repeatable shape to its motion, not just a frequency.

Nodes, antinodes, and why the sand moves at all

Forced to vibrate at one of its resonant frequencies, a plate doesn't move uniformly. Some regions — the antinodes — swing up and down with real amplitude. Other regions, along curves called nodal lines, barely move at all: displacement there is close to zero, all the time, at every instant of the cycle. A grain of sand sitting on an antinode gets bounced around by the plate under it; a grain sitting on a node feels almost nothing and stays put. Run the plate long enough and the sand migrates, grain by grain, away from the antinodes and onto the nodal lines — which is exactly what the simulation above is computing, not just drawing.

Reading the pattern

Each preset is a mode, labelled by two whole numbers n and m — roughly, how many half-waves fit across the plate in each direction. Turn either slider up and you're asking the plate to fit more oscillation into the same span, which means more nodal lines, a busier pattern, and — because bending a plate into a tighter wave costs more energy per cycle — a higher resonant frequency. That's why the relative-frequency figure under the sliders climbs with the pattern's complexity: it's proportional to n² + m², the standard scaling for a thin plate's bending-mode frequencies. It's a relative figure, not a number of hertz — see below.

This uses the standard textbook approximation for a square plate's free-edge modes — a superposition of two perpendicular standing waves, not the exact (and much messier) solution to the plate-bending equation. It gets the node count and symmetry right, which is what makes the figures look correct, but the frequency numbers above are relative to each other, not a measurement in hertz: real frequencies depend on the plate's material, thickness and size, none of which are modelled here.
