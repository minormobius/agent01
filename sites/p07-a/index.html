Gibson Jackpot

A toy age-based hazard model. Pick a country, tell it your age and where you sit on the income ladder, and it draws a survival curve — country risk, state resilience and personal SES stacked as multipliers on a baseline mortality curve. Underneath, the same machinery runs a slow-motion world population "jackpot": a simulated multi-decade collapse, calibrated to land on an 80% decline by 2099. None of this is a forecast — it's a working toy with made-up-but-monotonic numbers, built to make the shape of the idea visible.

A real single-year-age cohort simulation — annual births and deaths, not a drawn curve — where a single "shock intensity" knob is solved by bisection so the simulated world ends 2099 at 20% of its 2024 population, the requested 80% decline. The shock multiplies the same baseline hazard curve used above, rising over the century and landing harder on low-resilience places — which is also why your individual curve above bends up sooner if you're older today, since more of your remaining years fall inside the worst decades. Named for the "everything-at-once, most-people-don't-notice-a-single-cause" collapse that turns up in climate/futures writing (Gibson's The Peripheral coined the word for it) — used here only as the shape of the idea, not a citation of real demographic research.
