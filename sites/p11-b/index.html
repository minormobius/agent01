Chladni Plate

A square plate, forced to vibrate. Sand can't sit still where the plate is moving — so it collects exactly where the plate isn't.

mode (1, 2) ≈50 Hz (illustrative)

What you're looking at

The plate is forced to vibrate at one of its natural shapes — a mode. Most of the surface swings up and down, but a network of lines sits still no matter how hard the plate shakes: the nodal lines. Scatter grains on a real plate bowed at the right pitch and they bounce away from the moving regions (the antinodes) and pile up along the nodes — tracing the mode shape in sand. That is the actual 1787 demonstration; this page reproduces it with numbers instead of a violin bow.

Mode numbers (n, m)

Each preset is a pair of integers. The height of the plate at position (x, y) is modeled as the difference of two simple standing-wave grids:

f(x, y) = cos(nπx)·cos(mπy) − cos(mπx)·cos(nπy)

Swapping n and m gives the same two grids in the other order, and subtracting them is what breaks the plate's symmetry into the curved, looping shapes Chladni patterns are known for — a plain cos·cos grid alone just gives a checkerboard. Where f(x,y) is zero, the plate is flat for every instant of the oscillation: that's a node. The colour on the plate is how far it currently sits from zero, brightest at the antinodes.

Watching the sand settle

Each grain nudges itself away from wherever the plate is moving hardest right now (down the slope of |f|) and jitters slightly in proportion to how much the plate under it is shaking — a rough stand-in for grains actually being thrown by the vibration. Drop the amplitude to zero and the plate goes still: nothing pushes the sand anywhere, same as a plate that was never bowed.

This is illustrative, not a measurement. Real Chladni demonstrations use a specific plate (usually clamped or bowed at one point, often circular, not square with free edges), and the pattern depends on where it's held. The two-term formula above is the standard teaching approximation for a square plate, not a solved elasticity equation for any particular sheet of metal. The "Hz" shown for each preset scales with √(n²+m²), which is the right shape for how mode frequencies climb — it is not a frequency measured off a real plate.

A note on how this was built

The brief asked for the simulation in Rust, compiled to WebAssembly. That needs a Rust toolchain to produce a .wasm file, and this build had no compiler and no network — only a vendored, pre-built module could have been used, and none existed for this. The physics here runs in plain JavaScript instead; the rendering is three.js.
