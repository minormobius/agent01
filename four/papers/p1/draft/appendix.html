A · The 4D kernel

A thin spherical shell of unit charge and radius ss, felt at radius rr, gives the potential κ(t)/max(r,s)\kappa(t)/\max(r, s), where t=min(r,s)/max(r,s)t = \min(r, s)/\max(r, s) and κ\kappa is the law’s 1/|x−y|1/|x - y| averaged over the shell. On the 3-sphere the average over directions carries the weight 2πsin2θdθ\tfrac{2}{\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta, so

κ(t)=2π∫0πsin2θdθ1+t2−2tcosθ.\kappa(t) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin^2\theta\,d\theta}{\sqrt{1 + t^2 - 2t\cos\theta}}.

In ours the same average, with weight 12sinθdθ\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta, is exactly 1 for every tt: the shell theorem. In four dimensions it isn’t. The integral has a closed form in the complete elliptic integrals K\mathrm K and E\mathrm E of modulus tt,

κ(t)=4[(1+t2)E(t)−(1−t2)K(t)]3πt2,\kappa(t) = \frac{4\,[(1 + t^2)\,\mathrm E(t) - (1 - t^2)\,\mathrm K(t)]}{3\pi t^2},

which gives κ(1)=8/3π\kappa(1) = 8/3\pi (since E(1)=1\mathrm E(1) = 1), and the series

κ(t)=1−t28−t464−5t61024−35t816384−…\kappa(t) = 1 - \frac{t^2}{8} - \frac{t^4}{64} - \frac{5t^6}{1024} - \frac{35t^8}{16384} - \dots

Each term is tnt^n times the average of the Legendre polynomial Pn(cosθ)P_n(\cos\theta) over the 3-sphere; the odd ones vanish. The solver evaluates κ\kappa by Simpson’s rule in θ\theta with 400 intervals, tabulated at 2001 points of tt and interpolated. The closed form agrees with that quadrature to 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ for tt up to 0.9 (ledger kernelclosed); nearer 1 the quadrature’s own error dominates, a few parts in 10710^7.

The series’ first correction is the far pull of section 10. A cloud of NN electrons around a nucleus +Z+Z, seen from a distance RR outside it, gives (Z−N+∑i⟨ri2⟩/8R2+…)/R\big(Z - N + \sum_i \langle r_i^2\rangle / 8R^2 + \dots\big)/R. A neutral atom therefore acts on an electron through −Q/8R3-Q/8R^3 with Q=∑⟨r2⟩Q = \sum\langle r^2\rangle, and two neutral atoms on each other through −3QAQB/64R5-3Q_AQ_B/64R^5 (section 10). Figure A1 draws κ\kappa, and the potential inside and outside a shell.

B · The 4D electron gas

The solver’s exchange is the local form of the uniform electron gas of the same dimension and the same law. In DD dimensions a spin-unpolarized Fermi sea of density ρ\rho has ρ=2ΩDkFD/(2π)D\rho = 2\,\Omega_D k_F^D/(2\pi)^D, with ΩD\Omega_D the volume of the unit ball (π2/2\pi^2/2 for D=4D = 4). Its kinetic energy per volume is DD+2ρkF2/2\tfrac{D}{D + 2}\,\rho\,k_F^2/2: 310(3π2)2/3ρ5/3\tfrac{3}{10}(3\pi^2)^{2/3}\rho^{5/3} in ours (Thomas–Fermi’s 2.8712), 4π3ρ3/2\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\rho^{3/2} in four dimensions. Its exchange energy per volume is −ADρ1+1/D-A_D\,\rho^{1 + 1/D}. In ours A3=34(3/π)1/3A_3 = \tfrac34(3/\pi)^{1/3} = 0.738559, Dirac’s constant. In four dimensions under the pinned 1/r1/r, with the Fermi sea’s density matrix built on the Bessel function J2J_2 and the integral ∫0∞J2(x)2x−2dx=4/15π\int_0^\infty J_2(x)^2\,x^{-2}\,dx = 4/15\pi, it is A4=16/15πA_4 = 16/15\sqrt\pi = 0.601802. These agree with Loos and Gill’s expressions for the DD-dimensional gas (2016). The selftest checks the textbook values in ours and the 4D integral (blocks 4 and 18). The potential each electron feels is vx=−(1+1/D)ADρ1/Dv_x = -(1 + 1/D)\,A_D\,\rho^{1/D}, spin-polarized as in section 5 (each spin’s own density doubled), with each electron’s own share taken out.

C · Numerical convergence

The solver’s stop. The self-consistent field stops when the total energy changes by less than 10⁻⁷ hartree between iterations. Re-solving 19 atoms (4D 2, 4D 7, 4D 12, 4D 13, 4D 22, 4D 26, 4D 50, 4D 51, 4D 68, 4D 78, 4D 101, 4D 128, 4D 138, ours 2, ours 10, ours 11, ours 18, ours 26, ours 36) with the stop at 10⁻¹² moves no total energy by more than 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ hartree and no first ionization by more than 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ (ledger converged).

The radial grid. The baked grid has 700 points from 10⁻⁵ to 150 bohr, logarithmic with step 0.02364. 11 atoms (4D 2, 4D 12, 4D 13, 4D 22, 4D 50, 4D 78, 4D 128, 4D 138, ours 10, ours 18, ours 36) were re-solved on six other grids, each changing one parameter (ledger grid):

D · The law calculations

Section 2’s arguments use a separate one-electron solver (laws.js), so that the grid’s inner end can be moved. It uses the radial equation with L=l+(D−3)/2L = l + (D - 3)/2 on a logarithmic grid of 6000 points, counts the nodes of an outward Numerov solution at a trial energy, and bisects on the count to 10−1310^{-13} relative; a level that falls with the inner end of the grid is a fall to the centre. Its check is 4D hydrogen, −0.22222222 hartree against −2/9-2/9.

Gauss’s −α/r2-\alpha/r^2. At α\alpha = 0.45 there is no bound level on any grid. At α\alpha = 0.7 the lowest level is −5.87 × 10³, −5.87 × 10⁵ and −5.87 × 10⁷ hartree with the inner end at 10−310^{-3}, 10−410^{-4} and 10−510^{-5} bohr: εr02\varepsilon r_0^2 = −0.00587 and −0.00587, a fall to the centre set by r0r_0 (ledger gauss). The boundary between the two is Hardy’s constant ((D−2)/2)2=1((D - 2)/2)^2 = 1 (Barbatis 2024), which with the kinetic energy 12|∇ψ|2\tfrac12|\nabla\psi|^2 puts the critical α\alpha at 12\tfrac12 (Landau and Lifshitz 1977, sec. 35).

Power laws −1/rη-1/r^\eta. The splitting of 2p from 2s, as a fraction of the shell’s binding, is −6.2 % at η=0.8\eta = 0.8, 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ % at η=1\eta = 1, +15.4 % at η=1.2\eta = 1.2 and +64.0 % at η=1.5\eta = 1.5. For 3d against 3s it is −9.2 %, +21.9 % and +78.3 % (ledger uniqueness). Only η=1\eta = 1 keeps a shell together.

E · Checks against our world

E.1 Hydrogen. One electron needs no search and no exchange, and four-dimensional hydrogen has an exact answer: −Z2/2(n+12)2-Z^2/2(n + \tfrac12)^2 hartree, so −2/9-2/9 for the ground state. The solver gives −0.22222.

E.2 Two electrons. Helium-like atoms in four dimensions are checked against an independent code, Hartree–Fock in a Gaussian basis written for any dimension (gauss.js), and the same atoms in ours against PySCF at the MP2 level (selftest blocks 17 and 43). The two codes share no part of the radial solver. From outside, the only accurate four-dimensional numbers we have been able to read are correlated variational energies for helium-like ions (Escobar-Ruiz et al. 2021). Hartree–Fock (hf.js, section 5.12) lies above them by 0.012–0.014 hartree for ZZ = 2–10, as it must, since they include correlation; that is the size and sign of a correlation energy, a consistency check rather than a benchmark. A four-dimensional Hartree–Fock limit to test against (Loeser and Herschbach 1986) is in tables we have not obtained.

E.3 Our own table, and the solver’s lean toward d. The same solver, the same law (in ours 1/r is Gauss’s) and the same search, run in three dimensions, gives 32 of 36 of our measured ground configurations. The four it misses, titanium, vanadium, cobalt and nickel, each take one d electron too many against 4s, the known lean of local exchange toward d. The misses have one cause. Moving an electron from 4s into 3d comes out too cheap in every 3d atom we can compare (eV, the solver against measured: Sc 0.14 against 1.43, Ti −0.89 against 0.81, Fe 0.51 against 0.87, Co −0.35 against 0.42, Ni −1.16 against −0.03), short by 0.37–1.70 eV and a mean 1.05, always in d’s favour. The measured values are differences of each configuration’s lowest term, its levels averaged with weights 2J+12J + 1, from NIST’s level tables (Sansonetti and Martin 2005), because the solver has no spin–orbit coupling and lands each configuration in its lowest term. Taking the lowest level of each instead moves them by at most 0.06 eV and turns nickel’s sign (+0.03 rather than −0.03). That is local exchange without correlation. Resolving every open subshell’s whole term structure doesn’t remove it. With that correction (the lowest term less the average of the maximum-spin arrangements, which is what a spherically averaged field stands for), the bias stays at 1.05 eV and the configurations get worse, 26 of 32 against 28 of 32. So the paper corrects only Hund’s first rule (section 6), the one thing a spin-polarized averaged field cannot represent. Its first ionization energies are off by a median 4.3 % and at most 16.2 % (figure A2). That is the accuracy to expect of the 4D table: a method that gets our transition metals mostly right and our ionizations to a few percent, not a spectroscopic one.

E.4 Ions. Each ion of ours searched for its own ground state, rather than made by taking one electron from the atom’s configuration, changes no first ionization. Searched the same way, our +2 and +3 ions give second and third ionization energies within a median 2.0 % and 1.4 % of measured ones (Kramida et al. 2026) (worst V, 7 % and C, 4 %), closer than the first ionizations’ 4.3 %, and they lose their 4s electrons before 3d, as measured (Fe⁺ 3d⁶4s, Fe²⁺ 3d⁶, Fe³⁺ 3d⁵). In four dimensions the 3d row’s ions do the same. Elements in the first half of the 4d and 5d blocks, and the two that open row 6’s f block with a 5d electron, instead give up a d electron at +1 or +2 while keeping the outer s (53–56, 58, 61, 81–82, 114–118, 120, 122), as our yttrium and lutetium do (Y⁺ 5s², Lu⁺ 6s²). The same search for anions gives our electron affinities where the extra electron is bound by the field and exchange: the p-block acceptors (C 1.32 against 1.262, Si 1.30 against 1.39, Ge 1.25 against 1.233, Al 0.40 against 0.433, F 2.80 against 3.401, Cl 3.15 against 3.613, O 0.71 against 1.461 eV, measured second). It also finds every anion that should be unbound to be unbound: the noble gases, beryllium, magnesium and nitrogen. It fails where correlation does the binding (H -0.33 against 0.754, Li -0.08 against 0.618, Na -0.02 against 0.548, P -0.10 against 0.746). In ours, then, the method’s affinities hold above about 0.7 eV and not below (the measured values are from Rienstra-Kiracofe et al. (2002)). That is a calibration in our world, not an error bar for four dimensions: an affinity near threshold there would need its own checks, a wider radial grid for the extra electron, the anion’s and the atom’s fields both converged, and correlation.

E.5 Ionizations are converged in stop and grid. Tightening the solver’s stop or changing its grid moves no ionization energy by a millihartree (section 5 and appendix C).

E.6 Hartree–Fock as a second method. Hartree–Fock (section 5.12) on the same candidates gets 36 of 36 ground configurations of ours right, against local exchange’s 32. The two agree on 32, and every one of those is right; they differ on Ti, V, Co and Ni, and on each of these Hartree–Fock is right. The promotion of a 4s electron into 3d shows what each method does, against the same measured centroids as appendix E.3 (eV, error of local exchange and of Hartree–Fock: Sc −1.29/−0.42, Ti −1.70/−0.27, V −2.09/−0.12, Cr −1.73/−0.26, Mn −0.72/+1.19, Fe −0.37/+0.93, Co −0.76/+1.11, Ni −1.13/+1.31, Cu −0.44/+1.12). Local exchange makes d too cheap in all nine. Hartree–Fock makes it a little too cheap up to half filling (Sc–Cr, −0.42 to −0.12) and too dear after it (Mn–Cu, 0.93 to 1.31). So the two do not bracket the measured value; from scandium to chromium both lie on the same side of it. Their difference is a measure of how much an answer depends on the method, not an error bar: neither method bounds the other, and both omit the correlation that would move each toward the measured values.

In four dimensions the two agree on 126 of the 140 elements, and these are the table’s firm configurations. They differ on 14, which are open: 7 in the d rows (30, 31, 37, 38, 40, 58, 61), where local exchange puts one electron more in d than Hartree–Fock, as in ours; 5 at the end of row 6’s f block (107, 108, 109, 110, 111), where it puts one more in f; and 2 at the start of a block (53, 83). For the d rows, ours gives a lean. Before half filling (30, 31, 58) Hartree–Fock’s error in ours was small and in the same direction as local exchange’s, so its preference for s² should hold. After it (37, 38, 40, 61) its margins, 0.10 to 1.08 eV, are inside the volt by which it overprices d in ours. In the f row the first comparison was incomplete. Its candidates lay within the window of local exchange’s ground, and local exchange overbinds f, so configurations with one f electron fewer fell outside it. A window of 10 eV and a climb through Hartree–Fock’s own neighbourhood (section 5.12) close that gap, and our lanthanides calibrate what remains. Against the measured levels (Sansonetti and Martin 2005), Hartree–Fock gets 15 of 18 lanthanide ground configurations right (lanthanum to hafnium) and local exchange 12; where the two agree they are right 12 times in 14. In each of the 17 measured states that change the f count, Hartree–Fock makes f too stable, by 0.15 to 4.1 eV. So a configuration with one f electron fewer than Hartree–Fock’s ground could truly lie below it only if Hartree–Fock puts it less than BB = 4.1 eV up; one with more f only moves further up. In four dimensions f is bound far more deeply. The cheapest move of an f electron out of Hartree–Fock’s ground costs 5.8 eV in element 90, 12.6 in 100 and 19.9 in 109, against 0.9 eV in our terbium, where the measured cost is 0.04. Hence four verdicts on row 6’s f block:

  • Firm where the methods agree and every competitor with fewer f lies more than BB up, or the ground has no f: 81, 88–100 and 102–104.
  • Open though the methods agree where such a competitor lies within BB: 82 (1.53 eV), 84 (0.40), 85 (1.12), 86 (2.21) and 87 (3.86). Element 84 is the case of our cerium, whose measured 4f¹5d¹6s² both methods miss, agreeing on 4f²6s².
  • Leaning to Hartree–Fock where local exchange’s choice holds one f electron more: 83 and 107–111. Local exchange overbinds f by more still, so its extra f electron is the less likely.
  • Untested where no configuration with fewer f was solved: 101, 105, 106 and 112.

So 17 of the block’s 32 are firm, because in four dimensions the f shell’s binding outruns the calibration’s error; the open cases sit at the block’s two ends, where f competes with d and s at the start and is about to close at the end. In the d rows no Hartree–Fock ground configuration lies near the window’s edge. Every element’s verdict, both methods’ rankings and the margins are in data/hftable.json.
