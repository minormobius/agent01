Part III · What the law does (draft)

6 · What the law decides

To see which of the table’s features the law decides, every 4D element was solved a second time with one thing changed: the clouds’ potential taken as if the shell theorem held (κ=1\kappa = 1), and everything else, the four-dimensional kinetic energy, the degeneracies, exchange, the search, left as it was. Each search was seeded with the real table’s configuration, and the real law was re-solved in every counterfactual configuration, so a difference is the law’s, not a weaker search (appendix G.10). Of the 140 elements, 112 settle, every one up to element 88; the rest, 89–116, have no settled ground state under the shell theorem from any start tried, so what follows about the rows holds through row 5.

6.1 The rows survive the shell theorem. With the shell theorem forced on, the first 24 elements keep every configuration, and the noble gases close the same rows at elements 2, 12, 22, 50, 78, 138 (figure 6). So the rows don’t come from the shell theorem’s failure. Their lengths, 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, are sums of subshells of 2(l+1)22(l + 1)^2, filled in the order these calculations find under 1/r1/r; whether they survive a change of the exponent is a separate question.

6.2 What fills inside the rows is the law’s. Of the settled atoms, 57 change configuration, the first at element 25, the first d element. Under the shell theorem p beats d: element 25 takes 4s² 4p¹ where the law gives 3d¹ 4s², and the change runs on through the d and f blocks.

6.3 Partial screening holds outer electrons harder. In ours an inner cloud screens the nucleus from an outer electron whole. Here it screens only partly, by κ(t)\kappa(t), so outer electrons see more of the nucleus. Across the 110 atoms past helium that settle under both, the law holds the outermost electron a median 2.54 times as hard as the shell theorem would, from 1.06 to 9.55; among the noble gases the ratio grows from 1.09 (helium) to 2.90 (element 138). The largest is element 128’s: its full 5d¹⁸ shell holds its electron at 29.56 eV under the law and at 3.1 eV under the shell theorem, where the noble gases are again the hardest-held atoms, as ours are. The table’s hardest-held electron is partial screening at its strongest, a full shell whose own cloud hides the nucleus from it least.

6.4 Negative ions. Partial screening holds an extra electron harder too. One electron short of a full p shell (p⁷ in four dimensions), atoms bind an extra electron by 3.4, 5.7, 7.8, 10.3, 12.9 eV going down the group, where ours stay level (F 3.401, Cl 3.613, Br 3.364 eV, measured). 80 of the 140 bind one by more than 0.7 eV, the level above which the method held in ours (appendix E.4); the light noble gases don’t hold one at all.

6.5 The rows need the exponent 1/r. The rows survive the shell theorem, but not a change of exponent. Solved again under 1/rη1/r^\eta with η=0.5\eta = 0.5 and 1.51.5, every pair of charges alike, elements 1–100 (figure 1c, appendix F.8), the table’s noble gases move. Under η=0.5\eta = 0.5 an electron with more angular momentum is the more bound, element 4 is 1s² 2p², each shell fills whole before the next begins, and the noble gases fall at 2, 12, 40 and 100: hydrogen’s shells. Under η=1.5\eta = 1.5 the next shell’s s sinks below the current p (element 5 is 1s² 2s² 3s¹, 14 is 1s² 2s² 2p⁸ 3s²), and they fall at 2, 14, 24, 34 and 72 (82 of 100 settle). By the same rule the pinned law’s fall at 2, 12, 22, 42, 50, 70 and 78: its noble gases and two closed d-and-s atoms of zinc’s kind. Only 1/r1/r gives the rows the generalized Madelung rule predicts.

7 · Neutral atoms aren’t silent

Under the shell theorem a neutral atom is invisible from outside its cloud. Here it isn’t. A shell of charge qq and radius ss, felt at a distance RR outside it, gives qκ(s/R)/Rq\,\kappa(s/R)/R with κ=1−t2/8−…\kappa = 1 - t^2/8 - \dots (appendix A). Summed over the nucleus and the electrons the leading terms cancel, and what remains, for an electron at distance RR, is a pull −Q8R3-\tfrac{Q}{8R^3} with Q=∑⟨r2⟩Q = \sum\langle r^2\rangle. The solved noble gases follow it: at 30 bohr the full cloud’s potential and the far form agree to 0.22 %. Between two atoms with round clouds (closed shells, or a single s electron) the same failure gives a permanent attraction −3QAQB64R5-\tfrac{3Q_AQ_B}{64R^5}, with no dipole needed, which outranges London’s R−6R^{-6}; an open p, d or f shell isn’t round, and adds an ordinary quadrupole pull of the same order that depends on how the atoms are turned (appendix J.2).

For two like atoms the permanent pull’s coefficient is C5=3Q2/64C_5 = 3Q^2/64, and R*=C6/C5R^* = C_6/C_5 is the distance inside which dispersion is the larger, with C6C_6 from the solver’s coupled response by Casimir and Polder’s integral (appendix J.1, table 2; in ours its noble gases’ polarizabilities are off by −8 % to +4 % against Derevianko, Porsev, and Babb (2010)). These are computed for 15 four-dimensional atoms, those whose clouds are round: for hydrogen and helium R*R^* is 28 and 9.26 bohr, for the alkali-like and alkaline-earth-like atoms sampled 4.9–94, and for the other 5 closed-shell atoms sampled 0.59–1.5, inside the atom. Among them, past contact, the permanent term is the leading pull at every distance where a far-field form applies. Pulled apart by Hartree–Fock, two closed-shell atoms follow it (1.02–1.06 times the far form at 16 bohr), so the atoms the molecular codes separate into are this paper’s (appendix J.2).

That sets a requirement on every later model of four-dimensional matter. In ours the trace of a charge distribution’s second moment is dropped from multipole expansions, because 1/r1/r is harmonic and the trace carries no field. Here it is a neutral atom’s leading field, so a four-dimensional force field has to keep it; the later papers’ do, as a cloud term on every atom.

8 · Limitations and conclusion

Limitations. The force acts instantly (section 2.5), so only statics are safe, and nothing here moves. Heavy atoms would need relativity, which the law has given up for the force between charges, so the table past about element 100 is a first pass; round a point nucleus the innermost Dirac level fails at Z≈205Z \approx 205 in four dimensions (Katsura and Aoki 2006; GU, MA, and DONG 2002), beyond the table. Nuclei are declared stable to Z=140Z = 140. The method has local exchange and no correlation, which in ours favours d by about a volt (appendix E.3), every subshell’s cloud averaged over the sphere, and open subshells’ terms resolved to first order on the solver’s own orbitals. The counterfactual leaves row 6’s f block unsettled. Nothing here claims that four-dimensional chemistry looks like ours; the next papers take up bonds, liquids and crystals under the same law.

Conclusion. A world with four large dimensions of space has to choose its law between charges, because Gauss’s law gives it no atoms. Pinned at 1/r1/r, the only power law that keeps a shell together, it has atoms, and they have a periodic table: rows of 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, 60, noble gases at 2, 12, 22, 50, 78, 138, as the generalized Madelung rule predicts. The rows survive the shell theorem forced back on but not a change of exponent: they need 1/r1/r itself. What fills inside them from the first d element on doesn’t survive the shell theorem, because in four dimensions 1/r1/r isn’t harmonic and an inner cloud screens only partly: outer electrons are held about two and a half times harder, the hardest-held electron belongs to a full d shell rather than a noble gas, and a neutral atom pulls from afar. And four-dimensional space breaks Hund’s first rule for d², d¹⁶, 4f³ and 4f²⁹; for d², on element 26’s orbital, Gauss’s 1/r21/r^2 breaks it too.
