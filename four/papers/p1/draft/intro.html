Abstract

In four spatial dimensions the law between charges can’t be inherited from ours. Gauss’s law gives a potential falling as 1/r21/r^2, under which an atom either doesn’t bind or falls to its centre: there is no Bohr radius. We take the law as a choice instead. We keep the potential at 1/r1/r, the only power law whose levels keep a shell together, and follow what it costs: in four dimensions 1/r1/r isn’t harmonic, so there is no shell theorem and no Faraday cage, and the field theory that gives it gives up relativity for the force between charges. We then solve every neutral atom to Z=140Z = 140 self-consistently under that law. Rows of 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, 60 elements and noble gases at elements 2, 12, 22, 50, 78, 138 come out: the rows Négadi and Kibler’s generalized Madelung rule predicts for four dimensions. Solving the table again with the shell theorem forced on separates the law from the geometry. The rows are the geometry’s, but what fills them from element 25 on is the law’s. Without it p beats d, and outer electrons are held a median 2.54 times more weakly. The table’s hardest-held electron, element 128’s full d shell at 29.56 eV, would fall to 3.1 eV. Separately from the law, four-dimensional space breaks Hund’s first rule. Atoms with two electrons in, or two short of, a d shell pair their spins, and so do 4f³ and 4f²⁹, because the four-dimensional d shell has a pair state ours lacks. The table is corrected for it. The same solver in three dimensions gets 32 of 36 of our ground configurations and our ionization energies to a median 4.3 %.

1 · Introduction

Why does space have three dimensions? Ehrenfest (1917, 1920) turned the question into physics. He asked what in the laws of physics would change with the number of dimensions, and found, among other things, that under the inverse-power force Gauss’s law gives in DD dimensions, no planetary orbit is stable for D>3D > 3. Tangherlini (1963) carried the argument into general relativity and into quantum mechanics: the hydrogen atom with Gauss’s potential has no stable states above three dimensions. The usual conclusion is that a world with more than three large dimensions has no atoms. Callender (2005) points out what that conclusion leans on, that the laws carry over to other dimensions unchanged.

They needn’t. The literature on the hydrogen atom in DD dimensions (Nieto 1979; Al-Jaber 1998) and on dimensional scaling (Herschbach, Avery and Goscinski 1992; Loeser and Herschbach 1986) keeps the potential at 1/r1/r in every DD, mostly as a convention that keeps the problem solvable. With it, hydrogen has a Bohr radius in any dimension. Négadi and Kibler (1996) used the DD-dimensional hydrogen atom to extend Madelung’s filling rule (Madelung 1936) to other dimensions, and predicted the rows of a four-dimensional periodic table. Pyykkö and Zhao (1991) solved the atoms of a two-dimensional world self-consistently. To our knowledge no one has solved the atoms of a four-dimensional one, nor asked what the choice of 1/r1/r itself does to them.

This paper does both. Part I treats the law as a choice. It shows why one has to be made (section 2), why 1/r1/r exactly (section 3), and what it costs in four dimensions (section 4). Part II solves every neutral atom to Z=140Z = 140 under it (sections 5–8). It checks the same solver against our own table (section 6). It finds that four-dimensional space breaks Hund’s first rule, which the solver had assumed, and corrects for it (section 7), and it gives the table (section 8). Part III asks which of the table’s features the law decides. It solves the table a second time with the shell theorem forced back on (section 9), and follows the law into partial screening (section 10) and into the pull of neutral atoms (section 11). Section 12 states the limitations; section 13 concludes.
