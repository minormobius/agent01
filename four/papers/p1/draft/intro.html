Introduction (draft)

1 · Introduction

Why does space have three dimensions? Ehrenfest (1918, 1920) turned the question into physics. He asked what in the laws of physics would change with the number of dimensions, and found, among other things, that under the inverse-power force Gauss’s law gives in DD dimensions, no planetary orbit is stable for D>3D > 3. Tangherlini (1963) carried the argument into general relativity and into quantum mechanics: the hydrogen atom with Gauss’s potential has no stable states above three dimensions. The usual conclusion is that a world with more than three large dimensions has no atoms. Callender (2005) points out what that conclusion leans on, that the laws carry over to other dimensions unchanged.

They needn’t. The literature on the hydrogen atom in DD dimensions (Nieto 1979; Al-Jaber 1998) and on dimensional scaling (Herschbach, Avery, and Goscinski 1993; Loeser and Herschbach 1986a, 1986b; Ghosh, Kais, and Herschbach 2020) keeps the potential at 1/r1/r in every DD, mostly as a convention that keeps the problem solvable. With it, hydrogen has a Bohr radius in any dimension. Négadi and Kibler (1996) used the DD-dimensional hydrogen atom to extend Madelung’s filling rule (Madelung 1936) to any dimension and worked it out for two. Applied to four, their rule gives rows of 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, 60. Pyykkö and Zhao (1991) solved the atoms of a two-dimensional world self-consistently, by Hartree–Fock for ZZ = 1–24. Above three dimensions there are self-consistent atoms too, but not a table: Hartree–Fock for two-electron atoms in any DD (Loeser and Herschbach 1986a), the Thomas–Fermi atom in nn dimensions (Kventsel and Katriel 1981), and many-electron models built from the limit of large DD (Loeser 1987; Loeser et al. 1994). To our knowledge this paper is the first shell-resolved self-consistent survey of the neutral atoms through Z=140Z = 140 in four Euclidean dimensions, for a pairwise 1/r1/r interaction and electrons with two spin states. Its approximations are stated with it: spherical subshells, local exchange, no correlation, and a first-order correction for the open subshells’ terms (section 5), cross-checked by Hartree–Fock (appendix E.6). Nor, to our knowledge, has anyone asked what the choice of 1/r1/r itself does to the atoms.

This paper does both. Part I treats the law as a choice: why one has to be made (section 2), why 1/r1/r exactly (section 3), and what it costs in four dimensions (section 4). Part II solves every neutral atom to Z=140Z = 140 under it: the methods (section 5); Hund’s first rule, which four-dimensional space breaks and the solver corrects for (section 6); and the table, with each atom’s valence states (section 7). Part III asks which of the table’s features the law decides. It solves the table a second time with the shell theorem forced back on (section 8), follows the law into partial screening (section 9), and gives the response of atoms and the pull between them, the reference later work on molecules starts from (section 10). Section 11 states the limitations; section 12 concludes. Every method is first run on our own world against measured values; those checks are appendix E.
