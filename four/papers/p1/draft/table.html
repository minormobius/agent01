6 · Hund’s first rule in four dimensions

The solver gives each open subshell the most parallel spins it can hold. That is Hund’s first rule, and in ours it is the known answer for the ground terms of free atoms. In four dimensions it is an assumption, and for some fillings it is wrong.

6.1 Every term, from the open subshell’s determinants. For each open subshell of each ground configuration, its orbitals are the degree-ll harmonics on the 3-sphere, (l+1)2(l + 1)^2 of them (4 for p, 9 for d, 16 for f). The repulsion between two of its electrons is the law’s 1/|x−y|1/|x - y|, projected exactly onto the harmonics of degree 00 to 2l2l, with the radial parts integrated over the atom’s own orbital. Every arrangement of the subshell’s electrons with a given MSM_S is set up and the lowest energy found (Lanczos, matrix-free; a 4D d⁹ block has 15 876 arrangements). A term of spin SS appears in every block with MS≤SM_S \le S, so the ground term’s spin is the largest MSM_S whose block still reaches the lowest energy. In ours this reproduces Racah’s term separations for d² and d³ to the wavenumber, and the known ground terms of Cr³⁺, Mn²⁺, Pr³⁺ and Gd³⁺. Over all 23 open subshells of our table, maximum spin is lowest, as it should be.

6.2 d², d¹⁶, 4f³ and 4f²⁹ break the rule. In four dimensions all 36 open p subshells of the table keep the rule, and so do the d fillings d¹, d³–d¹², d¹⁴, d¹⁵, d¹⁷. The two exceptions are d² and its mirror d¹⁶, in all three d rows: element 26 (3d², 0.40 eV), element 39 (3d¹⁶, 0.77 eV), element 54 (4d², 0.30 eV), element 66 (4d¹⁶, 0.58 eV), element 114 (5d², 0.36 eV), element 126 (5d¹⁶, 0.63 eV). In each the ground term is a spin singlet of ten states, and the triplet the solver assumed lies 0.30–0.77 eV above it. In the f block 4f³ (element 83) fails too, and so does its mirror 4f²⁹ (element 108), three short of a full shell. Both have a ground term of spin ½, 0.15 and 0.26 eV below the quartet. No other f filling was found to break the rule, but the search is not complete. For f², f⁴, f⁵, f²⁸, f³⁰, f³¹ every spin was checked. For f⁶–f²⁵ only the highest two or three were, because the lower-spin blocks run to 10⁶–10⁸ arrangements; there the result is a bound, no lower term among the spins that could be run. Two fillings (elements 102 and 106) didn’t finish in the runner’s time.

6.3 The four-dimensional shell breaks it, not the law. It isn’t the law. On element 26’s own orbital Gauss’s 1/r21/r^2 gives the same order, with the singlet 0.82 eV below the triplet, against 0.40 eV under 1/r1/r; the pinned law makes the failure smaller, not larger. The cause is the shell itself. Four-dimensional d orbitals form the representation (1,1) of SO(4) ≅ SU(2) × SU(2)/ℤ₂. Two electrons in them make spatially symmetric states (2,2) ⊕ (2,0) ⊕ (0,2) ⊕ (1,1) ⊕ (0,0), all spin singlets (25, 10, 9 and 1 states), and antisymmetric ones (2,1) ⊕ (1,2) ⊕ (1,0) ⊕ (0,1), triplets (30 and 6). The singlet that comes lowest is the ten-state (2,0) ⊕ (0,2): two irreducible representations of SO(4), exchanged by a reflection, so a single level once reflections are counted. For two electrons in a d subshell each term’s energy is exactly F0+a2F2+a4F4F^0 + a_2F^2 + a_4F^4. The FnF^n are the radial parts of the repulsion and depend on the orbital. The an=⟨Cn(cosγ)⟩a_n = \langle C_n(\cos\gamma)\rangle over the term (γ\gamma is the angle between the two electrons seen from the nucleus) depend only on the term. a2a_2 measures how much the term lets the electrons line up along a common axis. The ten-state singlet holds them nearest right angles, ⟨cos2γ⟩\langle\cos^2\gamma\rangle = 0.125, half of random (0.250), where the best triplet reaches 0.188. It lies below every triplet whenever F4/F2<F^4/F^2 < 5/3. Element 26’s orbital has F4/F2F^4/F^2 = 0.39 under the law and 0.65 under Gauss’s, so the rule breaks either way. In ours the triplet ³F is the term that keeps the electrons nearest right angles (0.225, against ¹D’s 0.292 and random’s 0.333), and it is the lowest for every value of F4/F2F^4/F^2. Titanium’s d² has ³F lowest and its first singlet 1.25 eV up. Pauli keeps same-spin electrons apart, which is the usual reason for Hund’s rule; four-dimensional space gives paired spins a way to stay apart that is better still. In SO(4) terms, every rotation splits into a left and a right half, and a d orbital carries one unit of each. The singlet adds the two electrons’ left halves and cancels their right ones, or the other way round. Three-dimensional rotations don’t split, so ours has no such state. An interactive version is at four.mino.mobi/atom/hund.html.

What this establishes is narrower than a whole-atom ground term. The term energies are computed within one subshell on the atom’s own maximum-spin orbital, which is neither relaxed term by term nor correlated beyond the subshell, and the correction they give the table (section 6.4) is first order. The inversion holds under both laws tested, and for any orbital whose F4/F2F^4/F^2 is below 5/3. Hund’s first rule has been studied away from three dimensions, in two-dimensional helium-like atoms and quantum dots (Sako et al. 2012; Sako, Paldus, and Diercksen 2010) and by dimensional scaling (González 1991), and two-electron matrix elements for hydrogenic d² and f² pairs have been derived for any DD (Summerfield and Loeser 1999). We have not been able to read the last two in full, and none of the sources we could read states this inversion.

6.4 The correction moves energies and ionizations, no configuration. Where the rule fails, the solver’s energy for that configuration is the maximum-spin state’s, too high by the gap. The table in section 7 corrects that. Every d- and f-block element (92 of them) was searched again, with the neighbours’ configurations as extra seeds (49–65 settled candidates each). Every candidate got its open subshells’ gaps added, and the candidates were ranked again, with and without the correction, so that a change from the wider search is told apart from one from the terms. The terms change no ground configuration. They lower the eight Hund-breaking atoms’ energies, and they move first ionizations in pairs. An atom whose own ground term gains the gap holds its electron harder: 54 (6.41 → 6.71), 66 (18.82 → 19.40), 114 (6.92 → 7.27), 126 (24.92 → 25.55) eV. The next element, whose ion lands on the broken filling, gives one up more easily: 55 (8.28 → 7.91), 67 (20.56 → 19.93), 115 (9.28 → 8.83), 127 (27.23 → 26.56) eV. That is 0.30–0.67 eV either way. The wider search found one configuration the bake had missed. Element 82 is 4f¹ 5d¹ 6s², 0.36 eV below the bake’s 4f² 6s², which is our cerium’s kind; its ionization goes 7.03 → 7.39 eV. The table that follows is the corrected one (data/table4.csv). The Hund-spin table it replaces is supplement S1 (data/table4-hundspin.csv). How far to trust each configuration follows from appendix E.3. Take a bias of about a volt toward d and f: a ground configuration with a competitor holding fewer d and f electrons within a volt of it could be the other way round. 11 of the 92 d- and f-block elements have one: 30 (3d⁶ 4s², 0.55 eV up), 37 (3d¹³ 4s², 0.55 eV up), 53 (5s² 5p¹, 0.01 eV up), 58 (4d⁶ 5s², 0.20 eV up), 61 (4d⁹ 5s², 0.25 eV up), 64 (4d¹³ 5s¹, 0.95 eV up), 107 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.20 eV up), 108 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.79 eV up), 109 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.57 eV up), 122 (5d¹⁰ 6s², 0.54 eV up), 124 (5d¹³ 6s¹, 0.55 eV up).

6.5 The d² singlet in a ligand field. A spin singlet is not orbitally inert: the ten states of (2,0) ⊕ (0,2) still carry orbital structure, and in a compound a ligand field splits them, and might hand the ground to a triplet. The ion here is element 26’s 2+, 3d² over closed shells, with point charges −1 at 4 bohr in three arrangements of four dimensions: along the axes (eight, the 16-cell, four dimensions’ octahedron), at a tesseract’s corners (sixteen), and at the 24-cell’s vertices (twenty-four). The field is projected exactly onto the degrees a d × d product holds, scaled from zero, and every determinant of the two electrons is diagonalized, as in section 6.2. A four-dimensional d subshell splits 6 + 3 on the axes and 3 + 6 at the corners (ours’ octahedron 3 + 2, cube 2 + 3). On the axes the two electrons go into the six-fold set, and the singlet stays the ground at every field strength: its ten states split, the lowest of them 2-fold, an orbital doublet that a distortion could act on, and the lowest triplet stays 0.44 eV up, the free ion’s gap. At the corners the three-fold set is lowest, and two electrons in it favour a triplet: the singlet holds until the one-electron spread Δ\Delta reaches 2.4–2.5 eV, and a triplet is the ground beyond, but only just, 0.07 eV below the singlet even at Δ\Delta = 7.6 eV. The 24-cell splits the d subshell not at all (none): its vertices average every harmonic of degree 2 and 4 to zero, as the icosahedron does for ours, so a d ion in that site keeps its free-ion terms. Ours’ Ti²⁺ (3d²) is the check, with its triplet the ground at every field in both shapes, as Tanabe and Sugano (1954) found for d². These are point charges, a model of the field’s symmetry rather than of its size (atom/tools/ligandd2.mjs, data/ligandd2.json).

7 · The table

7.1 Rows and noble gases. The table’s rows hold 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, 60 elements (and 2 of the next by element 140), and its noble gases are elements 2, 12, 22, 50, 78, 138, each a peak of the first ionization energy (figures 2 and 3). Those are the rows the generalized Madelung rule of Négadi and Kibler (1996) gives when it is applied to four dimensions, which they stated for any dimension and worked out for two. To our knowledge this is the first self-consistent table that confirms them in four dimensions. The rule is a filling order. It builds the table’s skeleton of rows and blocks, which is how figure 2 is drawn. Like Madelung’s rule in ours, it doesn’t promise each atom’s ground configuration, and ours has exceptions to it, chromium and copper the first of about twenty. Read that way, 103 of the 140 ground configurations are exactly the rule’s. Each of the other 37 moves one or two electrons from one subshell to another: 34 from the outer s into the d or f subshell below it (10 of them two electrons), as our chromium, copper and palladium do, and the other 3 between other pairs: 81 (4f → 5d), 82 (4f → 5d) and 113 (5d → 6p). The solver finds more of these than there are. Over our first 36 elements it departs from the rule 6 times (Ti, V, Cr, Co, Ni, Cu), where the measured configurations depart 2 times (Cr and Cu). The reason is its lean toward d (appendix E.3), so the 4D count is likely too high, and these departures, not the rows, are where the table is least sure. Part III asks how much of the table the law decides.

7.2 The hardest-held electron isn’t a noble gas’s. Element 128 has an 18-electron d shell, full, with nothing outside it. It holds its outermost electron at 29.56 eV, harder than its row’s noble gas 138 (26.71 eV) and harder than any other atom in the table. Element 68, the same kind a row up, comes within a volt of its noble gas 78 (22.32 eV against 23.10). Ours goes the other way: palladium’s full 4d¹⁰ gives up an electron at 8.3 eV, xenon at 12.1 (Sansonetti and Martin 2005). Searching each ion for its own ground state leaves element 128’s ionization where it was. Seven other ions do rearrange, and their ionizations drop: 36 (11.42 → 10.94), 93 (9.14 → 8.90), 94 (9.20 → 8.43), 95 (9.27 → 7.99), 103 (9.96 → 9.67), 104 (10.05 → 9.29), 105 (10.13 → 8.92) eV. The main table carries those. Section 8 shows the law does it.

7.3 Every element in profile, on the interactive table. Figure 4 draws six atoms in profile: their radial densities, and what each one’s outermost orbital looks like on the 3-sphere (figure 5). The same two pictures for every element, from the same solver, are on the interactive table, where picking an element draws them and any of its subshells can be cut by any of the six planes of four-dimensional space (four.mino.mobi/table; a link ending #Z opens on element Z). Where the filling departs from the simple order, and how the ionization energy and the radius run with Z, are in the data files (table4.csv).

7.4 Valence states. Chemistry can pay to move an electron if the bonds it then makes pay more, so the reference records the cost. For each of 11 representative atoms, every configuration one electron away from the ground (any electron near the top moved to any subshell near the top) was solved by local exchange and by Hartree–Fock (table 1; all 11 with the ground’s spin ladder in data/valence.json). Ours is the calibration, 33 states against NIST’s levels (Sansonetti and Martin 2005). Moving an electron into a higher shell of the kind that makes a Rydberg series is good to a median 0.4 and 0.3 eV (18 states). The s→p promotion, the price of hybrid bonding, is not. For atoms whose p shell is less than half full (Be, B, C, Mg, Al, Si) both methods make it too cheap, by −2.3 to −0.6 (local exchange) and −1.7 to −0.9 (Hartree–Fock) eV, the near-degeneracy of an s² pair with p² that neither method holds; past half full (N, P, S) they make it too dear (−0.2 to 1.0 and 0.3 to 2.7 eV), where the measured state mixes with others. In four dimensions the p subshell holds eight, so every second-row atom up to element 8 is in the first group: element 4’s 2s→2p costs 1.3 and 1.5 eV by the two methods, darbon’s (element 7) 3.4 and 4.0 eV, element 14’s 3s→3p 3.1 and 2.9 eV, and on our evidence each is probably a volt or two short of the true cost. Element 26’s three lowest moves (into 4p, from 4s into 3d, from 3d into 4p) lie within 0.2 eV of each other by Hartree–Fock and a volt apart by local exchange, so its chemistry has several states to draw on and the methods disagree about which is cheapest. For element 53 Hartree–Fock finds a state below the table’s ground, the open configuration of appendix E.6.

7.5 Darbon’s hydride holds five hydrogens. The table implies a valence rule: an atom with vv electrons outside its core makes vv bonds, up to D+1D + 1 of them, and 2(D+1)−v2(D + 1) - v past that. That is a claim about molecules, and one hydride tests it directly. Darbon (element 7, 2s²2p³) should make five bonds, so DH₅ should exist, its hydrogens at the corners of the 5-cell, the simplest regular figure in four dimensions. Making five bonds takes the s→p promotion of section 7.4. At that geometry (D–H 3.23 bohr, by Hartree–Fock) we computed the whole curvature matrix of the energy by central differences, in all 24 coordinates of six atoms in four dimensions. With the 10 translations and rotations set apart, the 14 curvatures fall in groups of 5 + 4 + 1 + 4, as the 5-cell’s symmetry requires. None is negative: the lowest is 0.020 hartree/bohr², and the gradient is 5 × 10⁻⁶. So DH₅ is a true minimum, not a saddle that a symmetric search could settle on. It also lies below both balanced ways of coming apart. Losing one hydrogen, DH₅ → DH₄ + H, costs 1.55 eV by Hartree–Fock and 2.32 eV with MP2. Losing two as a molecule, DH₅ → DH₃ + H₂, costs 2.13 and 3.43 eV. Each fragment is in its lowest spin: DH₄ is a doublet and DH₃ a triplet, with DH₃’s singlet 1.01 (Hartree–Fock) and 0.85 eV (MP2) higher. Energies are by unrestricted Hartree–Fock and MP2 in the molecular basis. The fragments’ geometries were optimized only within symmetric shapes, with their hydrogens equivalent; DH₃ and DH₄ settle flat, their hydrogens in a plane or a 3-space through darbon. A lower, less symmetric fragment could lower both costs, and the curvature matrix, which describes DH₅ only, says nothing about that. The systematic test, every hydride and its fragments searched without symmetry, belongs to the chemistry this table starts.
