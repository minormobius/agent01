Part II · The table (draft)

6 · Checks against what is known

6.1 Hydrogen. One electron needs no search and no exchange, and four-dimensional hydrogen has an exact answer: −Z2/2(n+12)2-Z^2/2(n + \tfrac12)^2 hartree, so −2/9-2/9 for the ground state. The solver gives −0.22222.

6.2 Two electrons. Helium-like atoms in four dimensions are checked against an independent code, Hartree–Fock in a Gaussian basis written for any dimension (gauss.js), and the same atoms in ours against PySCF at the MP2 level (selftest blocks 17 and 43). The two codes share no part of the radial solver. From outside, the only accurate four-dimensional numbers we have been able to read are correlated variational energies for helium-like ions (Escobar-Ruiz et al. 2021). Hartree–Fock (hf.js, section 5.12) lies above them by 0.012–0.014 hartree for ZZ = 2–10, as it must, since they include correlation; that is the size and sign of a correlation energy, a consistency check rather than a benchmark. A four-dimensional Hartree–Fock limit to test against (Loeser and Herschbach 1986) is in tables we have not obtained.

6.3 Our own table. The same solver, the same law (in ours 1/r is Gauss’s) and the same search, run in three dimensions, gives 32 of 36 of our measured ground configurations. The four it misses, titanium, vanadium, cobalt and nickel, each take one d electron too many against 4s, the known lean of local exchange toward d. The misses have one cause. Moving an electron from 4s into 3d comes out too cheap in every 3d atom we can compare (eV, the solver against measured: Sc 0.14 against 1.43, Ti −0.89 against 0.81, Fe 0.51 against 0.87, Co −0.35 against 0.42, Ni −1.16 against −0.03), short by 0.37–1.70 eV and a mean 1.05, always in d’s favour. The measured values are differences of each configuration’s lowest term, its levels averaged with weights 2J+12J + 1, from NIST’s level tables (Sansonetti and Martin 2005), because the solver has no spin–orbit coupling and lands each configuration in its lowest term. Taking the lowest level of each instead moves them by at most 0.06 eV and turns nickel’s sign (+0.03 rather than −0.03). That is local exchange without correlation. Resolving every open subshell’s whole term structure doesn’t remove it. With that correction (the lowest term less the average of the maximum-spin arrangements, which is what a spherically averaged field stands for), the bias stays at 1.05 eV and the configurations get worse, 26 of 32 against 28 of 32. So the paper corrects only Hund’s first rule (section 7), the one thing a spin-polarized averaged field cannot represent. Its first ionization energies are off by a median 4.3 % and at most 16.2 % (figure A2). That is the accuracy to expect of the 4D table: a method that gets our transition metals mostly right and our ionizations to a few percent, not a spectroscopic one.

6.4 Ions. Each ion of ours searched for its own ground state, rather than made by taking one electron from the atom’s configuration, changes no first ionization. Searched the same way, our +2 and +3 ions give second and third ionization energies within a median 2.0 % and 1.4 % of measured ones (Kramida et al. 2026) (worst V, 7 % and C, 4 %), closer than the first ionizations’ 4.3 %, and they lose their 4s electrons before 3d, as measured (Fe⁺ 3d⁶4s, Fe²⁺ 3d⁶, Fe³⁺ 3d⁵). In four dimensions the 3d row’s ions do the same. Elements in the first half of the 4d and 5d blocks, and the two that open row 6’s f block with a 5d electron, instead give up a d electron at +1 or +2 while keeping the outer s (53–56, 58, 61, 81–82, 114–118, 120, 122), as our yttrium and lutetium do (Y⁺ 5s², Lu⁺ 6s²). The same search for anions gives our electron affinities where the extra electron is bound by the field and exchange: the p-block acceptors (C 1.32 against 1.262, Si 1.30 against 1.39, Ge 1.25 against 1.233, Al 0.40 against 0.433, F 2.80 against 3.401, Cl 3.15 against 3.613, O 0.71 against 1.461 eV, measured second). It also finds every anion that should be unbound to be unbound: the noble gases, beryllium, magnesium and nitrogen. It fails where correlation does the binding (H -0.33 against 0.754, Li -0.08 against 0.618, Na -0.02 against 0.548, P -0.10 against 0.746). In ours, then, the method’s affinities hold above about 0.7 eV and not below (the measured values are from Rienstra-Kiracofe et al. (2002)). That is a calibration in our world, not an error bar for four dimensions: an affinity near threshold there would need its own checks, a wider radial grid for the extra electron, the anion’s and the atom’s fields both converged, and correlation.

6.5 Numbers. Tightening the solver’s stop or changing its grid moves no ionization energy by a millihartree (section 5 and appendix C).

6.6 A second method. Hartree–Fock (section 5.12) on the same candidates gets 36 of 36 ground configurations of ours right, against local exchange’s 32. The two agree on 32, and every one of those is right; they differ on Ti, V, Co and Ni, and on each of these Hartree–Fock is right. The promotion of a 4s electron into 3d shows what each method does, against the same measured centroids as section 6.3 (eV, error of local exchange and of Hartree–Fock: Sc −1.29/−0.42, Ti −1.70/−0.27, V −2.09/−0.12, Cr −1.73/−0.26, Mn −0.72/+1.19, Fe −0.37/+0.93, Co −0.76/+1.11, Ni −1.13/+1.31, Cu −0.44/+1.12). Local exchange makes d too cheap in all nine. Hartree–Fock makes it a little too cheap up to half filling (Sc–Cr, −0.42 to −0.12) and too dear after it (Mn–Cu, 0.93 to 1.31). So the two do not bracket the measured value; from scandium to chromium both lie on the same side of it. Their difference is a measure of how much an answer depends on the method, not an error bar: neither method bounds the other, and both omit the correlation that would move each toward the measured values.

In four dimensions the two agree on 126 of the 140 elements, and these are the table’s firm configurations. They differ on 14, which are open: 7 in the d rows (30, 31, 37, 38, 40, 58, 61), where local exchange puts one electron more in d than Hartree–Fock, as in ours; 5 at the end of row 6’s f block (107, 108, 109, 110, 111), where it puts one more in f; and 2 at the start of a block (53, 83). For the d rows, ours gives a lean. Before half filling (30, 31, 58) Hartree–Fock’s error in ours was small and in the same direction as local exchange’s, so its preference for s² should hold. After it (37, 38, 40, 61) its margins, 0.10 to 1.08 eV, are inside the volt by which it overprices d in ours. Hartree–Fock separates f configurations far more widely than d ones (its margins in the f row are 2.35 to 5.80 eV), and this paper’s table of ours has no f block to test that against. No Hartree–Fock ground configuration lies near the edge of the window of candidates, so none is likely to have been missed by the window alone. Every element’s verdict, both methods’ rankings and the margins are in data/hftable.json.

7 · Hund’s first rule in four dimensions

The solver gives each open subshell the most parallel spins it can hold. That is Hund’s first rule, and in ours it is the known answer for the ground terms of free atoms. In four dimensions it is an assumption, and for some fillings it is wrong.

7.1 How the terms are found. For each open subshell of each ground configuration, its orbitals are the degree-ll harmonics on the 3-sphere, (l+1)2(l + 1)^2 of them (4 for p, 9 for d, 16 for f). The repulsion between two of its electrons is the law’s 1/|x−y|1/|x - y|, projected exactly onto the harmonics of degree 00 to 2l2l, with the radial parts integrated over the atom’s own orbital. Every arrangement of the subshell’s electrons with a given MSM_S is set up and the lowest energy found (Lanczos, matrix-free; a 4D d⁹ block has 15 876 arrangements). A term of spin SS appears in every block with MS≤SM_S \le S, so the ground term’s spin is the largest MSM_S whose block still reaches the lowest energy. In ours this reproduces Racah’s term separations for d² and d³ to the wavenumber, and the known ground terms of Cr³⁺, Mn²⁺, Pr³⁺ and Gd³⁺. Over all 23 open subshells of our table, maximum spin is lowest, as it should be.

7.2 Where it fails. In four dimensions all 36 open p subshells of the table keep the rule, and so do the d fillings d¹, d³–d¹², d¹⁴, d¹⁵, d¹⁷. The two exceptions are d² and its mirror d¹⁶, in all three d rows: element 26 (3d², 0.40 eV), element 39 (3d¹⁶, 0.77 eV), element 54 (4d², 0.30 eV), element 66 (4d¹⁶, 0.58 eV), element 114 (5d², 0.36 eV), element 126 (5d¹⁶, 0.63 eV). In each the ground term is a spin singlet of ten states, and the triplet the solver assumed lies 0.30–0.77 eV above it. In the f block 4f³ (element 83) fails too, and so does its mirror 4f²⁹ (element 108), three short of a full shell. Both have a ground term of spin ½, 0.15 and 0.26 eV below the quartet. No other f filling was found to break the rule, but the search is not complete. For f², f⁴, f⁵, f²⁸, f³⁰, f³¹ every spin was checked. For f⁶–f²⁵ only the highest two or three were, because the lower-spin blocks run to 10⁶–10⁸ arrangements; there the result is a bound, no lower term among the spins that could be run. Two fillings (elements 102 and 106) didn’t finish in the runner’s time.

7.3 Why it fails. It isn’t the law. On element 26’s own orbital Gauss’s 1/r21/r^2 gives the same order, with the singlet 0.82 eV below the triplet, against 0.40 eV under 1/r1/r; the pinned law makes the failure smaller, not larger. The cause is the shell itself. Four-dimensional d orbitals form the representation (1,1) of SO(4) ≅ SU(2) × SU(2)/ℤ₂. Two electrons in them make spatially symmetric states (2,2) ⊕ (2,0) ⊕ (0,2) ⊕ (1,1) ⊕ (0,0), all spin singlets (25, 10, 9 and 1 states), and antisymmetric ones (2,1) ⊕ (1,2) ⊕ (1,0) ⊕ (0,1), triplets (30 and 6). The singlet that comes lowest is the ten-state (2,0) ⊕ (0,2): two irreducible representations of SO(4), exchanged by a reflection, so a single level once reflections are counted. For two electrons in a d subshell each term’s energy is exactly F0+a2F2+a4F4F^0 + a_2F^2 + a_4F^4. The FnF^n are the radial parts of the repulsion and depend on the orbital. The an=⟨Cn(cosγ)⟩a_n = \langle C_n(\cos\gamma)\rangle over the term (γ\gamma is the angle between the two electrons seen from the nucleus) depend only on the term. a2a_2 measures how much the term lets the electrons line up along a common axis. The ten-state singlet holds them nearest right angles, ⟨cos2γ⟩\langle\cos^2\gamma\rangle = 0.125, half of random (0.250), where the best triplet reaches 0.188. It lies below every triplet whenever F4/F2<F^4/F^2 < 5/3. Element 26’s orbital has F4/F2F^4/F^2 = 0.39 under the law and 0.65 under Gauss’s, so the rule breaks either way. In ours the triplet ³F is the term that keeps the electrons nearest right angles (0.225, against ¹D’s 0.292 and random’s 0.333), and it is the lowest for every value of F4/F2F^4/F^2. Titanium’s d² has ³F lowest and its first singlet 1.25 eV up. Pauli keeps same-spin electrons apart, which is the usual reason for Hund’s rule; four-dimensional space gives paired spins a way to stay apart that is better still. In SO(4) terms, every rotation splits into a left and a right half, and a d orbital carries one unit of each. The singlet adds the two electrons’ left halves and cancels their right ones, or the other way round. Three-dimensional rotations don’t split, so ours has no such state. An interactive version is at four.mino.mobi/atom/hund.html.

What this establishes is narrower than a whole-atom ground term. The term energies are computed within one subshell on the atom’s own maximum-spin orbital, which is neither relaxed term by term nor correlated beyond the subshell, and the correction they give the table (section 7.4) is first order. The inversion holds under both laws tested, and for any orbital whose F4/F2F^4/F^2 is below 5/3. Hund’s first rule has been studied away from three dimensions, in two-dimensional helium-like atoms and quantum dots (Sako et al. 2012; Sako, Paldus, and Diercksen 2010) and by dimensional scaling (González 1991), and two-electron matrix elements for hydrogenic d² and f² pairs have been derived for any DD (Summerfield and Loeser 1999). We have not been able to read the last two in full, and none of the sources we could read states this inversion.

7.4 What it does to the table. Where the rule fails, the solver’s energy for that configuration is the maximum-spin state’s, too high by the gap. The table in section 8 corrects that. Every d- and f-block element (92 of them) was searched again, with the neighbours’ configurations as extra seeds (49–65 settled candidates each). Every candidate got its open subshells’ gaps added, and the candidates were ranked again, with and without the correction, so that a change from the wider search is told apart from one from the terms. The terms change no ground configuration. They lower the eight Hund-breaking atoms’ energies, and they move first ionizations in pairs. An atom whose own ground term gains the gap holds its electron harder: 54 (6.41 → 6.71), 66 (18.82 → 19.40), 114 (6.92 → 7.27), 126 (24.92 → 25.55) eV. The next element, whose ion lands on the broken filling, gives one up more easily: 55 (8.28 → 7.91), 67 (20.56 → 19.93), 115 (9.28 → 8.83), 127 (27.23 → 26.56) eV. That is 0.30–0.67 eV either way. The wider search found one configuration the bake had missed. Element 82 is 4f¹ 5d¹ 6s², 0.36 eV below the bake’s 4f² 6s², which is our cerium’s kind; its ionization goes 7.03 → 7.39 eV. The table that follows is the corrected one (data/table4.csv). The Hund-spin table it replaces is supplement S1 (data/table4-hundspin.csv). How far to trust each configuration follows from section 6.3. Take a bias of about a volt toward d and f: a ground configuration with a competitor holding fewer d and f electrons within a volt of it could be the other way round. 11 of the 92 d- and f-block elements have one: 30 (3d⁶ 4s², 0.55 eV up), 37 (3d¹³ 4s², 0.55 eV up), 53 (5s² 5p¹, 0.01 eV up), 58 (4d⁶ 5s², 0.20 eV up), 61 (4d⁹ 5s², 0.25 eV up), 64 (4d¹³ 5s¹, 0.95 eV up), 107 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.20 eV up), 108 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.79 eV up), 109 (5p⁸ 6s², 0.57 eV up), 122 (5d¹⁰ 6s², 0.54 eV up), 124 (5d¹³ 6s¹, 0.55 eV up).

8 · The table

8.1 Rows and noble gases. The table’s rows hold 2, 10, 10, 28, 28, 60 elements (and 2 of the next by element 140), and its noble gases are elements 2, 12, 22, 50, 78, 138, each a peak of the first ionization energy (figures 2 and 3). Those are the rows the generalized Madelung rule of Négadi and Kibler (1996) gives when it is applied to four dimensions, which they stated for any dimension and worked out for two. To our knowledge this is the first self-consistent table that confirms them in four dimensions. The rule is a filling order. It builds the table’s skeleton of rows and blocks, which is how figure 2 is drawn. Like Madelung’s rule in ours, it doesn’t promise each atom’s ground configuration, and ours has exceptions to it, chromium and copper the first of about twenty. Read that way, 103 of the 140 ground configurations are exactly the rule’s. Each of the other 37 moves one or two electrons from one subshell to another: 34 from the outer s into the d or f subshell below it (10 of them two electrons), as our chromium, copper and palladium do, and the other 3 between other pairs: 81 (4f → 5d), 82 (4f → 5d) and 113 (5d → 6p). The solver finds more of these than there are. Over our first 36 elements it departs from the rule 6 times (Ti, V, Cr, Co, Ni, Cu), where the measured configurations depart 2 times (Cr and Cu). The reason is its lean toward d (section 6.3), so the 4D count is likely too high, and these departures, not the rows, are where the table is least sure. Part III asks how much of the table the law decides.

8.2 The hardest-held electron isn’t a noble gas’s. Element 128 has an 18-electron d shell, full, with nothing outside it. It holds its outermost electron at 29.56 eV, harder than its row’s noble gas 138 (26.71 eV) and harder than any other atom in the table. Element 68, the same kind a row up, comes within a volt of its noble gas 78 (22.32 eV against 23.10). Ours goes the other way: palladium’s full 4d¹⁰ gives up an electron at 8.3 eV, xenon at 12.1 (Sansonetti and Martin 2005). Searching each ion for its own ground state leaves element 128’s ionization where it was. Seven other ions do rearrange, and their ionizations drop: 36 (11.42 → 10.94), 93 (9.14 → 8.90), 94 (9.20 → 8.43), 95 (9.27 → 7.99), 103 (9.96 → 9.67), 104 (10.05 → 9.29), 105 (10.13 → 8.92) eV. The main table carries those. Section 9 shows the law does it.
