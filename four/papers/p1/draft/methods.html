5 · Methods

5.1 The code. Everything in this paper is computed by code written for it: JavaScript with no outside libraries, run under Node.js, and the same files run in a browser (four.mino.mobi/atom). The atom is atom/js/atom.js, with the shell kernel in kernel.js. One electron in any law is papers/p1/laws.js, and the terms of open subshells are atom/tools/hund.mjs and termtable.mjs. Nothing is taken from a quantum-chemistry package. Two independent codes check it (section 6.2): atom/js/gauss.js, Hartree–Fock in a Gaussian basis (Boys 1950) for any DD, with the integrals of McMurchie and Davidson (1978), and in ours PySCF (Sun et al. 2018, 2020). Every number quoted is read from the code’s output by the paper’s build (appendix E).

5.2 The law inside an atom. An atom here is a point nucleus of charge ZZ and ZZ electrons, non-relativistic, with every pair of charges interacting through the pinned potential q1q2/rq_1q_2/r in four spatial dimensions. The same law acts between an electron and the nucleus and between two electrons; nothing in the method borrows Gauss’s law. Atomic units throughout (hartree, bohr).

Each electron moves in the field of the nucleus and of the other electrons’ clouds, averaged over directions, so the problem is radial. In DD dimensions a wavefunction ψ=r−(D−1)/2u(r)Y\psi = r^{-(D-1)/2}\,u(r)\,Y, with YY a harmonic of degree ll on the (D−1)(D - 1)-sphere, has a radial function uu that obeys

−12u″+[L(L+1)2r2+V(r)]u=εu,L=l+D−32,-\tfrac12\,u'' + \Big[\frac{L(L+1)}{2r^2} + V(r)\Big]\,u = \varepsilon\,u, \qquad L = l + \frac{D-3}{2},

so four dimensions differ from ours by the half-integer shift of LL (L=l+12L = l + \tfrac12) and by the degeneracies: a subshell of angular momentum ll holds 2(2l+1)2(2l + 1) electrons in ours and 2(l+1)22(l + 1)^2 in four dimensions. With V=−Z/rV = -Z/r the levels are −Z2/2(n+(D−3)/2)2-Z^2/2(n + (D - 3)/2)^2, so 4D hydrogen’s ground state is −2/9-2/9 hartree; the solver gives −0.22222 (section 6.1).

5.3 The clouds’ potential. A spherical shell of charge at radius ss, felt at radius rr, gives in ours 1/max(r,s)1/\max(r, s) (the shell theorem). In four dimensions under 1/r1/r it gives κ(t)/max(r,s)\kappa(t)/\max(r, s), t=min/maxt = \min/\max, with κ\kappa the 1/r1/r kernel averaged over a 3-sphere,

κ(t)=2π∫0πsin2θdθ1+t2−2tcosθ,\kappa(t) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin^2\theta\,d\theta}{\sqrt{1 + t^2 - 2t\cos\theta}},

which falls from κ(0)=1\kappa(0) = 1 to κ(1)=8/3π\kappa(1) = 8/3\pi = 0.848826 (appendix A; Simpson’s rule on 400 intervals, then a table of 2001 points with linear interpolation). Every electron–electron term in the self-consistent field, its energy and the ionized atom’s goes through this kernel. That is where the missing shell theorem enters the atom: an inner cloud screens the nucleus from an outer electron only partly.

5.4 Exchange. Local exchange from the uniform electron gas of the same dimension and the same law, εx(ρ)=−ADρ1/D\varepsilon_x(\rho) = -A_D\,\rho^{1/D} per electron for an electron density ρ\rho, with A3=34(3/π)1/3A_3 = \tfrac34(3/\pi)^{1/3} = 0.738559 and A4=16/15πA_4 = 16/15\sqrt\pi = 0.601802, as in the general-DD formulas of Loos and Gill (2016) for 1/r1/r jellium (appendix B). Spins are polarized by Hund’s rule (a subshell fills one spin first), and each electron’s own share is removed from both its Hartree and its exchange potential: the Hartree term by its own orbital’s cloud, the exchange term by the fully polarized exchange of that orbital alone, in the manner of the self-interaction correction of Perdew and Zunger (1981). There is no correlation term.

5.5 Solving one configuration. The radial equation is solved on a logarithmic grid of 700 points from r0r_0 = 10⁻⁵ to 150 bohr (step hh = 0.02364 in lnr\ln r) by Numerov’s method on y=u/ry = u/\sqrt r, with each level found by Sturm bisection on the node count of the outward solution (90 halvings), the outward integration stopped where h2f/12>12h^2 f/12 > \tfrac12 (for y″=fyy'' = f\,y) so that Numerov cannot fake nodes in the forbidden region, and the orbital matched to an inward solution at the turning point.

For a fixed configuration (occupations of each subshell, every orbital of a subshell sharing one radial function) the field is iterated to self-consistency: each orbital solved in the potential of the nucleus, the others’ clouds and exchange; the new clouds mixed into the old at 0.4; up to 80 iterations, stopping when the total energy changes by less than 10⁻⁷ hartree between iterations.

5.6 Is the stop tight enough? A stop on the energy can leave the far density less converged than the energy is; the plane-wave work met exactly that in its pseudopotentials’ all-electron atom, where exchange carried an unconverged tail into the potential. So 19 atoms across every row of both worlds were re-solved in their baked configurations and then driven on from that field to a stop of 10⁻¹² hartree (appendix C, audit-scf.mjs). Total energies and first ionization energies moved by at most 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ and 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ hartree, the outermost orbital energy by 1.7 × 10⁻⁶, and Q=∑⟨r2⟩Q = \sum\langle r^2\rangle (the far pull’s, and the forces between atoms’) by 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ of itself; the baked ⟨r2⟩\langle r^2\rangle agree with the tight ones to 7.0 × 10⁻⁵. The density beyond about 15 bohr is not converged relatively, but it is noise there: off by large factors only where it is 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻¹⁷ electrons per bohr, and converged to 10⁻⁶ where an atom has a loosely held s electron that puts real charge out that far. Nothing the paper quotes depends on it; a calculation that builds a potential from the far density through exchange must converge the density pointwise, as the pseudopotentials now do.

5.7 Is the grid fine enough? 11 atoms from every row of both worlds were re-solved in their baked configurations on six other grids, changing one of the number of points (500, 1000), the inner end (10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁶ bohr) and the outer end (100, 250 bohr) at a time (appendix C, convergence.mjs). First ionization energies moved by at most 1.4 × 10⁻⁴ hartree on any of them. Total energies moved by at most 6.7 × 10⁻² hartree, 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ of themselves, the largest for our own krypton, whose 1s sits deeper than any four-dimensional atom’s and is the grid’s hardest case. The paper quotes ionization energies and energy differences, not total energies.

5.8 The ground configuration. For each ZZ the ground configuration is searched, not assumed. The search starts from the hydrogenic filling (subshells in order of n, then l), solves it, and refills by the orbital energies of every subshell, occupied or not, in the field it has just found, up to 6 rounds or until the filling stops changing. It then tries moving one or two electrons between the open subshells, the two highest filled ones and the two lowest empty ones, solving each candidate from the current field. A candidate counts only if its field settles: an unsettled field can report an energy far too low, since with exchange a barely bound orbital can drift for hundreds of iterations. The lowest settled energy is the ground state.

For 7 elements of the 4D table (88, 101–103 and 105–107) the first search landed on an unsettled field. They were searched again seeded from both neighbours’ configurations with one or two electrons added or taken, each seed solved from scratch, and the lowest settled one kept.

The first ionization energy is ΔSCF, a difference of two self-consistent energies: the ion’s own ground state with an electron taken from whichever of the 3 least-bound occupied subshells leaves it lowest, relaxed from the atom’s field, minus the atom’s energy. Koopmans’ orbital energies are not used.

The table is 140 elements in four dimensions and 36 in ours, baked once (on GitHub’s runners past Z≈80Z \approx 80) into a single generated file, which a selftest re-solves in sample on every change.

5.9 The terms of open subshells. The search above gives every open subshell Hund spins. Section 7 finds where that’s wrong, and the table is corrected for it. Each d- and f-block element is searched again, seeded as the repair pass seeds, and every settled candidate’s energy gets its open subshells’ term correction: the lowest term less the lowest maximum-spin term. That comes from the subshell’s own repulsion on that candidate’s own orbital (atom/tools/termtable.mjs). It is zero wherever Hund’s first rule holds. The ionization is corrected the same way on both sides. Fillings whose spin blocks are too large to solve per candidate take the survey’s verdict (section 7.2), which for them is that the rule holds.

5.10 The counterfactual. To separate what the table owes to the law from what it owes to the geometry, every 4D element is solved a second time with the shell theorem forced on: the clouds’ potential taken as 1/max(r,s)1/\max(r, s) (our kernel) while everything else, the 4D kinetic operator, the degeneracies, exchange, the search, stays as it was. Each element’s search is seeded with the real table’s configuration and its neighbours’ ± one electron, so the counterfactual isn’t a weaker search; and the real law is re-solved in each counterfactual configuration, to check that the real table’s configuration is still the lower one under the law. Where no candidate’s field settled, a second, wider search started from every configuration in reach (the real table’s for the element and its neighbours ± one electron, the unsettled filling, and the counterfactual’s own neighbours ± one), each solved from scratch with the clouds mixed at 0.15 for up to 1500 iterations. A counterfactual atom counts as settled only if its field converged and its first ionization energy is positive (a negative one means an electron would rather leave: the search missed a lower configuration). By that rule 112 of 140 settle, every atom up to 88; the rest (89–116) are row 6’s f block and the first elements after it.

5.11 One electron, any law. The arguments of section 2 use a separate solver for one electron in any central potential (laws.js): the same radial equation and Numerov–Sturm method on a grid whose inner end can be moved, which is what tests a fall to the centre. For V=−α/r2V = -\alpha/r^2 it finds no level below α=12\alpha = \tfrac12 on any grid and, above it, a lowest level that scales as 1/r021/r_0^2 (section 2.1); for V=−1/rηV = -1/r^\eta it gives the splittings within each shell (section 3.1). Its check is 4D hydrogen: −0.22222222 against −2/9-2/9.

5.12 Checks. Every method above has a known-answer test in the selftest, run on every change: hydrogen in both worlds (block 1), the radial solver (2), the kernel against direct integration (3), exchange against the electron-gas constants (4, 18), the ground search (5, 7), two-electron atoms against an independent Gaussian Hartree–Fock code in four dimensions and MP2 against PySCF in ours (17, 43), our table against measured values (6: 32 of 36 configurations; ionization to a 4.3 % median error), and this paper’s own claims recomputed from the bake (67).
