Part I · The law (draft)

2 · Why a law has to be chosen

Ask what the atoms are in a world with four dimensions of space and there is no answer yet, because the question leaves out the law between charges. In our world that law isn’t a free choice. Gauss’s law, which is what a local, linear field theory of charge gives, says the field of a point charge spreads over the sphere around it; the sphere’s area grows as rD−1r^{D-1}, so the potential falls as r−(D−2)r^{-(D-2)}. In three dimensions that is 1/r1/r. In four it is 1/r21/r^2, and with 1/r21/r^2 there are no atoms.

2.1 Gauss’s law leaves no atom. One electron in V=−α/r2V = -\alpha/r^2 has a Hamiltonian with no length in it: stretch every distance by a factor and the kinetic and potential energies scale alike. In four dimensions Hardy’s inequality puts a floor under the kinetic energy,

∫|∇ψ|2≥(D−22)2∫|ψ|2r2=∫|ψ|2r2(D=4),\int |\nabla\psi|^2 \;\ge\; \Big(\frac{D-2}{2}\Big)^2 \int \frac{|\psi|^2}{r^2} \;=\; \int \frac{|\psi|^2}{r^2} \quad (D = 4),

so with the kinetic energy 12|∇ψ|2\tfrac12|\nabla\psi|^2 the pull wins only for α>12\alpha > \tfrac12. Below that nothing binds; above it the electron falls to the centre (Case 1950). We solved it rather than argue it (section 5, laws.js): for α=0.45\alpha = 0.45 there is no bound level on any grid, and for α=0.7\alpha = 0.7 the lowest level is −5.87 × 10³, −5.87 × 10⁵ and −5.87 × 10⁷ hartree on grids whose inner end is 10−310^{-3}, 10−410^{-4} and 10−510^{-5} bohr: it grows as 1/r021/r_0^2 (εr02\varepsilon r_0^2 = −0.00587, −0.00587), set by where the calculation stops rather than by the electron (figure 2a). Either way there is no Bohr radius, no size an atom could settle at. Ehrenfest made the classical version of this argument in 1917: under Gauss’s law in four dimensions no planetary orbit is stable either.

2.2 The ways out, and what each costs. Something has to give. The squeeze that resists the pull costs ℏ2/2mr2\hbar^2/2mr^2 because the electron’s energy grows as p2p^2; the pull costs 1/r21/r^2 because of Gauss. Change either and a length comes back. One way is to change the electron: an energy growing as p3p^3 or p4p^4 outgrows a 1/r21/r^2 pull close in, so atoms bind at every strength. It keeps Gauss’s law, and with it the shell theorem and the Faraday cage, but it gives up Galilean relativity for all matter: a moving laboratory and a still one see the same physics only if energy is quadratic in momentum. Another is a small fourth dimension, the way extra dimensions could hide in ours; but atoms larger than it are three-dimensional atoms and smaller ones fall again, so it doesn’t give four-dimensional chemistry. The third is to change the law: keep the electron, keep four large dimensions, and make the potential between charges fall as 1/r1/r. This universe takes the third, and the rest of part I says why 1/r1/r and what it costs. The choice between the first and the third is a judgement, stated as one: the electron route keeps our Faraday cages, the law route keeps our mechanics.

3 · Why 1/r, exactly

3.1 Only 1/r keeps a shell together. Among power laws V=−1/rpV = -1/r^p, those with 0<p<20 < p < 2 bind with a length of their own. Only p=1p = 1 has the hidden symmetry of the hydrogen problem, SO(D+1D+1) in DD dimensions (Fock 1935; Bander and Itzykson 1966), which makes a level depend on nn alone: in four dimensions −Z2/2(n+12)2-Z^2/2(n + \tfrac12)^2, with every ll below nn at the same energy. That degeneracy is what makes a shell, and four-dimensional shells hold ∑l<n2(l+1)2\sum_{l<n} 2(l+1)^2 electrons: 2, 10, 28, 60. We measured how fast it breaks (figure 2b): the 2p level sits −6.2 % from 2s at p=0.8p = 0.8, 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ % at p=1p = 1 and +15.4 % at p=1.2p = 1.2 (as a fraction of the shell’s binding; 3d against 3s goes the same way). Below 1 the more-turning electron is the more bound, above it the less, and either way the shell comes apart. The rows of the periodic table in section 8 rest on this.

3.2 The convention it agrees with. The literature on hydrogen in DD dimensions (Nieto 1979; Al-Jaber 1998) and on dimensional scaling (Herschbach, Avery and Goscinski 1992) takes 1/r1/r in every DD, mostly as a convention that keeps the problem solvable. Négadi and Kibler’s generalized Madelung rule (1996), which predicts the four-dimensional rows, builds on the same DD-dimensional hydrogen atom. Section 3.1 is why that convention is more than one: it is the only power law under which the question “what are the shells?” has the answer the rule needs.

4 · What 1/r costs in four dimensions

4.1 It isn’t harmonic. In three dimensions ∇2(1/r)=0\nabla^2(1/r) = 0 away from the charge, and that one fact is Gauss’s law. In DD dimensions ∇2rq=q(q+D−2)rq−2\nabla^2 r^q = q(q + D - 2)\,r^{q-2}, so in four

∇21r=−1r3(r≠0),\nabla^2 \frac{1}{r} = -\frac{1}{r^3} \qquad (r \ne 0),

which we check numerically (−1.00000 at r=1r = 1, −0.12500 at r=2r = 2). The flux of the field through a sphere round a charge grows with the sphere’s radius, linearly in four dimensions, as if empty space were charged with a density ∝1/r3\propto 1/r^3. There is no Gauss’s law for it.

4.2 No shell theorem, no Faraday cage. A thin spherical shell of charge, felt at radius rr, gives k(t)/max(r,s)k(t)/\max(r, s) with t=min/maxt = \min/\max and kk the 1/r1/r kernel averaged over the 3-sphere (section 5, appendix A). In three dimensions k=1k = 1: the shell theorem. In four, kk falls from 1 at the centre to k(1)=8/3πk(1) = 8/3\pi = 0.848826 at the shell (figure A1). So a cloud of charge never acts as if it sat at its centre; a hollow conductor’s centre sits at 3π/83\pi/8 = 1.178097 of its wall’s potential, and a charge inside is pushed toward the wall; and a neutral atom is felt from outside, as −∑⟨r2⟩/8s3-\sum\langle r^2\rangle/8s^3 at distance ss (section 11). Everything distinctive in part III comes from here.

4.3 A field theory for it. In Maxwell’s theory a charge’s potential is the Fourier transform of 1/k21/k^2, which is 1/r21/r^2 in four dimensions; 1/r1/r needs 1/k31/k^3. The book’s force chapter goes through five ways to get it:

  1. A local, relativistic field: impossible. 1/k31/k^3 isn’t a whole number of derivatives.
  2. Relativistic but non-local, □3/2\Box^{3/2}: the field’s scaling dimension is 1, below the unitarity bound of 1.5, so the theory has states of negative probability.
  3. A vacuum whose permittivity grows with wavenumber, ε(k)=ε0ℓk\varepsilon(k) = \varepsilon_0 \ell k: statics give 1/r1/r, but light disperses like deep-water waves, ω=ck/ℓ\omega = c\sqrt{k/\ell}, and there is no speed of light.
  4. A nonlinear vacuum, ∇⋅(|E|m−2E)=ρ\nabla\cdot(|E|^{m-2}E) = \rho with mm = 2.5: 1/r1/r, but fields no longer add.
  5. A small fourth dimension: 1/r1/r far away, but then atoms are three-dimensional (section 2.2).

This universe takes a sixth, which finishes route 3: the lens vacuum, ε(k)=ε0ℓk\varepsilon(k) = \varepsilon_0 \ell k and μ(k)=μ0/ℓk\mu(k) = \mu_0/\ell k. Then D/ε0D/\varepsilon_0 and μ0H\mu_0 H obey Maxwell’s equations in empty four-dimensional space exactly, so light is ordinary four-dimensional light at speed cc at every wavelength, and the energy is never negative. What pushes a charge is that light seen through a lens, E=(D/ε0)/ℓkE = (D/\varepsilon_0)/\ell k, which blurs it across space with a 1/r31/r^3 kernel; its statics are 1/ℓk31/\ell k^3, the pinned 1/r1/r. The price is relativity, for the force between charges only: the blur acts across all of space at once, so there is an absolute “now”, and a charge feels another’s change instantly, a little, everywhere. Atoms are static, so nothing in this paper depends on ℓ\ell or on the absolute now; the field theory is the subject of a later paper.

4.4 What is declared with it. Electrons are spin-½ fermions, two to an orbital. That is stated, not derived: in our world it follows from relativity and spin together, and this universe has given up relativity for the force. Nuclei are point charges +Z+Z, stable for every ZZ to 140 (declared: nuclear physics in four dimensions is another paper). The fine-structure constant is small, so atoms are solved without relativity (section 12). No other long-range field acts on charges.

4.5 Is matter stable? In ours it is a theorem that NN atoms have an energy proportional to NN (Dyson and Lenard 1967; Lieb and Thirring 1975), and the proofs lean on the shell theorem, which fails here. So we give evidence, not a proof. Atoms don’t collapse: Thomas–Fermi scaling gives total energies growing as Z3−2/DZ^{3-2/D}, Z7/3Z^{7/3} in ours and Z5/2Z^{5/2} in four dimensions (Kventsel and Katriel 1981), and the solved table follows it, with a fitted exponent of 2.527 over ZZ = 10–140 (ours: 2.387 over 10–36; both a little above the asymptotic value, as finite-ZZ corrections make them). Many atoms don’t run away either: every force between neutral atoms here (the permanent −3SASB/64R5-3S_AS_B/64R^5 of section 11, dispersion’s R−6R^{-6}) falls faster than R−4R^{-4}, so summed over four-dimensional space each atom’s share is finite. This is argued, not proved, and the paper says so.
