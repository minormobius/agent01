2 · The law between charges

Ask what the atoms are in a world with four dimensions of space and there is no answer yet, because the question leaves out the law between charges. In ours that law isn’t a free choice. Gauss’s law, which a local, linear field theory of charge gives, spreads a point charge’s field over the sphere around it, whose area grows as rD−1r^{D-1}, so the potential falls as r−(D−2)r^{-(D-2)}. In three dimensions that is 1/r1/r. In four it is 1/r21/r^2, and with 1/r21/r^2 there are no atoms.

2.1 Gauss’s law leaves no atom. One electron in the pull V=−α/r2V = -\alpha/r^2 has the Hamiltonian H=−12∇2−α/r2H = -\tfrac12\nabla^2 - \alpha/r^2, and nothing in it is a length. Stretch a wavefunction, ψ(x)→ψ(λx)\psi(x) \to \psi(\lambda x), and both terms scale by λ2\lambda^2, so a bound level at E<0E < 0 would bring one at λ2E\lambda^2 E for every λ\lambda. Either nothing binds or the levels run down without end. Hardy’s inequality (Barbatis 2024) decides which: for α≤12\alpha \le \tfrac12 the kinetic energy always outweighs the pull and nothing binds, and for α>12\alpha > \tfrac12 the wavefunction changes sign infinitely often on the way in and the electron falls to the centre (Case 1950; Landau and Lifshitz 1977, sec. 35) (appendix F.1). Our one-electron solver shows both (figure 1a). For α=0.45\alpha = 0.45 it finds no level on any grid. For α=0.7\alpha = 0.7 the lowest level is −5.87 × 10³, −5.87 × 10⁵ and −5.87 × 10⁷ hartree as the grid’s inner end moves from 10−310^{-3} to 10−510^{-5} bohr: set by where the calculation stops, not by the electron. There is no Bohr radius, and classically no stable orbit either (Ehrenfest 1918).

2.2 The way out taken. Something has to give: the squeeze ℏ22mer2\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e r^2} that resists the pull, or the pull. An electron whose energy grows faster than p2p^2 binds under Gauss’s law, at the cost of Galilean relativity for all matter. A small fourth dimension gives back three-dimensional atoms. This universe keeps the electron and four large dimensions and changes the law: the potential between charges falls as 1/r1/r. The choice between the first route and this one is a judgement, stated as one (appendix F.2): the electron route keeps our Faraday cages, the law route keeps our mechanics.

2.3 Only 1/r keeps a shell together. Any pull −1/rη-1/r^\eta with 0<η<20 < \eta < 2 binds and has a length of its own. What singles out η=1\eta = 1 is a symmetry. Under 1/r1/r every bound orbit is a closed ellipse (Santos, Soares, and Tort 2007), so the Runge–Lenz vector Ai=∑jLijpj−Zxi/rA_i = \sum_j L_{ij}\,p_j - Z x_i/r is conserved, and in quantum mechanics the rotations SO(DD) together with 𝐀\mathbf A generate SO(D+1D + 1) (Fock 1935; Alliluev 1958; Bander and Itzykson 1966). A level of hydrogen is then the set of degree-(n−1)(n - 1) harmonics on a sphere one dimension up, so every ll below nn shares the energy, in four dimensions −Z22(n+12)2-\frac{Z^2}{2(n + \frac12)^2}. Three things should be kept apart here. That each ll has (l+1)2(l + 1)^2 orbitals is the four-dimensional rotations’ doing, in any central potential. That every ll below nn shares one energy is 1/r1/r’s, through SO(5); together they make hydrogen’s shells of 2, 10, 28 and 60 electrons, each a group of levels with a gap above it. The table’s rows are a third thing, many electrons filling subshells in an order only a calculation finds (section 4.1). Without the symmetry nothing holds the ll together. On the same solver 2p sits −6.2 % from 2s at η=0.8\eta = 0.8, 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ % at η=1\eta = 1, +15.4 % at 1.2 and +64.0 % at 1.5, as a fraction of the shell’s binding (figure 1b). Rows would still form under another exponent, but nothing guarantees they would be the same, and the generalized Madelung rule, which starts from hydrogen’s degeneracy, wouldn’t apply (appendix F.3).

2.4 What 1/r costs: the shell theorem. In four dimensions ∇2(1/r)=−1/r3\nabla^2(1/r) = -1/r^3 away from the charge, so there is no Gauss’s law for it: the flux through a sphere round a charge grows with the sphere (appendix F.4). A thin spherical shell of charge with radius ss, felt at radius rr, gives κ(t)/max(r,s)\kappa(t)/\max(r, s), t=min(r,s)/max(r,s)t = \min(r, s)/\max(r, s), with κ\kappa the law’s 1/|x−y|1/|x - y| averaged over the shell. In ours κ=1\kappa = 1, the shell theorem, because 1/r1/r is harmonic and a harmonic function averages over a sphere to its value at the centre; in flat space of any dimension the only potentials with one are Gauss’s, plus at most r2r^2 and a constant (Tse, Markowich, and Phan 2026; Landkof 1972). Here the Laplacian is negative, so κ<1\kappa < 1: it falls from 1 to κ(1)=83π\kappa(1) = \tfrac{8}{3\pi} = 0.848826 at the shell (appendix A, figure A1). Three things follow. An inner cloud screens the nucleus from an outer electron only partly. A hollow conductor’s centre sits at 3π/83\pi/8 = 1.178097 of its wall’s potential, so there is no Faraday cage. And a neutral atom is felt from outside, as −Q8R3-\tfrac{Q}{8R^3} at a distance RR, with Q=∑⟨r2⟩Q = \sum\langle r^2\rangle over its electrons (section 7). Everything distinctive in Part III comes from here.

2.5 Where 1/r comes from, and what is declared. A potential 1/rη1/r^\eta in DD dimensions is the Fourier transform of 1/kD−η1/k^{D-\eta}, so the pinned law needs 1/k31/k^3 where Maxwell’s statics give 1/k21/k^2. No local field equation gives it. Of the five routes we examined (appendix F.5), the one that survives is a vacuum whose permittivity grows with wavenumber, ε0ℓk\varepsilon_0\ell k with ℓ\ell a length (Tarasov and Trujillo 2013). With the permeability μ0/(ℓk)\mu_0/(\ell k), the fields 𝐃/ε0\mathbf D/\varepsilon_0 and μ0𝐇\mu_0\mathbf H obey four-dimensional Maxwell’s equations exactly, and what pushes a charge is that light seen through a lens, 𝐄=(𝐃/ε0)/(ℓk)\mathbf E = (\mathbf D/\varepsilon_0)/(\ell k); we call it the lens vacuum. Its price is relativity, for the force between charges only: the static 1/r1/r acts across space at once. This paper uses only its statics, which are all an atom’s ground state needs. The free electron keeps its own relativity, which the lens vacuum gives up only for the force between charges, and that fixes how many electrons an orbital holds. In 4 + 1 dimensions the smallest Dirac electron has four components, the product of the four spatial Dirac matrices serves as time’s, and a slow electron keeps the two components of one chirality, which turn with one half of a four-dimensional rotation only: two spin states, two to an orbital (appendix F.6). A four-state electron is possible in principle and would double every shell. The same relativity of the free electron gives Pauli’s exclusion. The half of the spin-statistics theorem that makes half-integer spin a fermion needs only a Dirac equation and an energy bounded below, not the locality the force here lacks: electrons that could share a state would have no ground state, the pinned 1/r1/r between them notwithstanding (appendix F.6). What stays declared is that the electron is that smallest spinor. Nuclei are point charges stable to Z=140Z = 140, and the fine-structure constant is small, so atoms are solved without relativity. Single atoms don’t collapse (their energies follow the Thomas–Fermi Z5/2Z^{5/2} (Kventsel and Katriel 1981), fitted exponent 2.527), but the stability of matter in bulk, whose proofs in ours lean on the shell theorem, is open (appendix F.7).
