Part I · The law (draft)

2 · Why a law has to be chosen

Ask what the atoms are in a world with four dimensions of space and there is no answer yet, because the question leaves out the law between charges. In our world that law isn’t a free choice. Gauss’s law, which is what a local, linear field theory of charge gives, says the field of a point charge spreads over the sphere around it. The sphere’s area grows as rD−1r^{D-1}, so the field falls as r−(D−1)r^{-(D-1)} and the potential as r−(D−2)r^{-(D-2)}. In three dimensions that is 1/r1/r. In four it is 1/r21/r^2, and with 1/r21/r^2 there are no atoms.

2.1 Gauss’s law leaves no atom. One electron in the pull V=−α/r2V = -\alpha/r^2 has the Hamiltonian H=−12∇2−α/r2H = -\tfrac12\nabla^2 - \alpha/r^2, and nothing in it is a length. Stretch a wavefunction, ψ(x)→ψ(λx)\psi(x) \to \psi(\lambda x), and both terms scale by λ2\lambda^2. So if there were a bound state of energy E<0E < 0 there would be one at λ2E\lambda^2 E for every λ\lambda: a lowest level, and with it a size for the atom, is impossible. Either nothing binds or the levels run down without end. Which of the two happens is decided at the origin. Close in, an s wavefunction goes as rσr^\sigma with

σ(σ+D−2)+2α=0,σ=−1±1−2α(D=4).\sigma(\sigma + D - 2) + 2\alpha = 0, \qquad \sigma = -1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 2\alpha} \quad (D = 4).

For α≤12\alpha \le \tfrac12 the roots are real, and Hardy’s inequality (Barbatis 2024)

∫|∇ψ|2≥(D−22)2∫|ψ|2r2=∫|ψ|2r2(D=4)\int |\nabla\psi|^2 \;\ge\; \Big(\frac{D-2}{2}\Big)^2 \int \frac{|\psi|^2}{r^2} \;=\; \int \frac{|\psi|^2}{r^2} \quad (D = 4)

says the kinetic energy 12∫|∇ψ|2\tfrac12\int|\nabla\psi|^2 always outweighs the pull: H≥0H \ge 0, and nothing binds. For α>12\alpha > \tfrac12 the roots are complex and the wavefunction near the origin is r−1cos(2α−1lnr+δ)r^{-1}\cos\!\big(\sqrt{2\alpha - 1}\,\ln r + \delta\big). It changes sign infinitely often on the way in, and every node below a level means another level beneath it, so there is no lowest one: the electron falls to the centre (Case 1950; Landau and Lifshitz 1977, sec. 35).

A calculation shows both. Our one-electron solver (laws.js, appendix D) integrates the radial equation of section 5 by Numerov’s method on a logarithmic grid that stops at an inner radius r0r_0, and finds each level by bisecting on the number of nodes. For α=0.45\alpha = 0.45 it finds no bound level on any grid. For α=0.7\alpha = 0.7 the lowest level is −5.87 × 10³, −5.87 × 10⁵ and −5.87 × 10⁷ hartree with r0=10−3r_0 = 10^{-3}, 10−410^{-4} and 10−510^{-5} bohr. It grows as 1/r021/r_0^2 (εr02\varepsilon r_0^2 = −0.00587, −0.00587), set by where the calculation stops rather than by the electron (figure 1a). Either way there is no Bohr radius, no size an atom could settle at. Ehrenfest made the classical version of this argument (Ehrenfest 1918): under Gauss’s law in four dimensions no planetary orbit is stable either.

2.2 The ways out, and what each costs. Something has to give. The squeeze that resists the pull costs ℏ2/2mer2\hbar^2/2m_e r^2, because the electron’s energy grows as the square of its momentum pp. The pull goes as 1/r21/r^2 because of Gauss. Change either and a length comes back. One way is to change the electron: an energy growing as p3p^3 or p4p^4 outgrows a 1/r21/r^2 pull close in, so atoms bind at every strength. It keeps Gauss’s law, and with it the shell theorem and the Faraday cage. But it gives up Galilean relativity for all matter: a moving laboratory and a still one see the same physics only if energy is quadratic in momentum. Another is a small fourth dimension, the way extra dimensions could hide in ours. But atoms larger than it are three-dimensional atoms and smaller ones fall again, so it doesn’t give four-dimensional chemistry. The third is to change the law: keep the electron, keep four large dimensions, and make the potential between charges fall as 1/r1/r. This universe takes the third, and the rest of part I says why 1/r1/r and what it costs. The choice between the first and the third is a judgement, stated as one: the electron route keeps our Faraday cages, the law route keeps our mechanics.

3 · Why 1/r, exactly

3.1 Only 1/r keeps a shell together. Any pull V=−1/rηV = -1/r^\eta with 0<η<20 < \eta < 2 binds, and has a length of its own. The squeeze ℏ2/2mer2\hbar^2/2m_e r^2 beats the pull close in, the pull beats it far out, and they balance at a size that scales as (ℏ2/me)1/(2−η)(\hbar^2/m_e)^{1/(2-\eta)}. So η=1.5\eta = 1.5 would give atoms with a size, and so would η=0.8\eta = 0.8. What singles out η=1\eta = 1 is a symmetry that no other exponent has.

Classically, under 1/r1/r every bound orbit is a closed ellipse. By Bertrand’s theorem only 1/r1/r and r2r^2 do that, and r2r^2 confines rather than falling off. An orbit lies in a plane in any number of dimensions, so this holds in four. A closed ellipse keeps a fixed direction, its long axis. So the motion conserves a vector besides the angular momentum, the Runge–Lenz vector Ai=∑jLijpj−Zxi/rA_i = \sum_j L_{ij}\,p_j - Z x_i/r, where Lij=xipj−xjpiL_{ij} = x_i p_j - x_j p_i is the angular momentum in the plane ijij. Under any other exponent the ellipse precesses, its axis turns, and 𝐀\mathbf A isn’t conserved.

In quantum mechanics the rotations of DD-dimensional space, SO(DD), together with the DD components of 𝐀\mathbf A generate the rotations of a space with one dimension more, SO(D+1D + 1). Fock found this by carrying momentum space onto a sphere one dimension up (Fock 1935), and Bander and Itzykson worked out the group theory (Bander and Itzykson 1966). Why it happens only for 1/r1/r can be put in one line. The Schrödinger equation in momentum space contains the potential’s Fourier transform. For 1/rη1/r^\eta in DD dimensions that is 1/kD−η1/k^{D-\eta}, which for η=1\eta = 1 is 1/kD−11/k^{D-1}. Carried onto the sphere, 1/kD−11/k^{D-1} becomes, up to factors the projection itself supplies, Gauss’s potential of a point charge in D+1D + 1 dimensions, and the natural functions of that are the sphere’s own harmonics. For any other η\eta the transform carries over to something else, and the larger symmetry is lost.

The symmetry does the work. States that the larger rotations carry into one another must have the same energy. A level of hydrogen is the set of degree-(n−1)(n - 1) harmonics on that sphere, and the ordinary rotations split this set into l=0,1,…,n−1l = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1. So every ll below nn shares the energy, which in four dimensions is −Z2/2(n+12)2-Z^2/2(n + \tfrac12)^2. With (l+1)2(l + 1)^2 orbitals to each ll, shell nn holds ∑l<n2(l+1)2\sum_{l<n} 2(l + 1)^2 electrons: 2, 10, 28, 60. That degeneracy is what makes a shell, a group of levels with a gap above it.

Without the symmetry nothing holds the ll together. On the same solver, 2p sits −6.2 % from 2s at η=0.8\eta = 0.8, 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ % at η=1\eta = 1, +15.4 % at η=1.2\eta = 1.2 and +64.0 % at η=1.5\eta = 1.5 (as a fraction of the shell’s binding; 3d against 3s goes the same way, +78.3 % at η=1.5\eta = 1.5; figure 1b). Below 1 the electron with more angular momentum is the more bound, above it the less. So under η=1.5\eta = 1.5 a one-electron atom’s 2p is bound little more than a third as hard as its 2s, and each ll is a level of its own. A table would still have rows, since many electrons split the ll in ours too. But the rows would be set by how the exponent treats electrons that come near the nucleus, and would have to be found again for every η\eta. The generalized Madelung rule, which starts from hydrogen’s degeneracy, wouldn’t apply. Under 1/r1/r the rows come from counting harmonics, and that is what lets part III tell the geometry’s share of the table from the law’s.

3.2 The convention it agrees with. The literature on hydrogen in DD dimensions (Nieto 1979; Al-Jaber 1998) and on dimensional scaling (Herschbach, Avery, and Goscinski 1993) takes 1/r1/r in every DD, mostly as a convention that keeps the problem solvable. Négadi and Kibler’s generalized Madelung rule (1996), which gives the four-dimensional rows when it is applied to D=4D = 4, builds on the same DD-dimensional hydrogen atom. Section 3.1 is why that convention is more than one: it is the only power law under which the question “what are the shells?” has the answer the rule needs.

4 · What 1/r costs in four dimensions

4.1 It isn’t harmonic. In three dimensions ∇2(1/r)=0\nabla^2(1/r) = 0 away from the charge, and that one fact is Gauss’s law. In DD dimensions ∇2r−η=η(η+2−D)r−η−2\nabla^2 r^{-\eta} = \eta(\eta + 2 - D)\,r^{-\eta-2}, which vanishes only for Gauss’s own η=D−2\eta = D - 2. In four,

∇21r=−1r3(r≠0),\nabla^2 \frac{1}{r} = -\frac{1}{r^3} \qquad (r \ne 0),

which the solver’s finite differences reproduce (−1.00000 at r=1r = 1, −0.12500 at r=2r = 2). By the divergence theorem the flux of the field through a sphere round a charge is the integral of −∇2(1/r)-\nabla^2(1/r) over the ball inside it. The integrand is 1/r31/r^3 and the ball’s volume element 2π2r3dr2\pi^2 r^3\,dr, so the flux grows linearly with the sphere’s radius, as if empty space were charged with a density ∝1/r3\propto 1/r^3. There is no Gauss’s law for it.

4.2 No shell theorem, no Faraday cage. A thin spherical shell of charge with radius ss, felt at radius rr, gives κ(t)/max(r,s)\kappa(t)/\max(r, s), with t=min(r,s)/max(r,s)t = \min(r, s)/\max(r, s) and κ\kappa the law’s 1/|x−y|1/|x - y| averaged over the shell (section 5, appendix A). In three dimensions κ=1\kappa = 1, the shell theorem, and the reason is the mean-value property: a function with zero Laplacian averages, over any sphere, to its value at the centre, and 1/|x−y|1/|x - y| is such a function of yy. In four dimensions ∇2(1/r)<0\nabla^2(1/r) < 0, and a function whose Laplacian is negative averages to less than its value at the centre. So κ<1\kappa < 1: it falls from 1 at the centre to κ(1)=8/3π\kappa(1) = 8/3\pi = 0.848826 at the shell (figure A1). That has three consequences. A cloud of charge never acts as if it sat at its centre. A hollow conductor’s centre sits at 3π/83\pi/8 = 1.178097 of its wall’s potential, and a charge inside is pushed toward the wall. And a neutral atom is felt from outside, as −Q/8R3-Q/8R^3 at a distance RR, where Q=∑⟨r2⟩Q = \sum\langle r^2\rangle over its electrons (section 11). Everything distinctive in part III comes from here.

4.3 Which field theory gives 1/r. A potential is what a field theory’s static equation gives for a point charge, and the equation is simplest in Fourier space, where each pair of derivatives becomes a factor k2k^2 of the wavenumber kk. Maxwell’s statics give a point charge the potential φ∝1/k2\varphi \propto 1/k^2, and that transforms back to 1/rD−21/r^{D-2} in space, so 1/r21/r^2 in four dimensions. A potential 1/rη1/r^\eta in DD dimensions is the transform of 1/kD−η1/k^{D-\eta}, so the pinned 1/r1/r needs 1/k31/k^3. There are five ways to get it (the site’s force chapter, four.mino.mobi/atom/force.html, works through each). Four fail outright. The third survives with a flaw that can be removed, and it is the one this paper takes.

  1. A local, relativistic field: impossible. A local field equation is a polynomial in k2k^2, a whole number of pairs of derivatives, and k3=(k2)3/2k^3 = (k^2)^{3/2} isn’t one.
  2. Relativistic but non-local: the potential obeys (k2−ω2/c2)3/2φ∝ρ(k^2 - \omega^2/c^2)^{3/2}\,\varphi \propto \rho, Maxwell’s wave operator raised to the power 3/23/2, which in space and time is an integral over all of both. The field’s scaling dimension is then 1 in five-dimensional spacetime. Positive probabilities need at least 1.5 (the unitarity bound, where an ordinary free field sits), so the theory has states of negative probability.
  3. A vacuum whose permittivity grows with wavenumber, ε0ℓk\varepsilon_0\,\ell k with ℓ\ell a length. The static equation becomes ε0ℓk⋅k2φ=ρ\varepsilon_0\ell k \cdot k^2\varphi = \rho, which is 1/k31/k^3, the pinned 1/r1/r. With the permeability left at μ0\mu_0, light obeys k2=ℓkω2/c2k^2 = \ell k\,\omega^2/c^2, so ω=ck/ℓ\omega = c\sqrt{k/\ell}. It disperses like deep-water waves, and there is no speed of light. This is the route we take, and the flaw is removed below.
  4. A nonlinear vacuum, ∇⋅(|𝐄|β−2𝐄)=ρ\nabla\cdot(|\mathbf E|^{\beta-2}\mathbf E) = \rho. It keeps a Gauss’s law, for the flux of |𝐄|β−2𝐄|\mathbf E|^{\beta-2}\mathbf E instead of 𝐄\mathbf E. A point charge then has |𝐄|β−1rD−1|\mathbf E|^{\beta-1}r^{D-1} constant, and a 1/r1/r potential (|𝐄|∝1/r2|\mathbf E| \propto 1/r^2) needs β=(D+1)/2\beta = (D + 1)/2 = 2.5. But fields no longer add: the field of two charges isn’t the sum of each one’s.
  5. A small fourth dimension: 1/r1/r far away, but then atoms are three-dimensional (section 2.2).

Route 1 doesn’t exist, route 2 has states of negative probability, route 4 gives up fields that add, and route 5 gives back three-dimensional atoms. That leaves route 3. The permittivity fixes the statics, and the statics are all that the pinned law asks of it. The permeability is still free, and it decides only how fields move. Left at μ0\mu_0 it gives the dispersive light above. We choose μ0/ℓk\mu_0/\ell k, and call the result the lens vacuum. The product of permittivity and permeability is then ε0μ0\varepsilon_0\mu_0 at every wavenumber, so light keeps the one speed cc. The fields 𝐃/ε0\mathbf D/\varepsilon_0 and μ0𝐇\mu_0\mathbf H obey Maxwell’s equations of empty four-dimensional space exactly, so light is ordinary four-dimensional light at every wavelength, and the energy is never negative. What pushes a charge is 𝐄=(𝐃/ε0)/ℓk\mathbf E = (\mathbf D/\varepsilon_0)/\ell k, that light seen through a lens. Dividing by kk in Fourier space is, in space, spreading over a kernel 1/r31/r^3 (the transform of 1/k1/k in four dimensions), so 𝐄\mathbf E at a point is a blur of 𝐃\mathbf D over all of space. Its statics are 1/ℓk31/\ell k^3, the pinned 1/r1/r. The price is relativity, for the force between charges only. The blur acts across all of space at once, so there is an absolute “now”, and a charge feels another’s change instantly, a little, everywhere.

This paper uses only route 3’s statics: charges at rest and the 1/r1/r between them, which is all an atom’s ground state needs. Every choice of permeability gives the same statics, so nothing here depends on that choice, on ℓ\ell, on how the lens vacuum’s fields move and radiate, or on the absolute now. Those dynamics, of the same lens vacuum, are the subject of a later paper.

4.4 What is declared with it. Electrons are spin-½ fermions, two to an orbital. That is stated, not derived: in our world it follows from relativity and spin together, and this universe has given up relativity for the force. Nuclei are point charges +Z+Z, stable for every ZZ to 140 (declared: nuclear physics in four dimensions is another paper). The fine-structure constant is small, so atoms are solved without relativity (section 12). No other long-range field acts on charges.

4.5 Is matter stable? In ours it is a theorem that NN atoms have an energy proportional to NN (Dyson and Lenard 1967; Lieb and Thirring 1975), and the proofs lean on the shell theorem, which fails here. So we give evidence, not a proof. Atoms don’t collapse. Thomas–Fermi scaling gives total energies growing as Z3−2/DZ^{3-2/D}, Z7/3Z^{7/3} in ours and Z5/2Z^{5/2} in four dimensions (Kventsel and Katriel 1981), and the solved table follows it, with a fitted exponent of 2.527 over ZZ = 10–140 (ours: 2.387 over 10–36; both a little above the asymptotic value, as finite-ZZ corrections make them). Many atoms don’t run away either. Every force between neutral atoms here (the permanent −3QAQB/64R5-3Q_AQ_B/64R^5 of section 11, dispersion’s R−6R^{-6}) falls faster than R−4R^{-4}, so summed over four-dimensional space each atom’s share is finite. This is argued, not proved, and the paper says so.
