Stars that age: our Sun first

Section 3 built stars at zero age only. It stood in for their lives with the time a zero-age centre, held at its temperature and density, takes to burn half its hydrogen, and calibrated that clock on our Sun by a declared factor. This section ages stars instead: hydrogen burnt shell by shell, the stirred regions mixed, the star re-solved at every step. Before four-dimensional stars are aged that way, the machinery is run on our own Sun and checked against the standard solar models, in four layers so that a mismatch can be placed: the burning law alone, the structure alone, the calibration, and the history.

4.1 Results on our Sun

The burning law is right. At Model S’s own temperatures, densities and abundances (Christensen-Dalsgaard et al. 1996), shell by shell out to half the Sun’s mass, our energy generation is 0.96–1.04 times Model S’s. Without screening it would be 0.83–0.92.

The structure carries the opacity’s error. Solved on Model S’s composition profile, with our opacity, gas and burning, the star has LL = 0.91 L☉ and a centre at 15.36 MK and 134 g/cm³. Model S has 15.67 MK and 154 g/cm³. Half its light comes from inside qq = 0.106 of the mass, against Model S’s 0.089. So with the composition given, our Sun is 13% less dense at the centre and its burning is spread a little wider. That is our opacity, a textbook Kramers law with electron scattering, 1.3–1.5 times Model S’s (OPAL) through the core. Four-dimensional stars do not use it: they have their own opacity, computed from the lens vacuum.

Calibrated as standard models are, the Sun ages correctly. A standard solar model sets its initial helium so the luminosity is L☉ at 4.57 Gyr. Done the same way here, the initial hydrogen is X0X_0 = 0.687; Bahcall, Pinsonneault & Basu’s model has 0.708 (Bahcall, Pinsonneault, and Basu 2001). At 4.57 Gyr the centre is at 15.73 MK (theirs 15.70, Model S 15.67). The luminosity history L(t)/L(4.57Gyr)L(t)/L(4.57\,\mathrm{Gyr}) follows theirs to within 3.6% from 0.2 to 4.6 Gyr. After that ours brightens faster, to 1.53 at 8 Gyr against 1.36, 12.0% apart. The centre’s hydrogen runs out (XcX_c = 10⁻³) at 9.0 Gyr. Pols’s 1 M☉ model of near-solar composition does it after 9 (Pols n.d., sec. 10.2.2).

What does not match, and why. The present centre is 18% less dense than the standard models’ (126 against 153 and 154 g/cm³). That is the structure’s own shortfall above, carried along. Its hydrogen is XcX_c = 0.365 against 0.340 (BP2000), 0.337 (Model S) and 0.347 (B16 (Vinyoles et al. 2017)). It stays higher later too: 0.206 at 6.5 Gyr against 0.145, and XcX_c = 0.008 at 8.6 Gyr against their 8. All three reference models let helium settle into the core, which lowers its hydrogen; ours have no diffusion. How much of the difference that accounts for would need a reference model without it, which these papers do not tabulate.

Section 3’s clock, tested. Aged, our Sun’s centre halves its hydrogen in 4.84 Gyr. Held at the temperature and density of the settled zero-age star (0.1 Gyr, 13.7 MK), fresh gas would take 9.6 Gyr, a factor of 1.99. Section 3 took 2.41, from a zero-age Sun built under a simpler burning law. The factor depends on what counts as zero age. The run’s very first star, before its helium-3 and carbon-12 have come into balance, sits at 14.7 MK and would take 5.8 Gyr, a factor of only 1.20. Ageing the four-dimensional analogues themselves removes the convention, and they have now been aged on this machinery, with the charge law’s plasma (screening under the pinned 1/r1/r and the lens vacuum’s cutoff on light). Of the 29 Sun analogues section 3 found, 26 have been aged so far, and every one of them burns half its central hydrogen, in 10.6–177 Gyr: 2.3–39 times the 4.6 Gyr section 3 gave them. The other three (G = 10−11 at s = 1.5152; G = 10−17 at s = 1.75; G = 10−20 at s = 1.75) never started on the earlier solver and are being aged now. The slowest sit at the strength nearest ours, s = 1.5152: 43, 125, 177 Gyr from G = 10⁻¹⁴ to 10⁻²⁰. One run stops soon after halving: at G = 10−14, s = 1.5152, half the hydrogen is gone at 43.1 Gyr and the solver finds no star past 43.3. Over its last 1 Gyr the stirred core shrank from 0.14 to 0.05 of the mass, while the centre grew 26% denser and 3% cooler and the light rose 17%. The solver keeps every shell in thermal balance, with no energy drawn from contraction, so it cannot follow a core changing that fast. Where it fails does not depend on where the shooting meets.

The opacity, measured and then computed. Everything above carries the textbook opacity: electron scattering plus Kramers’s law. Along Model S’s interior that law is 0.16–1.35 times OPAL’s tables (Iglesias and Rogers 1996), and it is why the centre above is too thin. With OPAL’s tables in the interior (Asplund et al.’s mixture (Asplund et al. 2009), with Ferguson et al.’s below 5600 K (Ferguson et al. 2005), as MESA distributes them (Paxton et al. 2010)), the star’s outer layers integrated as an envelope on the surface model’s gas with mixing length α, and the Sun calibrated on two numbers at MIST’s ZZ = 0.0143 (X0X_0 to L☉ and α to R☉ at 4.57 Gyr), X0X_0 = 0.7068 and α = 1.61. At 4.57 Gyr the centre is then 15.39 MK and 149 g/cm³, against BP2000’s 15.70 and 153, and XcX_c = 0.360. Against MIST’s published tracks (Choi et al. 2016; Dotter 2016), at 1–3 M☉ and half the centre’s hydrogen, LL is 0.90–0.94 of MIST’s, RR 0.90–0.99 and TeffT_\mathrm{eff} 0.99–1.03. On the textbook opacity RR was 0.63–0.94 and TeffT_\mathrm{eff} up to 1.34. Lives to XcX_c = 10⁻³ come to 0.83–0.88 of MIST’s at 1.6–3 M☉, where MIST’s cores overshoot into fresh fuel, and 1.14 at 1 M☉, where its helium settles inward. Neither is in our stars.

OPAL is measured input, not the book’s own physics. The book’s own opacity is the surface model’s, carried inward. Free–free takes a Born–Elwert Gaunt factor. The metals’ bound–free and free–free come from every ion of fifteen elements, each solved by the book’s atom and ionized by Saha with Debye lowering. Along Model S’s interior, metal-free gas comes to 1.02–1.09 of OPAL’s metal-free tables, against 1.20–1.49 with the Gaunt factor set to 1. The ions supply 45%–61% of what OPAL’s metals add, and the whole comes to 0.55–0.87 of OPAL’s. What is left is the lines, the bound–bound transitions, which the book does not have yet.

Without metals. The metals and their lines drop out of a metal-free star. Only hydrogen’s and helium’s lines remain missing from the book, and they matter only in the outer envelope. So there the book’s own opacity can be tried nearly whole, against MIST’s metal-poor tracks ([Fe/H] = −4) at the same hydrogen. At half its central hydrogen, a 1 M☉ star on OPAL’s metal-free tables has 0.83 of MIST’s LL and 0.97 of its TeffT_\mathrm{eff}, and lives 1.15 as long. On the book’s own opacity it has 0.78, 0.96 and 1.24. At 0.8 M☉ the two give LL 0.83 and 0.79; at 1.6 M☉ 0.87 and 0.80, with lives 1.11 and 1.23. So the book’s own opacity costs a few per cent of the light against OPAL’s. Both fall short of MIST’s luminosity by more than at the Sun’s metals, on the same tables, so that shortfall is not the opacity. Where it comes from is open. MIST’s helium settling matters more in a metal-poor star, whose stirred envelope is thin. Its equation of state is another difference.

4.2 Methods

4.2.1 The star

The star is section 3’s solver: hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium, Fermi–Dirac electrons, convection at the adiabatic gradient, our opacity. Its composition may now vary with mass. The energy comes from burning only. The slow contraction’s share, εgrav\varepsilon_\mathrm{grav}, is left out; it is small on the main sequence. The composition lives on a fixed grid in q=m/Mq = m/M, 401 points spaced as q=(j/400)1.5q = (j/400)^{1.5}, finer at the centre.

With OPAL’s opacity, the tables are read in logT\log T and logR\log R (R=ρ/T63R = \rho/T_6^3) by cubic interpolation, linearly in XX and ZZ, the low- and high-temperature tables blended over logT\log T = 3.80–3.88 as MESA does. The envelope is the surface model’s gas from the photosphere inward. It starts with a grey atmosphere from τ = 10⁻⁴ to ⅔, then integrates inward in lnP\ln P at fixed LL to 3 × 10⁵ K, where the interior takes over. Its convection is mixing length (Böhm-Vitense’s cubic, in Kippenhahn & Weigert’s form), with ℓ=αHP\ell = \alpha H_P. Its last step is cut to end exactly at 3 × 10⁵ K, so the fit point moves smoothly with RR and LL. Every star is settled for 10⁶ yr before its clock starts, so that its carbon-12 and helium-3 come into balance, as MIST’s zero age is taken after them.

4.2.2 The burning

Each shell carries its own protons, helium-3, helium-4 and carbon, nitrogen and oxygen isotopes. It burns on every REACLIB reaction of the pp chains and the CN and NO cycles (Cyburt et al. 2010), with weak screening by Debye–Hückel (Pols n.d., eq. 6.31) and each weak reaction’s neutrino share taken off (pp 0.265, ⁷Be 0.814, ⁸B 6.71 MeV (Pols n.d., sec. 6.4.1)). Short-lived species (deuterium, beryllium-7, the CNO cycle’s β-unstable nuclei) sit at their steady state in the structure. Helium-3 is evolved, because outside the Sun’s centre it never reaches its steady state. Each shell burns implicitly (backward Euler with Newton) at its own temperature and density. In the structure each shell’s energy is a local power law in ρ\rho and TT, refitted at each solution until the luminosity settles to 10⁻⁴.

4.2.3 Stirring and stepping

A region the solver stirs burns as one zone, on the mass-weighted mean of its shells’ rate constants. Each flux is linear in its rate constant, so that is exact while the zone stays mixed. Burning each shell on its own over a step starves a convective core’s centre of the fuel the stirring brings in: in its first few tens of millions of years, our Sun’s carbon-12 burning stirs up to a tenth of its mass. Each step is second-order: burn half a step at the present star’s conditions, re-solve, then burn the whole step at that half-way star’s conditions. A step is accepted only if what the shells burnt over it matches what the half-way star radiated to 0.3%. Otherwise it is halved. Over the Sun’s life the ratio stayed within 0.997–1.003. To 4.57 Gyr that took 203 models. Tightening the check to 0.1% (662 models) moves LL by 0.1% and XcX_c by 0.0010.

4.2.4 The references

Bahcall, Pinsonneault & Basu’s standard model (BP2000) gives LL, RR and TeffT_\mathrm{eff} against age in its tables 1–3, the centre’s hydrogen in its §III.4 and the present centre in its table 5 (Bahcall, Pinsonneault, and Basu 2001). Model S is the present Sun, read from its authors’ model file (Christensen-Dalsgaard et al. 1996). Its abundances, ε and κ serve the first two checks. B16-GS98 gives the present centre’s helium and metals (Vinyoles et al. 2017, table 4). Every number used is kept in data/ssm-ref.json and data/modelS.json with its locator.
