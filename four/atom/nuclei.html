Nuclei

What it takes for 4D to have nuclei at all: a stronger strong force, helium-4 three times as tightly bound as ours, nuclear matter held by the crowd, no gap at mass 8, and a periodic table that could run to element 230. Which element is commonest stays open.

What was declared, and what it takes

The book began by declaring nuclei: heavy point charges, stable up to element 78 (chapter 2). That was enough for atoms, which see a nucleus only as its charge. It isn't enough for stars, which make nuclei, or for the question every chapter since has run into: which elements are common? Our world's answer, iron and oxygen and the rest, comes from nuclear physics: which nuclei hold together, and which ones stars can build. Here that physics is worked out for 4D, with every method first checked on our own nuclei.

The one thing 4D changes is the one the stars chapter met: a head-on meeting carries a barrier, 3/4 · ħ²/2μr², because in four dimensions there's more room to miss. Inside a nucleus, where nucleons sit a femtometre or two apart, it's large. The force between nucleons is taken pinned, as the force between charges is: the same function of distance in both worlds. Two of them are used, of quite different shapes, to show the conclusions don't hang on one: Malfliet and Tjon's (Yukawa terms, a repulsive core that grows without limit) and the Minnesota force (soft Gaussians). Both are fitted to how our nucleons scatter, and both give our deuteron (2.2 MeV).

The strong force has to be stronger

computed With our force, 4D's deuteron isn't bound: the barrier wins. A star can't start burning hydrogen without one (its first step makes a deuteron), and the universe's first minutes would make nothing at all. Scale the force's attraction by a factor s and ask where things change. Our world sits in a window: the deuteron binds, and the diproton (two protons) doesn't, which would let stars burn explosively fast. 4D has the same window, higher up:

Helium-4

computed Four nucleons (two protons, two neutrons, spins paired) fill the lowest shell in any dimension, so helium-4 needs no sign changes in its wavefunction, and diffusion Monte Carlo solves it exactly for a given force. The checks: our helium-4, 31.1 MeV under Malfliet–Tjon (the published value for it is 31.36) and 28.5 under Minnesota (measured: 28.30). Held by the crowd

A nucleus far bigger than helium is mostly inside, where each nucleon is surrounded. Its binding per nucleon tends to that of nuclear matter, an endless uniform sea of nucleons, which can be computed: Brueckner's method, which follows each pair through all its collisions inside the sea, written here for any dimension. computed Its two-nucleon machinery reproduces the deuteron exactly in both worlds. On our world it shows the known flaw of such forces: nuclear matter binds harder the denser it gets, never settling. What settles it in ours is partly three nucleons together. So a three-nucleon repulsion is added, the same function of distance in both worlds, with one strength fitted to our nuclear matter's binding (16 MeV per nucleon). That puts our saturation at 0.162 and 0.189 nucleons per fm³ (measured 0.16), and moves our helium-4 by about a MeV. a choice Its range (0.7 to 1.4 fm) isn't fixed by our world, and sets how it carries into 4D: it's the uncertainty below.

It's the gases chapter's discovery again (chapter 14): in four dimensions more neighbours fit within reach of the force, so matter is held by the crowd. Helium-4 has only four nucleons, and can't use it. So big 4D nuclei are far more bound per nucleon than helium, by a wider margin than ours (16 against 7).

Past helium

That says the valley is deep. It doesn't say stars can reach it: the first steps past helium could be uphill. Ours are. Nothing of mass 5 or 8 holds together in our world, and our stars cross that gap only by the triple-alpha reaction, three helium nuclei meeting almost at once and caught by a lucky level in carbon-12.

tried, failed Monte Carlo past helium needs a guess at where the wavefunction changes sign, and with these forces the guess fails on our own nuclei: helium-6, beryllium-8 and carbon-12 come out at 22, 31 and 0 MeV against the measured 29, 56 and 92. Nothing 4D is claimed from it.

computed one calibration What works is to treat light nuclei as what they are, clusters of helium-4s. Two helium-4s meet through the nucleon force spread over both, and the Pauli principle keeps their relative motion out of the states that would crowd a fifth nucleon into the lowest shell (Saito's orthogonality; the count is the same in any dimension). One factor λ on that folding is set so that our beryllium-8 sits where it does, just unbound. It comes out 1.9 under either force: almost exactly 2, which is what the half of the force that exchanges nucleons should add. Uncertain by a large factor, but far past iron's 56 every way, and most of that range lies past where an atom can hold its electrons (below).

How far a table can go

computed A heavy nucleus pulls its innermost electron so hard that, relativistically, the electron's level sinks into the sea of negative energies: past that charge, an atom can't be neutral. Dirac's equation in four dimensions has our radial form with the spin–orbit number moved by the extra direction, and its non-relativistic limit is 4D hydrogen (chapter 6). Round a point nucleus the innermost level fails at Zα = 3/2, element 205 (ours: Zα = 1, element 137). With a nucleus of its real size, solved: ours dives near element 170 (the literature: 170–173), 4D's near element 232. So a 4D table could run to about 232, and the 78 solved here are not its end: 4D's nuclei, held by the crowd, hold together far past that, and it's the electrons that stop the table.

What's computed and what's open

Computed and checked on ours: the strength window (both forces), helium-4 (exact, both forces), nuclear matter (the deuteron exactly; ours saturating with one fitted strength), helium-4 clusters (one factor calibrated on beryllium-8, near its natural value), Dirac's limit. Chosen: the force's strength (the window's requirement) and the three-nucleon force's range (the uncertainty band). Rough: where binding peaks. open Which element is commonest, this world's iron. Stars can burn past helium; how far they get before the electric barrier between ever-larger nuclei stops them sets it, and that needs the stars chapter's burning, stage by stage, with these nuclei. Also open: how neutron-rich 4D nuclei can be (4D neutron matter sits at the edge of binding, so the price of extra neutrons is lower than ours), and nuclei past helium solved properly, not as clusters.
