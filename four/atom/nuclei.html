ATOM⁴ · chapter 15

Nuclei

What it takes for 4D to have nuclei at all: a stronger strong force, helium-4 three times as tightly bound as ours, nuclear matter held by the crowd, no gap at mass 8 or anywhere up the helium-4 ladder, and a periodic table that could run to element 230. Which element is commonest now rests on how hot stars get.

What was declared, and what it takes

The book began by declaring nuclei: heavy point charges, stable up to element 78 (chapter 2). That was enough for atoms, which see a nucleus only as its charge. It isn't enough for stars, which make nuclei, or for the question every chapter since has run into: which elements are common? Our world's answer, iron and oxygen and the rest, comes from nuclear physics: which nuclei hold together, and which ones stars can build. Here that physics is worked out for 4D, with every method first checked on our own nuclei.

The one thing 4D changes is the one the stars chapter met: a head-on meeting carries a barrier, 3/4 · ħ²/2μr², because in four dimensions there's more room to miss. Inside a nucleus, where nucleons sit a femtometre or two apart, it's large. The force between nucleons is taken pinned, as the force between charges is: the same function of distance in both worlds. Two of them are used, of quite different shapes, to show the conclusions don't hang on one: Malfliet and Tjon's (Yukawa terms, a repulsive core that grows without limit) and the Minnesota force (soft Gaussians). Both are fitted to how our nucleons scatter, and both give our deuteron (2.2 MeV).

Held by the crowd

A nucleus far bigger than helium is mostly inside, where each nucleon is surrounded. Its binding per nucleon tends to that of nuclear matter, an endless uniform sea of nucleons, which can be computed: Brueckner's method, which follows each pair through all its collisions inside the sea, written here for any dimension. computed Its two-nucleon machinery reproduces the deuteron exactly in both worlds. On our world it shows the known flaw of such forces: nuclear matter binds harder the denser it gets, never settling. What settles it in ours is partly three nucleons together. So a three-nucleon repulsion is added, the same function of distance in both worlds, with one strength fitted to our nuclear matter's binding (16 MeV per nucleon). Past helium

That says the valley is deep. It doesn't say stars can reach it: the first steps past helium could be uphill. Ours are. Nothing of mass 5 or 8 holds together in our world, and our stars cross that gap only by the triple-alpha reaction, three helium nuclei meeting almost at once and caught by a lucky level in carbon-12.

The ladder, helium-4 by helium-4

That's the crowd again, at the scale of a nucleus. It also says where the cluster picture ends. By the third to sixth helium-4 (counting beryllium-8's two), a capture releases more than a helium-4 is bound by, so the helium-4s can't stay whole inside it. Past carbon or oxygen, a 4D nucleus is a drop of nuclear matter, not a molecule of helium-4s, and the drop is what the crowd section computed. The two agree: at mass 28–32 the ladder's binding per nucleon is still climbing toward the trusted bulk value, below it in every case where both exist.

There is no energy gap anywhere on 4D's ladder. Ours has two, at mass 5 and 8, and stars cross them only by the triple-alpha's luck; 4D's has none to the top of what's computed, and the drop says none after. So energy doesn't stop 4D stars building. What decides how far they get is rates: the electric barrier, how readily each nucleus sheds its energy as light, the levels that make a reaction resonant, and which reactions win the race in the time a star has. Energetically open isn't the same as running.

The same, with the Pauli principle exact

The ladder's helium-4s are points with a force between them, and the Pauli principle enters only as states they're forbidden to use. The direct test of that is to build the nuclei from nucleons. computed In Brink's model each helium-4 is four nucleons sharing one Gaussian orbital, and a nucleus is one determinant of all of them, antisymmetrized, so the Pauli principle is exact: nothing removed, no factor fitted per nucleus. The first capture, and the light it needs

Two helium-4s that end bound have to get rid of the energy, and in a star that means light. The simplest light, a dipole photon, is closed to them: they're identical, so their centre of charge sits on their centre of mass and there's no dipole to radiate. Both are spinless, so meeting head-on and landing in beryllium-8's ground state would take a photon carrying no turning at all, which no photon can be. What's left is the next kind, a quadrupole photon. It can carry them from a head-on meeting into a spinning state of beryllium-8 (spin 2), or from a glancing meeting (a d-wave) into the ground state. Our own helium-4 captures onto carbon and oxygen are spinless meeting spinless too, and run partly on exactly this.

computed 4D's beryllium-8 has a bound spin-2 state in every case, 2.5 to 164 MeV below two free helium-4s, in the cluster model above with its forbidden d-state removed. It sits 5–9 MeV above the ground state, the ground state's turning partner. Ours has one too, 3.0 MeV above threshold and unbound, which both forces put 1.3–1.8 MeV too low, so read the shallowest 4D one as maybe a MeV less bound.

The first minutes

Before any star, the hot young universe cools through nuclear temperatures for a few minutes, and what it makes then is what the first stars start from. Ours made hydrogen and a quarter helium, and almost nothing else: mass 5 and mass 8 don't hold together, and by the time helium exists the universe is too cool and thin to go round them. 4D has neither gap. computed three choices open The standard first estimate, written for any dimension: photons and baryons in the hot bath, neutrons frozen out and decaying, the deuterium bottleneck (deuterons survive the photons only below a temperature set by their binding, Saha's equation), every neutron left then ending in helium-4, and then the captures computed above, protons onto helium-4 and helium-4 onto helium-4, as the universe cools, using helium up; mass 5's binding from the microscopic calculation above (the folded potential reset to it where it binds). Three things the book hasn't fixed enter as choices: the weak force, taken as ours (neutrons freeze out at 0.8 MeV and live 879 s); η, the baryons per photon (ours 6.1 × 10⁻¹⁰); and the clock, how fast the universe cools, which gravity's law would set. Expanding as ours does, with the rate following radiation's energy density (T⁵ in 4D), the time goes as T^(−5/2), and its scale t₁ (the age at 1 MeV, ours 1.32 s) is left free. The check, ours: the bottleneck breaks at 0.066 MeV and leaves 0.235 helium by mass (measured 0.245).

Where the deuteron binds as ours, 4D's first minutes stop at helium, as ours did. The bottleneck breaks at 0.057–0.076 MeV, much as ours does, every neutron left goes into helium-4, and then nothing more: mass 5 is unbound, so protons can't capture onto it, and two helium-4s meet too rarely in the thinning bath to make more than a trace of beryllium-8. How far stars build

A star's core runs only as hot as its mass lets it. As it burns out a fuel it contracts and heats, as the virial theorem has it, until its electrons are packed so tightly that their degeneracy holds it up; then it cools. So each core mass has a highest temperature, and a stage whose temperature is above it never lights. Above the Chandrasekhar mass degeneracy can't hold at any density, and the core heats until burning stops paying. computed rough The estimate, in any dimension: the core is the gas polytrope (stars chapter), its centre's pressure balanced by the ions as a hot gas and the electrons as they are at that temperature, relativistic and partly degenerate; its hottest point found over every radius. With masses in units of the Chandrasekhar mass, gravity's strength drops out, which matters here, since G is fixed only by the planets. The check, ours (cores of matter with two nucleons per electron; known values in brackets): hydrogen lights in stars from 0.08 of it (0.08 M☉ of 1.44: 0.056), helium in cores from 0.29 (a helium star lights from about 0.3 M☉: 0.22), carbon from 0.73 (1.06 M☉: 0.74). 4D stars build in steps by mass, as ours do, but with helium lit almost from the start. Hydrogen lights in stars from 0.15–0.16 of the Chandrasekhar mass, and helium in cores from 0.13–0.19: a core about as heavy as the lightest star that burns hydrogen at all, where ours needs one four times as heavy. 4D's helium needs only four to seven times hydrogen's temperature, ours about thirty (two helium-4s meeting, against our triple-alpha's three). So ours has a long band of stars, the red dwarfs, that burn hydrogen for ever and never helium; in 4D that band is narrow, perhaps empty: a star needs only to grow a helium core most of its own mass to light it. Then the ladder, a rung per step in mass: a core too light for carbon ends as beryllium-8, then carbon, oxygen, pxygen (element 10), and so on up, each rung needing a heavier core, until from element 20 on only cores at the Chandrasekhar mass or above take the next step. Those go further, because in 4D every capture keeps paying far past iron's charge: where they stop is where binding per nucleon peaks, which the liquid drop above puts past element 100 (rough), unless something else ends them first. open Which of these get back out into the gas that makes planets, and in what proportion: that's the mass a star of each kind sheds and how it ends, and how many stars of each mass form. Ours return their carbon and nitrogen mostly from the winds of middling stars and their oxygen to iron from the explosions of massive ones.

Hydrogen and helium together

How far the protons carry a nucleus comes down to one count: how many protons it catches while the gas around it burns its hydrogen. That is a ratio of two rates at one temperature, the capture's against hydrogen's own burning, so the density drops out, and no clock has to be borrowed from our stars. computed The check, ours: while the Sun's centre burns half its hydrogen, at 15.7 million K, a carbon-12 catches about 4,900 protons, a nitrogen-14 about 13 and an oxygen-16 about 0.25: carbon goes to nitrogen, which piles up, and oxygen is only partly touched, as in our Sun's core (the same rates as the textbook's, its formula giving nitrogen-14 some 3.5 × 10⁸ years there).

In 4D the same count turns up something bigger. Hydrogen burns as ours, by the weak force (proton + proton, our rate through 4D's barrier, the stated choice the stars chapter made). But in 4D two helium-4s fuse to beryllium-8 by light alone, since mass 8 is bound: an electromagnetic capture, against hydrogen's weak one. How far a table can go

computed A heavy nucleus pulls its innermost electron so hard that, relativistically, the electron's level sinks into the sea of negative energies: past that charge, an atom can't be neutral. Dirac's equation in four dimensions has our radial form with the spin–orbit number moved by the extra direction, and its non-relativistic limit is 4D hydrogen (chapter 6). Round a point nucleus the innermost level fails at Zα = 3/2, element 205 (ours: Zα = 1, element 137). With a nucleus of its real size, solved: ours dives near element 170 (the literature: 170–173), 4D's near element 232. So a 4D table could run to about 232, and the 78 solved here are not its end: 4D's nuclei, held by the crowd, hold together far past that, and it's the electrons that stop the table.

Computed and checked on ours: the strength window (both forces), helium-4 (exact, both forces), nuclear matter (the deuteron exactly; ours saturating with one fitted strength), helium-4 clusters (one factor calibrated on beryllium-8, near its natural value), the helium-4 ladder (two-body, every sign right on ours), Dirac's limit. Rough: where binding peaks, and each rung's burning temperature. open Which element is commonest, this world's iron. The ladder settles that the climb is energetically open, every capture downhill. The first capture is computed and runs, by quadrupole light, at the temperatures where 4D stars burn hydrogen, and how far each core mass can climb the ladder is estimated. Still needed: each rung's own capture rate past the first, and what stars of each mass give back to the gas (their winds, how they end, how many of each form), which is what sets the abundances planets are made from. The universe's first minutes are computed as a first estimate, with three choices left open (the weak force, η, the clock): they stop at helium as ours did, because mass 5 is unbound microscopically, except near the window's top. A spin–orbit force for mass 5, and the clock (gravity's law), are open. Which element 10 or 9 is commoner, and so which water fills the oceans, follows from the same burning. Element 10, pxygen, is on the ladder, five helium-4s; element 9 is off it, made only by side reactions, as our fluorine is, thousands of times rarer than oxygen. That leans toward Xater by analogy, not yet computed. Also open: how neutron-rich 4D nuclei can be (4D neutron matter sits at the edge of binding, so the price of extra neutrons is lower than ours), and nuclei past helium solved properly, not as clusters.
