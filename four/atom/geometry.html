Geometry

Four directions at right angles, counted: spheres that grow as r³ so everything spreading from a point thins as 1/r³, more room at right angles, planes that meet at a point, turning in planes, a 3-sphere that can be combed, and knots that untie.

Room grows faster follows

Everything in this book happens in four dimensions of space: four directions, each at right angles to the other three, which we call x, y, z and w. Time is extra. Nobody can picture the fourth direction, but we can count in it, and counting is enough. This chapter is the counting. It has no physics in it, and the rest of the book leans on it.

Start with a sphere: every point at one distance r from a centre. Its surface, and the ball inside it:

In four dimensions a sphere's surface grows as r³. So anything that spreads out from a point and isn't used up on the way (light, the lines of a field, heat, a puff of gas) is spread over a surface growing as r³, and thins as 1/r³. Ten times farther away there's a thousandth as much; in ours, a hundredth.

That one fact sets the problem the rest of the book solves. A charge's field, spread over spheres by Gauss's law, falls as 1/r³, so the energy between two charges falls as 1/r². chapter 2 shows that no atom survives that, and why this universe pins the force at 1/r instead. Light keeps the 1/r³ (chapter 3), so a lamp twice as far off is an eighth as bright. And a 4D atom has more neighbours at every distance, which is why 4D's gases are held together by the crowd rather than by their nearest neighbours (chapter 13).

More room at right angles follows

Pick two directions at random. In a flat world every angle between them is equally likely. In ours, angles near 90° are likelier: there's a whole circle of directions at right angles to any one, and only a point at 0°. In 4D there's a whole sphere of them, so random directions crowd toward right angles even more:

The mean of |cos θ| between two random directions is 0.64 in 2D, 0.50 in ours and 0.42 in 4D. Aiming is harder: a cone of 30° round a direction catches 6.7% of all directions in ours and 2.9% in 4D. So two 4D molecules tumbling into each other line up well less often, and a search for how they fit best has more to search (chapter 12).

More neighbours touch. Around one ball, 6 equal balls can touch it in a plane, 12 in ours and 24 in 4D, at the corners of the 24-cell, the regular shape with no cousin in our world (chapter 7). Yet the densest packing fills less: 91%, 74%, and 62% in 4D. There's more room at every distance, and less of it gets filled.

Planes meet at a point follows

Away from a shared point it's the same story, one size up. In ours two lines usually miss each other; in 4D a line usually misses a plane, and two lines almost always do. Two planes still meet, but only at a point.

Two planes can be completely at right angles. The xy plane and the zw plane share only the origin, and every direction in one is at right angles to every direction in the other. Two planes in general sit at two angles to each other, not one. This keeps turning up: the space at right angles to a flat molecule is itself a plane, so Xater's lone pairs make a ring round it (chapter 10), and two planets orbiting in different planes needn't ever come near each other (chapter 14).

Turning happens in planes follows

A turn moves points round within a plane. There's one plane of axes in 2D, 3 in ours (xy, yz, zx) and 6 in 4D (xy, xz, xw, yz, yw, zw), so a 4D turn needs six numbers where ours needs three. In ours every turn has an axis, a line left where it was. In 4D a turn can move every line: turn xy and zw together, each at its own rate, and only the centre stays put. When the two rates are equal, every point runs round a great circle at the same speed (the third tile above). How this gives spin a handedness, and makes a 4D electron's spin feel only half of a magnetic field, is chapter 4.

The 3-sphere can be combed follows

Try to comb the hair on a ball flat all over, and there's always a crown: somewhere the hair stands up or swirls round a point. That's a theorem (Poincaré and Brouwer's, the "hairy ball"): any smooth flow over our sphere stops somewhere. The wind on Earth always has a calm spot.

The 3-sphere, the surface of a 4D ball, can be combed. Turn xy and zw at the same rate, as above. Every point of the 3-sphere moves, all at the same speed, and each runs round a great circle. These are Hopf's circles. They fill the 3-sphere and no two meet; any two stay the same distance apart all the way round; and within the 3-sphere each pair is linked once, like two rings of a chain. Projected into our space they make nested tori of circles:

Hopf's circles, a few of them, projected from the 3-sphere into our space. Each colour is one torus of circles: pink, the single ring through the middle; then tori of circles farther out, each circle linked through the others. Past the blue ones the circles grow until one becomes the line through the ring's centre. Drag to turn.

Why it matters here: gas circling a star covers a sphere, so in our world its streams must cross somewhere, and streams that cross collide, lose energy and settle into one plane. That's a reason our solar system is flat. In 4D the streams needn't cross, so a 4D nebula needn't flatten into a disk. open Whether real 4D nebulae stay round is being simulated now.

Knots untie, and gloves turn over follows

A knot in a string unties in 4D: lift one strand off into w, pass it over the other and set it back. No string can be knotted in 4D, and no two rings can be linked. Surfaces can be: a sphere can be knotted in 4D, as a string is in ours.

A left glove becomes a right one by half a turn through w, as a flat L on a table becomes a backward one by flipping it over through our third direction. So nothing that is left- or right-handed in our world is handed in 4D. A 4D molecule needs five different neighbours round one atom to be handed (chapter 8 has both to turn).

Checked, and what it gives the rest
