Stars

If gravity is pinned at 1/r like the force between charges, a 4D star holds together as ours does, solved and checked. Its outer layers press inward, its core must burn hotter, and long-lived stars need one more ingredient: the weak force.

Does a star hold together?

A star is a ball of gas holding itself up against its own weight. Two things decide whether it can. First, it must be bound: its total energy negative. Second, it must be stable: squeezed, its pressure must rise faster than its weight. Both come from the virial theorem. For gravity falling as 1/r^k in d dimensions, a gas with heat-capacity ratio γ has total energy

E = W (1 − k / d(γ − 1)),  W the gravitational energy (negative)

so a star is bound only if γ > 1 + k/d. A bound star does something essential: losing energy makes it hotter, since it contracts and its gas speeds up. That's how a cloud heats itself to ignition, and how a burning star regulates itself. A monatomic gas has γ = (d + 2)/d (5/3 ours, 3/2 in 4D, from chapter 14). A gas of light has γ = (d + 1)/d.

Under Gauss's law a 4D star is exactly marginal, as the atom was. Its gas's γ = 3/2 is the critical value: squeezing costs just what gravity pays, at every size. Pinned at 1/r, a 4D star of gas is bound with E = W/2, exactly as ours is. It clears its threshold (5/4) by the same factor of two that ours clears 4/3. And in every dimension, under a 1/r pull, a star made of light is exactly marginal. So, as for us, the heaviest stars, held up by their own radiation, live on the edge. 4D would have a maximum stellar mass as we do.

A star, solved

Those are energies. To see a star, solve it: the gas's pressure must balance its weight at every radius, with the weight computed from all of the star. Here that's done by the self-consistent field: guess the density, compute gravity from it, set the density the gas would have in that gravity, repeat. Each star is the simplest kind, its gas on one adiabat (a polytrope), radius 1 and central density 1. The checks are ours: the solver reproduces the textbook Lane–Emden stars, the n = 1 star's sin(πr)/πr profile and the central-to-mean densities of 5.99 and 54.18. The virial theorem holds to 2e-05 or better.

Our monatomic star is 6.0 times as dense at its centre as on average. The 4D one, pinned, is 14.2 times. A 4D star is more centrally concentrated, partly because 4D's volume crowds out toward the surface (as r³) and partly for the next reason. Under Gauss's law the same star is 36 times as dense at its centre, but that one is the marginal star, with E = 0.

No shell theorem: the outer layers press inward

In our world the mass outside any radius pulls on nothing inside it (Newton's shell theorem), so a layer feels only the mass beneath it. Under the pinned 1/r there's no shell theorem (the same reason a neutral atom isn't invisible). Inside a shell, gravity points toward the centre. So the outer layers add to the squeeze:

At a quarter of the way out, 59% of the inward pull comes from the mass outside. Halfway out, 26%. In ours, exactly none at any radius. 4D stars are squeezed from both sides. Outside, a star isn't quite a point mass either: a small correction, falling as 1/r³, makes planets' orbits slowly precess. They stay stable: the main pull is still 1/r, whose orbits are Kepler's closed ellipses (chapter 4).

Lighting it

A star burns when its nuclei tunnel through their mutual repulsion and fuse. Inside a star everything is ionized, and between two bare nuclei the pinned force is exactly ours: the same 1/r barrier. What 4D changes is subtler. In four dimensions even a head-on (s-wave) meeting carries a built-in centrifugal barrier, 3/4 · ħ²/2μr², because there's more room to miss. Near the nucleus it's large: at 2 fm it's about ten times the electric barrier between two protons. Tunnelling through both, in the WKB approximation with Langer's correction (the integral checked against the textbook Gamow factor), at equal nuclear physics:

At the same temperature, 4D nuclei tunnel through 15 to 56 times less often than ours (protons, nuclei 1 to 4 fm across), and 9 times less often for deuterium. A star makes up a shortfall the way ours do, by contracting until its core burns fast enough: hotter, denser, or both. If temperature alone made it up, the core would run 1.7 to 2.2 times hotter, around 30 million kelvin, which is the dotted line. That's an upper end, not a prediction. A 4D rate per pair of nuclei carries one more length in its units than ours, so "as fast as ours" only means something once a 4D star's density is known, and that needs gravity's strength and 4D's nuclear physics. The barrier deficit is the robust number.

Is 4D just hotter because there's another direction to put energy into? No. Each direction of motion holds the same kT/2 at a given temperature, so a 4D nucleus moves faster than ours at the same temperature, not slower. More of them are fast, too: the energy spread carries an extra √E. But the 4D cross-section's geometry cancels that weight exactly, in every dimension. What's left is the barrier, and the deficit above is all barrier.

How big, how small

A contracting ball heats until its electrons, squeezed, resist by Pauli's principle alone. That caps its temperature, so the smallest star that lights holds about α_G^(−4/3) (kT_burn / m_e c²)^(2/3) protons in 4D, where α_G = G m_p²/ħc measures gravity's strength (pinned, it's again a pure number: about 6 × 10⁻³⁹ in ours). The largest is where light's pressure takes over, about α_G^(−4/3) protons. The dead remnant's limit, where relativistic electrons can no longer hold it up (Chandrasekhar's), lands at the same scale. In ours both carry α_G^(−3/2). So the window of star masses, largest over smallest, is about (m_e c²/kT_burn)^(2/3) in 4D (the power 3/4 in ours), whatever gravity's strength: roughly 30 in 4D with its hotter cores, against roughly 100 in ours by the same rough count (the real one, with all the factors, is 0.08 to 150 solar masses). These are scaling estimates, not solved stars.

One of them is solved: the dead remnant's limit. Electrons squeezed to near light speed press as c p_F n/(d + 1), the stiffness 1 + 1/d that gravity under 1/r needs exactly, so a ball of them is the polytrope of index d. In ours that's Chandrasekhar's star, and the solver gives 1.435 solar masses for two nucleons per electron (1.457, the textbook). Pinned in 4D the index-4 polytrope closes too, at a finite radius, so 4D has a Chandrasekhar mass: 1.52 α_G^(−4/3) nucleons. A dead 4D star heavier than that collapses, as ours do.

What sets G

Nothing in physics fixes Newton's G, in our world or this one. Gravity pinned through the lens keeps the force's units, so G could be ours. It can't be, for this reason: 4D matter is far denser per 4-volume than ours is per 3-volume (a rock's atoms, a few ångströms apart, give about 2 × 10¹³ kg/m⁴), and a ball's surface gravity grows as G ρ R². Escape speed obeys v² = 2gR in any dimension, so a planet with Earth's gravity and Earth's hold on its air has Earth's radius in 4D too, and that fixes G ρ₄ = 2g/π²R². computed The rock, now computed (chapter 14's salts, with the elements the stars give back, chapter 15): nagnesium pxide, Ng²⁺Px²⁻, our magnesia's counterpart, and todium gluoride, To⁺G⁻, our sodium fluoride's, come out 1.8–2.3 × 10¹³ kg/m⁴ (checked: our sodium fluoride 2.56 g/cm³ against 2.56 measured, our magnesia 3.66 against 3.58). For a planet of that rock, G is 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ to 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ of ours; with a core of the stars' iron, up to twice as dense overall as Earth is over its rock, as low as 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁷. With that G the stars come out Sun-sized, and a planet weighs 6 × 10⁻⁶ to 9 × 10⁻⁶ of a Chandrasekhar star (Earth weighs 3 × 10⁻⁶ of our Sun). So G is a choice, made by a requirement: that there be planets like ours. follows And at that G the main sequence above says what kind of sun it gets: a star as long-lived as ours is 1.1 times the Chandrasekhar mass, 30,797 K at its surface, blue-white, with a core at 19 million K; a planet warm enough for liquid water sits 1.1 × 10¹¹ m out and its year lasts 1.2 of ours. It falls well inside the stars' window for G, above the floor where nothing heavier than helium is made in time.

The fuel: the weak force, added

Our Sun burns hydrogen, and its first step turns a proton into a neutron. That takes the weak force, which chemistry never needs. Without it a star could burn only fuel left from the universe's first minutes (deuterium and other light nuclei, which fuse by the strong force alone), then shine by contracting, for millions of years rather than billions. So the weak force is added to the universe's list, with its neutrino. It's the first ingredient added for astronomy rather than chemistry. Stars burn hydrogen as ours do, and live long enough for planets to have time.

What 4D stars burn to

A 4D star doesn't stop at helium. Where hydrogen and helium burn together it turns its core into one or two elements eight to fifteen places up the table, and its heaviest cores go on toward element 103–137 (chapter 15). computed How much each stage releases, per nucleon (MeV):

A 4D star gets about three times our energy from each unit of hydrogen, less a MeV or so to neutrinos for the protons it turns into neutrons. follows What that does:

  • Its core runs about as hot. A core runs as hot as its reactions need to get through their barriers; what each reaction pays barely enters. The burning goes as about T9–15 here (our proton chain about T⁴, our carbon cycle T¹⁸), so needing a third as many reactions cools the core by about a tenth: a star as long-lived as our Sun burns at 15–21 million K.
  • It's brighter for its fuel, or lives longer. At our Sun's pace of burning it gives out about three times the energy; at our Sun's brightness it lasts about three times as long. How bright a star of each mass is waits on how light gets out of a 4D star, and on G.
  • It has no helium-burning life. Ours burn hydrogen, then light the helium the hydrogen left. A 4D core burns both together, so when its hydrogen is gone there's nothing left to light short of the ash itself, which takes a core at the Chandrasekhar mass: most 4D stars go from hydrogen burning to dead, as stars of gluorine, oeon or pxygen.
  • Its last stages hold as much as its whole hydrogen life. Everything after helium gives ours about 2 MeV per nucleon, so our heavy stars' last stages last years, then days. A 4D heavy core's ash fusing and its march to equilibrium give it about as much again as its hydrogen did. And near the Chandrasekhar mass it lights them squeezed so hard it can't regulate them: those cores most likely explode, throwing their rock row and their iron out (chapter 15).

The main sequence, by G

Gravity's strength is still open (chapter 5), and in 4D it matters twice over: it sets how bright a star of each mass is, and starlight thins as 1/r³, so a planet's warmth hangs on brightness more steeply than ours does. So the main sequence is solved at each G. computed A star in balance, solved: pressure against gravity (pinned 4D's from the whole star, since it has no shell theorem), energy from the book's own rates, carried out by light or, where light can't, by stirring; the electrons as they are, partly degenerate; the surface where the star turns transparent. 4D's opacity is worked out for this universe: light scattering off free electrons through the lens vacuum, σ = (3π³/4) re² c/ω, which falls as the light gets bluer, so a hotter 4D star is clearer; and absorption by electrons passing ions (free–free), where 4D's extra direction makes the sum over passes converge with none of the logarithm ours needs. It goes as ρT⁻⁵ (Kramers' ours, ρT−3.5). The same reasoning gives our free–free at the Sun's centre about half of Kramers', so it's run at half and twice its value too, the band in brackets below. The check, ours: the young Sun comes out at 0.64 of today's Sun's brightness and 13.5 million K at its centre (the textbook's 0.7 and 13.5), and stars from half a solar mass to thirty within about one and a half times their published brightness.

A 4D star brightens as about the fifth power of its mass (ours, the third and a half), and at a given share of the Chandrasekhar mass it burns its fuel as G2/3: weaker gravity makes bigger, cooler, longer-lived stars. follows So a long life and the colour of sunlight are different stars in 4D unless G is very weak. Where G is within a few powers of ten of the Earth-like estimate, a star that lasts as long as our Sun is blue-hot, tens of thousands of kelvin, and a star the colour of sunlight is a faint one that outlasts the universe many times. Its planets' years run from under an hour at 10−8 of our gravity to a lifetime at 10−20. And a star that long-lived exists up to at least 10−8 (four tenths of the Chandrasekhar mass, 20,000 K): the stars set no ceiling on G within what's solved, only the floor below about 10−22, where nothing finishes in time to make anything heavier than helium (chapter 15). And at a given colour, a star's life hardly depends on G at the cool end (the lifetime curves for the two weakest gravities nearly lie on one another): what weaker gravity changes is how massive, and how bright, a star of that colour is. And the core temperature falls with G too, which is what decides what the core makes (chapter 15): G reaches all the way to the water. open The coolest surfaces: below about 5,000 K 4D's iydrogen, holding its electron by only 6 eV, takes it back, and the gas stops being the ionized one these opacities assume, so the faintest stars' colours are rough. The plasma: screening and frozen fields

A star's core is a plasma, and the lens vacuum (chapter 3) changes two things about it: how much the free charges help nuclei fuse, and how long a magnetic field lasts.

computed Screening. Each nucleus gathers a cloud of opposite charge, which lowers the barrier two nuclei must tunnel through by the potential the cloud makes at the nucleus: a rate gains eH (Salpeter's screening). In ours that potential is Debye's, √(4πκ) per unit charge, κ = Σ n q²/kT. With 4D's pinned kernel, 4π²/|k|³ in place of 4π/k², the same calculation gives (4π²)4/3 κ1/3/12√3π: finite, though the screened potential keeps a power-law tail (the |k|³ isn't smooth, so the cut-off isn't exponential), and growing as the density's cube root rather than its square root. computed Strong screening. Past H ≈ 1 the cloud isn't a small correction. Each nucleus sits in its own sphere of electrons, just enough to cancel its charge, and two fusing nuclei merge their spheres, gaining the difference in the spheres' electrostatic energies (Salpeter's ion spheres). In ours a sphere's energy is −0.9 Z²/a, the textbook value and the check. In 4D there's no shell theorem, so the electron ball's energy with itself has to be added up shell by shell with the pinned potential: −0.816 Z²/a (checked against a Monte Carlo of the 4-ball), with the sphere's radius a growing as Z1/4, so the energy grows as Z7/4 where ours grows as Z5/3. The adopted factor is the smaller of the two exponents, weak (electrons counted) and strong, the usual bridge: each overestimates outside its own regime.

Ordinary 4D stars burn a few percent faster than their bare rates for protons, and up to a third faster for helium-4 pairs; the lightest, densest, most degenerate cores up to 1.8 times as fast for protons and up to seven times for helium-4 (the ion spheres cap the weak formula's fourteen). Heavy nuclei gain a few times in the hot, massive cores that can burn them (×2.3 for element 10 with element 10 in the heaviest stars, ×30–500 in Sun-long ones, against ×27 in our Sun), and 1010–1014 in the lightest stars, which never get hot enough to burn them. So screening speeds up 4D's deep burning by factors, not powers of ten. open These are main-sequence cores; the hotter, denser cores of the later burning stages (chapter 15) aren't modelled yet, and between H ≈ 1 and the ion spheres' regime the bridge is rough (ours uses fitted intermediate formulas; 4D has none).

computed Frozen fields. The lens vacuum's μ = μ₀/ℓk makes a current's field pinned like the charges' (its vector potential 4π²J/c²|k|³), so with Ohm's law a field of size L decays at a rate c²k³/4π²σ where ours decays at c²k²/4πσ: the magnetic Reynolds number grows as L² instead of L. And the electrons' collisions that set σ change: a small-angle Coulomb deflection is 2b₀/b in both worlds, but in 4D the impact parameters fill a three-dimensional plane, so the momentum lost grows linearly out to the screening length instead of as Spitzer's logarithm. Our Sun's centre, the check, gives a magnetic diffusivity of 6.6×10⁻³ m²/s, Spitzer's value.

A 4D star's magnetic field never decays by resistance: 1025–1035 years at the star's own size, a magnetic Reynolds number of 1028–1035 against our Sun's 1013. Whatever field a 4D star is born with, or makes, it keeps; field lines can only break and rejoin through collisionless physics at the smallest scales. The likely picture is fossil fields, strong and permanent, which sets the stars' winds, their spin-down and the weather close-in planets live in. open Whether a 4D star can make a field at all (its spin can sit in two planes, its field is a two-form, its helicity a three-form, and our anti-dynamo theorems needn't carry over), and a magnetized wind with the lens's μ(k) for how fast a star spins down.
