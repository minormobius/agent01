Stars

If gravity is pinned at 1/r like the force between charges, a 4D star holds together as ours does, solved and checked. Its outer layers press inward, its core must burn hotter, and long-lived stars need one more ingredient: the weak force.

Gravity's routes

Chapter 1 pinned the force between charges at 1/r and left gravity open. Gravity hardly matters to atoms, but it makes the stars. Its problem is the same as charge's. A field obeying Gauss's law in four dimensions falls as 1/r², and for 1/r we need a propagator of 1/k³. So the same forks appear (charge's, for comparison), with gravity's own twists. Gravity pulls on everything alike, it's nonlinear in our world already, and it has waves and black holes of its own.

1 · Keep 4D gravity as it is: Gauss's law, 1/r²

The four-dimensional version of Einstein's gravity, whose slow, weak limit is 1/r². It keeps everything: locality, relativity, the equivalence principle, waves at c.

costs: no stable orbit, and a star of gas is exactly marginal (below). Stiff bodies like rock could hold themselves together, but nothing would orbit anything. No solar systems.

2 · Keep relativity, give up locality: a graviton with □^(3/2)

As for charge, a fractional power of the wave operator gives 1/r statics and keeps relativity.

costs: negative probabilities, by the same unitarity bound as charge's route 2.

3 · A dispersive vacuum for gravity

Give gravity's vacuum a response that grows with wavenumber, as charge's route 3 does.

costs: gravitational waves disperse with no speed limit. Long waves outrun short ones without bound.

4 · Give up superposition: a nonlinear law

Make gravity's Gauss law nonlinear, ∇·(|g|^(1/2) g) ∝ ρ in 4D, the kind of law Bekenstein and Milgrom wrote for our galaxies. A point mass then pulls as 1/r. Gravity is already nonlinear in our world, though not in its weak, everyday limit. This law keeps locality, and in our world such laws have been given relativistic completions.

costs: masses don't add. A body's pull grows as its mass to the 2/3 power, so two stars together pull less than twice one, and a planet's motion feels the galaxy it sits in. In exchange the divergence theorem still holds: the shell theorem survives. A round star acts as a point, its outer layers don't press inward, and orbits round it are exactly Kepler's.

5 · A small fourth dimension, for gravity only

Let gravity see three dimensions while matter sees four.

costs: coherence. Gravity pulls on all energy, including motion along w, so it can't ignore a dimension that matter moves in. This route breaks the premise.

6 · The lens, as for charge (assumed below)

Gravity's static pull seen through the same kind of lens, a 1/k³ kernel, so 1/r between masses. Everything still falls alike, and masses add. Gravity's waves can stay as 4D Einstein's.

we'd pay: relativity for gravity's pull, an absolute "now" for gravity as for charge, and no black holes as Einstein's are defined. No shell theorem either: a star's outer layers press inward, and orbits round a star slowly precess.

Routes 4 and 6 are the real choice, and they predict different skies. Under the lens, orbits round a star precess and masses add. Under the nonlinear law, orbits close exactly but a cluster of stars pulls less than its parts. Both give bound stars, because both make a star's gravitational energy scale as 1/R. This chapter assumes the lens, for its symmetry with charge, and marks where route 4 would differ. The choice is open.

Does a star hold together?

A star is a ball of gas holding itself up against its own weight. Two things decide whether it can. First, it must be bound: its total energy negative. Second, it must be stable: squeezed, its pressure must rise faster than its weight. Both come from the virial theorem. For gravity falling as 1/r^k in d dimensions, a gas with heat-capacity ratio γ has total energy

E = W (1 − k / d(γ − 1)),  W the gravitational energy (negative)

so a star is bound only if γ > 1 + k/d. A bound star does something essential: losing energy makes it hotter, since it contracts and its gas speeds up. That's how a cloud heats itself to ignition, and how a burning star regulates itself. A monatomic gas has γ = (d + 2)/d (5/3 ours, 3/2 in 4D, from chapter 13). A gas of light has γ = (d + 1)/d.

Under Gauss's law a 4D star is exactly marginal, as the atom was. Its gas's γ = 3/2 is the critical value: squeezing costs just what gravity pays, at every size. Pinned at 1/r, a 4D star of gas is bound with E = W/2, exactly as ours is. It clears its threshold (5/4) by the same factor of two that ours clears 4/3. And in every dimension, under a 1/r pull, a star made of light is exactly marginal. So, as for us, the heaviest stars, held up by their own radiation, live on the edge. 4D would have a maximum stellar mass as we do.

A star, solved

Those are energies. To see a star, solve it: the gas's pressure must balance its weight at every radius, with the weight computed from all of the star. Here that's done by the self-consistent field: guess the density, compute gravity from it, set the density the gas would have in that gravity, repeat. Each star is the simplest kind, its gas on one adiabat (a polytrope), radius 1 and central density 1. The checks are ours: the solver reproduces the textbook Lane–Emden stars, the n = 1 star's sin(πr)/πr profile and the central-to-mean densities of 5.99 and 54.18. The virial theorem holds to 2e-05 or better.

Our monatomic star is 6.0 times as dense at its centre as on average. The 4D one, pinned, is 14.2 times. A 4D star is more centrally concentrated, partly because 4D's volume crowds out toward the surface (as r³) and partly for the next reason. Under Gauss's law the same star is 36 times as dense at its centre, but that one is the marginal star, with E = 0.

No shell theorem: the outer layers press inward

In our world the mass outside any radius pulls on nothing inside it (Newton's shell theorem), so a layer feels only the mass beneath it. Under the pinned 1/r there's no shell theorem (the same reason a neutral atom isn't invisible). Inside a shell, gravity points toward the centre. So the outer layers add to the squeeze:

At a quarter of the way out, 59% of the inward pull comes from the mass outside. Halfway out, 26%. In ours, exactly none at any radius. 4D stars are squeezed from both sides. Outside, a star isn't quite a point mass either: a small correction, falling as 1/r³, makes planets' orbits slowly precess. They stay stable: the main pull is still 1/r, whose orbits are Kepler's closed ellipses (chapter 4).

Lighting it

A star burns when its nuclei tunnel through their mutual repulsion and fuse. Inside a star everything is ionized, and between two bare nuclei the pinned force is exactly ours: the same 1/r barrier. What 4D changes is subtler. In four dimensions even a head-on (s-wave) meeting carries a built-in centrifugal barrier, 3/4 · ħ²/2μr², because there's more room to miss. Near the nucleus it's large: at 2 fm it's about ten times the electric barrier between two protons. At the same temperature, 4D nuclei tunnel through 15 to 56 times less often than ours (protons, nuclei 1 to 4 fm across), and 9 times less often for deuterium. A star makes up a shortfall the way ours do, by contracting until its core burns fast enough: hotter, denser, or both. If temperature alone made it up, the core would run 1.7 to 2.2 times hotter, around 30 million kelvin, which is the dotted line. That's an upper end, not a prediction. A 4D rate per pair of nuclei carries one more length in its units than ours, so "as fast as ours" only means something once a 4D star's density is known, and that needs gravity's strength and 4D's nuclear physics. The barrier deficit is the robust number.

Is 4D just hotter because there's another direction to put energy into? No. Each direction of motion holds the same kT/2 at a given temperature, so a 4D nucleus moves faster than ours at the same temperature, not slower. More of them are fast, too: the energy spread carries an extra √E. But the 4D cross-section's geometry cancels that weight exactly, in every dimension. What's left is the barrier, and the deficit above is all barrier.

How big, how small

A contracting ball heats until its electrons, squeezed, resist by Pauli's principle alone. That caps its temperature, so the smallest star that lights holds about α_G^(−4/3) (kT_burn / m_e c²)^(2/3) protons in 4D, where α_G = G m_p²/ħc measures gravity's strength (pinned, it's again a pure number: about 6 × 10⁻³⁹ in ours). The largest is where light's pressure takes over, about α_G^(−4/3) protons. The dead remnant's limit, where relativistic electrons can no longer hold it up (Chandrasekhar's), lands at the same scale. In ours both carry α_G^(−3/2). So the window of star masses, largest over smallest, is about (m_e c²/kT_burn)^(2/3) in 4D (the power 3/4 in ours), whatever gravity's strength: roughly 30 in 4D with its hotter cores, against roughly 100 in ours by the same rough count (the real one, with all the factors, is 0.08 to 150 solar masses). These are scaling estimates, not solved stars.

The fuel: the weak force, added

Our Sun burns hydrogen, and its first step turns a proton into a neutron. That takes the weak force, which chemistry never needs. Without it a star could burn only fuel left from the universe's first minutes (deuterium and other light nuclei, which fuse by the strong force alone), then shine by contracting, for millions of years rather than billions. So the weak force is added to the universe's list, with its neutrino. It's the first ingredient added for astronomy rather than chemistry. Stars burn hydrogen as ours do, and live long enough for planets to have time.

Orbits

Stars need planets to matter, and planets need orbits. Under the pinned 1/r, two bodies orbit as Kepler said. A central pull keeps an orbit in one plane, even in 4D, and a 1/r pull closes it into an ellipse in any number of dimensions (chapter 4). Under Gauss's 1/r² no orbit is stable. Orbits round a real star precess

Without a shell theorem, a round star doesn't pull exactly like a point. Outside it, its pull carries a small correction, −1/r + ⟨r²⟩/8r³, where ⟨r²⟩ is the mean square of where its mass sits (0.32 R² for the 4D star solved above). That turns an orbit's closest point backwards by

Δϖ = −(3π/4) ⟨r²⟩ / p² per orbit

(p the orbit's semi-latus rectum, from Landau's formula for a 1/r³ perturbation). Checked by integrating orbits in the solved star's own pull, it agrees to 1.3% three star radii out, where the next, smaller correction still shows, and to 0.09% at twelve. For a star the Sun's size, an orbit at Earth's distance would turn back 3.4 arcseconds per orbit, and one at Mercury's 23. Mercury's own, from relativity, is about 0.1. It's slow, but an astronomer would find it, and it tells the routes apart: under route 4's nonlinear law the shell theorem holds and this precession is zero.

Planets in different planes barely notice each other

In 4D two orbits can lie in completely orthogonal planes, xy and zw, which meet only at the star. Then the two planets' positions are always at right angles, so their distance is √(r₁² + r₂²). For circular orbits that never changes, and neither can tug the other along its orbit. Run with Jupiter-mass planets for 3,000 orbits:

Equal radii is the strangest case. The two planets share a period, so their tugs on each other add up orbit after orbit, and each plane turns all the way round: in the run, planet 1's orbit swung completely into the plane planet 2 had started in, and back, in about 1,500 orbits. The two planes stayed at right angles throughout, and the planets never came closer than √2.

A 4D planetary system can pack its planets far tighter than ours, at nearly the same distance from the star, in different planes. Planes partly turned apart lie in between: at 30°, two planets 5% apart in radius came within 0.53 of each other and still survived 3,000 orbits.

The planes don't sit still. The planets' pulls turn their planes through 4D, by as much as 88% of planet 1's orbit swinging out of where it began. But the angle between the two planes holds, the 4D version of how our planets' tilts to one another keep while their nodes slide round:

Days with two rates

A planet spins too, and a 4D spin is a turning in a plane, or in two planes at once at two rates (chapter 4). A planet spinning both ways would have a sky that cycles on two clocks, its sunrises returning only when the two line up. Which way real planets spin depends on how they formed and how tides wear them, and that's open.

Checked here: Kepler's closed orbits in 4D, the precession formula against integration, and the two-planet runs, with 3,000 orbits per case. That's short against a planetary system's life. Longer runs, more planets, and tides are next.
