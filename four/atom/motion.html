Motion and spin

F = ma, orbits, rotation in planes, tumbling bodies, electron spin with a handedness, and bonds that twist three ways.

Things that move

Points move by F = ma, with four components where we have three, and momentum is kept. Under any central force a body never leaves the plane of its position and velocity, so a 4D orbit is a flat curve. The plane just leans in any of four directions. Under the pinned 1/r it's one of Kepler's ellipses, closed, because the Runge–Lenz vector, A = p²x − (x·p)p − x/r, is kept in any number of dimensions. Under Gauss's 1/r³ force there's no stable orbit (Ehrenfest, 1917): nudge a circle and the planet spirals off. Try both below.

Turning happens in planes

In 3D you turn about an axis. In 4D a turn moves one plane and fixes the plane perpendicular to it, and there are six planes (xy, xz, xw, yz, yw, zw). So angular velocity and angular momentum have six components. They aren't arrows: they're one number per plane. A 4D body can turn in two perpendicular planes at once, at two rates: a double rotation. With equal rates (an isoclinic turn), nothing but the centre stands still, and every point moves round a great circle.

The six planes split into two independent sets of three, which commute. Every 4D turn is a left isoclinic turn and a right one at once. That's SU(2) × SU(2): two copies of the group that turns spin in our world, where there's one.

Spin, with a handedness

Electrons still come in pairs. The smallest spin-½ objects of 4D turning have two states, so each orbital holds two electrons, as in our world. That's what gives the periodic table its shells of 2, 10, 28. It's also what a relativistic electron gives: in 4 + 1 dimensions the Dirac equation has four components. The direction of time's matrix there is the product of the four space ones, so a slow electron's two large components carry only one half of SU(2) × SU(2).

That makes the spin handed. An electron's spin turns with the left half of any rotation and ignores the right half. A full turn in a single plane flips its sign, as ours does. A left-isoclinic turn spins it twice as fast. A right-isoclinic turn doesn't touch it at all: in the "turning" view the needle stops. Which half electrons belong to is a choice the universe makes once, and all matter shares it.

Magnetism has the same split. A magnetic field has six components, one per plane, and they too divide into halves. Only the left half can flip an electron's spin. A field made only of the right half bends electrons' paths but leaves their spin levels unsplit.

Assumed, not derived: that the electron is the smallest spinor (a four-state one, holding four to an orbital, is possible in principle), and that half-integer spin means it obeys Pauli's exclusion. The theorem behind that holds for local, relativistic theories, and the lens vacuum isn't fully one. We take both as part of the universe's definition.

Atoms in a magnet computed

There's no cross product. Our magnetic field is really one number per plane, three of them, and we draw it as an arrow only because in 3D every plane has one line at right angles to it. In 4D a plane's partner is another plane, so the field stays six numbers, one per plane, and it pushes a charge by turning its velocity within those planes: F = qBv, B acting on v as a turn does (the fields toys). Only some fields are isoclinic. Every field is a left half plus a right half, and it's isoclinic when one half is zero. A field from a single flat coil has equal halves: one plane turns, and the plane across it is left free.

The electron's equation, derived. A slow electron obeys Pauli's equation, which needs no relativity: it's the square of a first-order equation (Lévy-Leblond's, 1967), and squaring is where the spin's magnetism comes from. In 4D the first-order operator is built from quaternion units, and their product gives (τ̄·a)(τ·b) = a·b + iσ·ℓ(a∧b), where ℓ takes a plane's left half. So an electron's energy in a field is π²/2m − (q/2m)σ·ℓ(B): its spin feels the field's left half only, as strongly as ours for a field in one plane (g = 2), twice as strongly for a left-isoclinic field, and not at all for a right one. The book stated the left-only coupling above; this is where it comes from.

Every atom is diamagnetic: a field squeezes its electrons' orbits a little, raising its energy by (Σ⟨r²⟩/4d)|B|², so it's pushed out of a magnet, by both halves alike. That's Langevin's formula, in any dimension. From the solver's own clouds it gives our helium's measured susceptibility (within 0.2%), and neon, argon and krypton 9 to 12% too strong, the usual size of that formula's error. 4D's atoms are bigger, so they're more diamagnetic: 4D helium 2.8 times ours.

Most atoms carry a spin, and their pull is handed. Counting unpaired electrons by Hund's first rule on the solver's configurations gives our ground-state spins as measured for 34 of 36 elements (the misses are configurations the solver gets wrong). In 4D, 66 of 78 atoms carry a spin; only elements 2, 4, 12, 14, 22, 24, 42, 50, 52, 68, 70 and 78 don't. A 4D d shell has nine orbitals, so elements 32 and 60 hold 10 unpaired electrons, where our most is chromium's 6. A free spin is drawn into a magnet (Curie's paramagnetism, S(S + 1)/3kT), and at room temperature that outweighs diamagnetism about 187 times for 4D hydrogen. But only the left half pulls:

Spinning things. A ball of charge that spins is a current, so it makes a magnetic field shaped like its spin, falling as 1/r³ as ours does; a ball spinning in two planes makes both. Seen from inside our 3D space, the six numbers of the field split into two 3D fields. The part within our space turns a moving charge's path as our magnetic field does; a spin in xy makes it, shaped exactly like a bar magnet's. The part reaching into w has nothing to turn within our space: it pushes a charge moving through it out of our space altogether. A spin in zw makes only that part, as seen from the ball's middle (the spinning ball, to fire charges past). Spinning metal isn't enough, in either world, since its electrons and nuclei turn together. What spinning does to metal is the Barnett effect: a spun lump of iron magnetizes a little, because its electrons' spins feel the turning as a field. Here they feel only its left half, so iron spun one way magnetizes and spun the mirror way doesn't (follows, not computed).

Unpaired electrons by element, by Hund's first rule on the solver's configurations (sphere-averaged, so the transition metals are the least sure). Left out: orbital moments, which feel both halves but need open-shell states the solver doesn't have; atoms bound into molecules, which mostly pair their spins (Xater and XH₃ are closed-shell, so diamagnetic); and magnets made of matter (ferromagnetism), which is far off.

Tumbling, and twisting

A rigid body tumbles by Euler's equations in six components: in the body's frame dM/dt = [M, Ω], with M_ab = (j_a + j_b)Ω_ab. Energy, tr M² and the Pfaffian are kept. Manakov showed in 1976 that enough more is kept for the motion to be exactly solvable, as it is in 3D. A free 4D box never settles: the "tumbling" view tumbles one with nothing pushing it.

A bond fixes a line, and the turns that keep a line are the 3D turns of the space across it: three angles of twist about every single bond, where ours has one. Each darbon of D₂H₈ holds four hydrogens on a small tetrahedron across the bond, and the two tetrahedra turn against each other in any 3D way. 4D chains are far floppier than ours, which matters to anything long and folded: proteins, plastics.

For the molecular-dynamics view

Positions, velocities and the step-by-step update (velocity Verlet) carry over unchanged, and so do bond-stretch and bond-angle terms, at 104.48°. What's new is twist potentials in three angles, and six-component angular velocity for rigid parts. The pairwise forces are re-derived in forces between molecules.
