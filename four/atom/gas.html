Gases

PV = NkT in any dimension, the real gas's B₂ computed from the pairs, a refrigerator's throttle, and why 4D's noble gases are held by the crowd rather than their neighbours, so that they boil far hotter than their wells suggest.

The ideal gas, in any dimension

A gas is atoms flying about and hitting the walls. Pressure is the momentum they carry into a wall each second, per unit of wall, and in 4D a wall is a 3D surface. Count it and you get PV = NkT in any number of dimensions. The front page's boxes count it live. What changes with d is where the energy goes: each direction of motion holds kT/2, so a 4D atom carries 2kT, not 3/2 kT. Its heat capacities are C_v = d/2 and C_p = d/2 + 1 (in k per atom), and their ratio is γ = (d + 2)/d: 3/2 in 4D, against our 5/3.

The speeds spread as Maxwell's distribution, with the 3-sphere's area 2π²v³ where ours has 4πv²:

Squeeze a gas without letting heat out and it warms, holding TV^(γ − 1) fixed. Halve the volume and a 4D gas warms by √2 = 1.41, ours by 2^(2/3) = 1.59. To double the temperature takes a 4× squeeze in 4D and 2.8× in ours: a 4D gas is harder to heat by squeezing, because its energy is spread over one more direction. Every engine and every refrigerator runs on that exchange, so 4D's run a little softer. It follows from counting, with no physics of 4D's own.

A real gas: the second virial coefficient

Real atoms pull on each other and can't overlap. The first correction to PV = NkT, at low density n, is P/nkT = 1 + B₂(T) n, where

B₂(T) = −½ ∫ (e^(−u(r)/kT) − 1) dV,   dV = 2π² r³ dr in 4D (4πr² dr in ours)

and u(r) is the pair's energy: computed, for the noble gases, from the solver's own atoms. Negative B₂ means the pull wins and the gas is denser than ideal. Positive means the atoms' size wins. The Boyle temperature is where it changes sign.

The checks. The integral is exact against hard spheres and square wells in 2 to 5 dimensions. For Lennard-Jones atoms it gives the Boyle temperature at 3.4179 ε/k and the inversion (below) at 6.431 ε/k, the published values. Run on our computed argon pair, it puts argon's Boyle temperature at 320 to 580 K. Measured, it's about 407 K (sources give 392 to 411), inside the range. At room temperature it gives B₂ = -3 to -38 cm³/mol, around the measured −16. The method carries the forces chapter's 1.4 to 1.8 times overbinding, and that's what the ranges are.

Our gases cross zero at about 2.8 ε/k. 4D's cross at about 28. Measured against its own well, a 4D noble gas behaves as if it were ten times stickier than its well is deep. Element 12's Boyle temperature is 16,000 to 29,000 K, and element 22's 61,000 to 109,000 K.

Held from a distance

The well isn't where 4D's pull is. Two neutral 4D atoms attract as −B/R⁵ out to any distance (the permanent pull), and the space around an atom at distance r grows as r³. Their product falls only as 1/r², so the far shells add up. Ours fall as R⁻⁶ and the space as r², a product falling as 1/r⁴, so our gases feel almost nothing past the first neighbours. Our noble gases are held by their neighbours. 4D's are held by the crowd. Argon's twelve nearest give 80% of its crystal's hold. Element 12's twenty-four give 31%, and the whole crowd holds each atom with 39 times its pair's well depth, where argon's holds with 7.5. Summed pair by pair, element 12's crystal pulls itself tighter than the pair's spacing, onto the repulsive wall where the pair model is least sure. These numbers are at the pair's spacing, which is the cautious choice.

The refrigerator

A refrigerator's cold comes from a valve. Push a gas through a narrow opening from high pressure to low (Joule and Thomson's throttle) and it cools, because the atoms climb out of each other's pull as they spread apart. But only below the gas's inversion temperature. Above it, the atoms' collisions win and the gas warms instead. At low pressure the change is

ΔT/T ≈ n (T dB₂/dT − B₂) / C_p

so the sign of T B₂′ − B₂ decides it. The chart gives the cooling per throttle at the same crowding n (taking the pair's own size as the unit of volume), so ours and 4D's can be compared. Solid is the computed pair, and dashed is it divided by 1.8.

Every 4D noble gas cools in a throttle up to tens of thousands of kelvin. Element 12 cools below 31,000 to 56,000 K. Ours stop far lower: argon's computed inversion is 600 to 1,100 K, around the measured 794. Even element 2, 4D's helium, cools below 3,100 to 5,600 K. Our helium, at room temperature, warms in a throttle, and has to be cooled below about 40 K before a valve will cool it at all, and our hydrogen below about 200 K. A 4D throttle at room temperature cools every noble gas it could hold, element 2 included. 4D needs no precooling. At three times its well depth, at the same crowding, element 12 cools by 8 times as much per throttle as argon.

Yet in its own terms the 4D gas is ours, rescaled. The inversion comes at 1.85 times the Boyle temperature for argon and 1.93 for element 12 (and 1.88 for Lennard-Jones). Measured against the whole pull, B₂'s integral, rather than the well, the low-density gas is the same gas in both worlds. In 4D the well is simply the wrong yardstick.

Element 2 is the least sure. The same method overbinds our helium about four times, and the forces chapter leaves our helium out for that reason. Divide element 2's pull by four and it still cools below about 1,300 K, well above room temperature. Light atoms also need quantum corrections to B₂ at low temperature, which this classical integral leaves out.

When does it boil? An open question

The forces chapter estimated boiling points the way ours go: our noble gases boil at about 0.7 of their well depth, and condense below a critical point at about 1.26. That rule counts only the well. The far pull just shown says it can't be right in 4D. Here are three corresponding-states rules, each read off our argon: from the well, from the Boyle temperature (between mean field's 8/27 of it and argon's 0.37), and from the crystal's hold.

In our world the three agree, and each range holds the measured critical point: neon 42–75 K from the well against 44.5 measured, argon 147–265 against 150.9, krypton 207–373 against 209.4. They agree because our noble gases' potentials all have the same shape. In 4D they split, by a factor of 5 to 8. From the well, element 12's critical point is 730 to 1,300 K. From its Boyle temperature it's 4,800 to 11,000 K, and from its crystal 3,600 to 6,500 K.

We take the two that count the crowd: element 12 condenses below about 3,600 to 11,000 K, and element 22 below about 10,000 to 40,000 K. A liquid's atom feels all its neighbours at once, and in 4D most of its pull comes from beyond the nearest. Mean-field theory, which builds the critical point from that whole integral, gets better the more neighbours each atom feels. And four is the liquid–vapour critical point's upper critical dimension: from four dimensions up, mean field's description of the critical point (van der Waals', in effect) becomes exact, apart from slowly varying logarithmic corrections. That is a theorem about the universality class, not a computation of ours. 4D's noble gases are hot-furnace liquids, and the forces chapter's 400 to 730 K was a floor.

Element 2 can't be placed. The two crowd rules put it at 300 to 1,200 K, but if its pull is overbound four times as our helium's is, that falls to roughly 70 to 270 K, below room temperature or near it. Whether 4D has any noble gas that stays a gas at room temperature is open. So is which gases it has at all: 4D hydrogen's molecule hasn't been computed.

No "boiling point at one atmosphere" can be given, because 4D has no atmosphere to set one. Pressure in 4D is an energy per 4-volume, and which pressure is natural waits on a planet, which waits on gravity. The critical point is the number that can be compared. The real test is to simulate liquid and vapour side by side, on argon first to calibrate, then on element 12. It's a job for the compute runner.

What's computed and what's open

Computed: the ideal gas in any dimension; B₂, the Boyle and inversion temperatures, and the throttle's cooling, for the noble gases of both worlds, from their computed pairs; how much of each gas's pull comes from afar; the crystal's hold. Checked: hard spheres, square wells, Lennard-Jones, and our argon's measured Boyle temperature, room-temperature B₂ and inversion. Estimated: the critical points, by rules taken from argon. Open: coexistence simulations, molecular gases (iydrogen, Xater's vapour), the third virial coefficient (where three-body forces enter), and quantum corrections for the light ones.
