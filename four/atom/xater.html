Pxygen and Xater

Element 10 and its water, computed by a molecular solver written for any dimension and checked on our own water: Xater's shape, its pull, and its hydrogen bond.

Pxygen, element 10

4D's second row holds ten electrons: two in 2s, then eight in 2p, which has four orbitals here. Element 10, Pxygen, has six of the eight: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, two short of the noble gas at 12, as our oxygen is two short of neon. Like oxygen it keeps its spare electrons apart, four spinning one way and two the other (Hund's rule), 1.7 eV below pairing them all off.

It holds its outermost electron with 13.1 eV (the solver's figure; for our oxygen it gives 12.6, measured 13.6). What matters more is its partner. In our world hydrogen holds its electron with 13.6 eV, just as hard as oxygen, but 4D hydrogen holds on with only 6.05. So in a bond between them Pxygen takes more than its share. Its bonds to hydrogen are lopsided, with the hydrogen at the positive end, far more so than ours.

Counting: the outer shell has five orbitals (2s and four 2p) and eight electrons. Two orbitals are half full and make bonds, and three are full. Pxygen makes two bonds and keeps three lone pairs. Darbon's geometry would put the two bonds 104.48° apart, almost exactly our water's 104.5°. The computation below says otherwise.

Molecules, computed

The atom solver can't do molecules: it averages each atom into a sphere. So ATOM⁴ has a second solver, Hartree–Fock on Gaussian functions, written once for any number of dimensions. Every integral a molecule needs comes down to one function. In 3D that's Boys's F_n(T). In d dimensions it becomes G_n(T) = ∫₀¹ u²ⁿ (1 − u²)^((d−3)/2) e^(−Tu²) du, which obeys the same rules, so the rest of the textbook method carries over unchanged.

The checks. Hydrogen comes out exact in both worlds, 4D's 2p level too. Helium reaches the method's known limit in 3D. In 4D it equals the atom solver's helium to a millionth: for two electrons the two solvers are the same theory, computed differently. A 4D molecule's energy doesn't change when it points another way. The solver lands where Hartree–Fock is known to land, and misses what Hartree–Fock is known to miss: about a third of the energy holding water together, which is correlation. It gets shapes and dipoles well and underestimates bond energies, the same way in both worlds. So compare Xater with our water as computed, not as measured.

Xater, H₂Px

Xater is bent at 120°, wider than our water (computed 106.3°) and than the 104.48° the counting suggested. A bigger basis moves it by under a degree. Its bonds are 2.79 bohr long, 1.57× ours, because 4D's atoms are bigger. And it's soft: straightening it costs 0.21 eV, against water's 1.42. That's still about 8 times room temperature's 0.026 eV, so it stays bent, but it flexes far more than water does. Its bonds hold less: pulling Xater apart into atoms takes 4.3 eV, against water's 6.8 by the same method, because 4D hydrogen has less of a hold to share.

Its dipole moment is 1.64 au, 2.1× our water's computed the same way: that's the lopsided bond. A dipole's field here is the same as in our world, because the potential is 1/r. So two Xaters pull on each other as μ²/R³ just as two waters do, but 4.5 times harder.

Its lone pairs make a ring. The top orbital is two-fold: two 2p orbitals at right angles to the molecule's plane, equal in energy. In 3D the directions at right angles to a plane form a line, and water's lone pair there is one orbital with two lobes. In 4D they form a whole plane, and a turn within that plane moves nothing in the molecule. So Xater's charge sits in a ring around it, and a neighbour can come in from anywhere around that ring.

The hydrogen bond

A hydrogen of one Xater points at the Pxygen of another, coming in from the ring's side. Computed with the pair's shape found by minimizing (and corrected for each molecule borrowing the other's basis functions): the two Pxygens sit 6.5 bohr apart, and the bond is worth 13.3 kcal/mol, 3.1× water's 4.3 by the same method. At this basis Hartree–Fock gets water's bond about 21% too strong and misses its correlation, so read the ratio and not the number: Xater's hydrogen bond is about three times as strong as water's.

It's short, too. The shared hydrogen is 1.3 times as far from its neighbour's Pxygen as from its own; in water's pair, 2.2 times. Xater's hydrogen bonds are halfway to being shared, like the strongest hydrogen bonds in our world (the F–H–F of salts), not like water's.

What kind of liquid

Each Xater has two hydrogens to give and a ring to take them, so it can link into a network as water does, two hydrogen bonds per molecule. With each bond about three times water's, pulling a Xater out of its liquid should cost two to three times as much, which makes it a liquid far past our water's boiling point. That's an estimate from the bond, not a computation of the liquid, and it's low: three Xater in a ring hold about a quarter harder than their three pairs, by Hartree–Fock (chapter 13), where our water's gain is under a tenth.

Open: correlation, the part the method misses, is counted for the pair in the census (MP2): it weakens Xater's pair a little. The liquid is simulated in the next section. How many hydrogens the ring will take: it has room for more than two, and three or more molecules have to be computed to know. And the refractive index, which the rainbow needs. Whether Xater is 4D's water at all is the next chapter's question.

The liquid, simulated

computed Liquid and vapour side by side, trading molecules and volume until they agree (the Gibbs ensemble, chapter 14): 300 rigid Xaters on the force field fitted to Hartree–Fock pairs, each box with its full charge sum (4D needs it: a cut-off sum is off by 42 kcal/mol a molecule, methods). On that field Xater is a liquid from at most 1,500 K to a critical point near 9,200 K (7,900–9,200 K, by which critical exponent is assumed; one seed a temperature), and its liquid holds each molecule with 117 kcal/mol, about eleven times our water's 10.5 (measured). The estimate from the bond above, two to three times, was far too low: in 4D most of a liquid's hold comes from beyond its nearest neighbours, as for the noble gases (chapter 14). Its crowd adds nothing measurable: summed over every triple in the 1,000 K liquid, three-body forces come to −10 ± 19 kcal/mol a molecule, where our water's, counted the same way, add 16% to its liquid's hold at 450 K.

The check, our water's own model (SPC/E) at room temperature by the same code: 3.3×10−9 m²/s against the published 2.4×10−9, about 30% fast. Below 1,500 K the runs start from the 1,500 K liquid at its density, so they are cooled at constant volume.

At 1,500 K Xater flows like our water at room temperature. At 1,000 K and below it doesn't: each molecule moves less than its spacing (about 4.4 bohr) in 27 ps and its wander levels off, rattling in the cage of its neighbours. On this field the liquid jams between 1,000 and 1,500 K. That's where a cooled liquid stops, not its melting point, which can sit higher: the melting point needs the crystal heated beside the cooled liquid (open).

How far to trust 9,000 K. The simulation is checked: our water's own model gives 631 ± 2 K against the published 640. The field is the doubt. Our water on the same kind of field, fitted to Hartree–Fock pairs, condenses below only 250 K, against 647 measured: Hartree–Fock misses correlation (dispersion) and the crowd's polarization, which hold our water's liquid together. If Xater's field fell short by the same factor its critical point would be near 23,700 K. But the two things water's field misses needn't carry over. At the pair's best arrangement correlation adds −0.87 kcal/mol to our water's −3.7 (a fifth; standard large basis), and between −0.45 and +0.48 to Xater's −26, by basis: nothing, within the basis. And our water's crowd adds about a sixth to its liquid's hold, where Xater's adds nothing measurable. open That last bar is wide, ±19 kcal/mol from 48 triples: 128 more are running. A field fitted to correlated (MP2) pairs at every distance, as done for our OF₂ and 4D's PxGl₂, would check the far pull too. Until then: Xater is a liquid over thousands of kelvin, jams below about 1,000–1,500 K, and its critical point is 9,000 K on its Hartree–Fock field, so liquid Xater needs a hot world (the open questions).
