Ten ways to meet

How the census first missed the strongest pairs, and what the miss shows: two molecules meet in six ways in our world and ten in 4D, and a crowd of them in far more. Watch the search, and see what it can and can't promise.

The miss

Chapter 12 first reported that a pair of Xater molecules binds at 13.3 kcal/mol. A liquid run on a force field fitted to Hartree–Fock pairs then held pairs together harder than that. Searched properly, the pair binds at 27.4: twice as hard, and in a different shape. Nothing was wrong with the arithmetic. The census had only looked in one place. It slid one molecule along a line and tilted it in one plane, two numbers, inside the family of arrangements our water's hydrogen bond makes. That family was our world's intuition, carried into a world where it doesn't hold.

This chapter is about that miss, because it holds a finding. Molecules have many more ways to meet in four dimensions than in three. Any search that stops looking early can miss the answer, and in 4D there is far more room to miss it in.

Counting the ways

To place one rigid molecule against another you move it, one number for each direction, and turn it, one number for each plane of turning (chapter 4). In d dimensions that's d moves and d(d − 1)/2 turns. Moving grows like d and turning like d², so as dimensions are added, turning takes over:

A rigid molecule's pose in d dimensions takes d(d + 1)/2 numbers; a pair's relative pose takes the same, and N molecules take N − 1 times as many. Bendable molecules add their own shapes on top.

Our water pair has six numbers to get right. Xater's has ten, and six of those are turns. A search that checks ten values along each number checks a million arrangements in our world and ten billion in 4D. Nobody searches that way; a descent from a random start follows the slope instead. But it shows how thin a two-number slice is: the census looked at a sheet through a ten-dimensional space.

One pair: a funnel

For a single pair, the landscape is kind. Of 400 random starts on Xater's force field, all 400 slid into the same arrangement, the compact antiparallel pair of chapter 12. Of 400 for our water, 378 found its one linear hydrogen bond, and the rest drifted apart or ran out of steps. Each pair's landscape is a funnel: one minimum, with every slope leading to it. For our water that's the known answer. Of the ten stationary points mapped on the water pair's surface, only the linear pair is a minimum; the others are passes between copies of it.

So the miss wasn't the landscape being hard. Any descent over all ten numbers, started almost anywhere, would have found the deeper pair. The miss was choosing the slice. Our first full search made a related mistake, and it is worth saying plainly. It counted descents that stalled before the bottom as separate minima. At a turn wound past many radians, the numbers describing it get badly conditioned and the descent crawls. Six "minima" for Xater were one minimum reached six times, not quite all the way. The descent now re-centres its turn as it goes and stops only when the slope is gone. Chapter 12's energies stand, because each was rescored and relaxed by Hartree–Fock itself.

Many molecules: a maze

A pair is the easy case. Add molecules and the minima multiply. Quench a liquid's arrangement to its nearest minimum, the trick behind settling every start above, and the number of distinct minima grows exponentially with the number of molecules. That is the inherent-structure picture of liquids and glasses. Three molecules already have 12 numbers in our world and 20 in 4D; seven have 36 and 60.

So we counted. Clusters of three to seven molecules were searched from random starts on each world's force field, with 2400 starts for six and 1600 starts for seven, the same in every world; three, four, five had the first count's 500 to 1000. Every start descended until its slope was gone, and the distinct minima were tallied.

Solid lines: minima found, where two are the same if their energies agree within 0.1 kcal/mol and their heavy atoms' distances within 0.1 bohr. The bar under each point runs down to the count when minima within 0.3 kcal/mol and 0.5 bohr are merged. Dashed: the Chao1 estimate of how many there are, from how many were found only once or twice, drawn only where fewer than half were seen just once (beyond that it's a guess bigger than anything measured, and the table says "still climbing"). The force field is fitted to pairs and adds them up; it has no three-body forces.

Both worlds' minima multiply, and 4D's much faster. From two molecules to seven, our water's minima go 1, 5, 5–7, 8–9, 37–45, 138–152; Xater's go 1, 2–3, 8–10, 20–25, 148–268, 342–765, and element 9's XH₃'s 1, 2, 14–16, 64–98, 348–1022, 540–1505. Where two numbers are given, the true count lies between them. The larger tells apart arrangements of the same energy by their shape, which can split one soft minimum in two. The smaller merges any two within a test three times looser, which can merge two different ones. From four molecules on, even 4D's smaller counts are above our water's larger ones. The best arrangement gets harder to find as well. Of the starts for six molecules, 8% found our water's best, 6% Xater's and 2% XH₃'s; for seven, 2%, 0.9% and one start in 1600.

None of this is the whole count. Only our water's six has nearly settled: the second half of its starts added 4 new minima. Everywhere else the discovery curve is still climbing. For seven molecules the second half of the starts added 28 new minima for our water, 266 for Xater and 733 for XH₃, where 1425 of its 1505 minima were seen just once. There the best one found, seen only once, is not shown to be the best there is. The clusters also hold together far harder in 4D: the best of seven binds at 5.1 kcal/mol per molecule for our water and 34.1 for Xater. These are the force field's energies, not Hartree–Fock's, and the force field has no three-body forces, which in 4D turns out to matter a great deal (below).

Watch five at once

Here five molecules do it live, on the same force field. Each start drops them at random in a ball and lets them slide downhill together until nothing pushes any more; then the next start. Start s here is the runner's start s, with the same seed and the same descent, so where it ends can be named from the runner's list. Five waters search 24 numbers and five Xaters 40. Of the runner's 600 starts with five waters, 75% found the best arrangement; of its 500 with five Xaters, 26%.

Each molecule's heavy atom has its own colour, so you can follow it. "watch" runs a descent at an easy pace; "race" runs as fast as your device allows. Energies are the force field's, for the whole cluster, in kcal/mol. A tab that isn't showing pauses.

For a liquid this hardly matters: a liquid visits a great many minima, and the simulation samples them rather than finding one. It matters for the solid. Which crystal a 4D water freezes into is a search for one best arrangement among very many, and on this evidence 4D's search space is far larger than ours. That's where the extra turns will bite.

It may also shape the freezing itself. A liquid with more minima to visit has more entropy, and a crystal freezes where its energy gain pays for that entropy (T = ΔH/ΔS), so more minima push the freezing point down, and many nearly equal crystals make freezing slow, grainy and glassy. But 4D's clusters bind several times harder, which pushes it up. The prediction, for the liquid simulation to test: a freezing point low for its cohesion, and a liquid that would rather be a glass. That reasoning rests on the pairwise force field's clusters, and the audit below takes those apart for 4D, so it's reopened, not tested.

Three at once: the force field audited computed

Everything on this page's clusters was done on a force field that adds molecules up in pairs. Our water nearly works that way: three of its molecules hold a little harder than their three pairs, by under a tenth. Whether 4D's do was never checked. So we checked, with Hartree–Fock on whole trimers, every energy in the trimer's own basis so the three-body part comes out of the calculation rather than being assumed.

In 4D, a ring of three holds far harder than its pairs. The three-body part is 23% of a Xater ring's binding and 42% of an XH₃ ring's, against 8% for three of our water's molecules from its ice. That is cooperativity: each bond pulls charge across its molecules and strengthens the next. 4D's molecules are big and soft (Xater twice as polarizable as our water, XH₃ four times), and a force field built from pairs can't see it at all. So the cluster energies above, and which cluster is best, are that field's answers for 4D, not Hartree–Fock's.

The same field has two more faults in 4D. It binds each 4D best pair too weakly: Xater's at −23.4 against Hartree–Fock's −27.4 kcal/mol, XH₃'s at −23.3 against −27.5. And it binds crowded pairs too strongly: of 24 close contacts taken from crystals and scored by Hartree–Fock, it makes 19 too attractive, by up to 15 kcal/mol.

A better field, and how far it gets. We built a polarizable one from each molecule alone: charges fitted to its own Hartree–Fock electrostatic potential, sites that polarize as its Hartree–Fock polarizability says, and only the overlap repulsion fitted to pairs. In 4D charges alone can't reproduce a molecule's potential: they miss it by half. The reason is 4D's own. Under the pinned 1/r a round electron cloud isn't seen from outside as a point (the shell theorem fails, chapter 2), so each atom adds a potential of its own, falling as 1/r³. With that term on each atom the fit comes within 7–11%, as close as charges get for our water. This field gets the rings' cooperativity right in sign and rough size, but overshoots it (three-body −25.2 for the Xater ring, −66.5 for XH₃'s): its damping between molecules is borrowed from our world's molecules and is too weak for 4D's. The next step is to fit that one number to the trimers.

Which crystal? computed

Searching for a crystal means relaxing molecules and the cell's shape together from random starts, many times, and keeping the lowest, the way crystal structures of our molecules are predicted. The known answer comes first. Our water's ice, built by hand on its diamond net and relaxed on the pairwise field, settles at −7.5 kcal/mol per molecule with each molecule's 4 neighbours hydrogen-bonded, oxygens 5.74 bohr apart. That's looser than real ice (5.20 bohr), as a field fitted to Hartree–Fock's weaker hydrogen bond should make it. Random starts find it without being shown it. From 300 random cells of 2 molecules, the lowest crystal found is it (−7.5), and all of the 8 lowest are hydrogen-bonded nets with every molecule giving two bonds. From 300 random cells of 4 molecules, the lowest crystal found is it (−7.5), and all of the 8 lowest are hydrogen-bonded nets with every molecule giving two bonds.

4D's waters don't pass yet. On the pairwise field Xater and XH₃ collapse into crystals packed far tighter than their molecules' size allows. On the polarizable field they still pack densely, each molecule touching thirty or more. Hartree–Fock disagrees with both. Over every pair of molecules within 10 bohr in the polarizable field's densest Xater crystal (102 pairs), Hartree–Fock's energies add to +6.0 kcal/mol per molecule, repulsive, where the pairwise field said −25.0. Trimers cut from those crystals have small three-body parts (−1.9 to +1.5), so the crowd doesn't rescue them. So which crystal a 4D water makes is open, waiting on a field fitted to the rings and these crowded pairs at once.

What a search can promise

A search gives one guarantee. The energy it finds is one that some arrangement really has, so the true best can only be lower. Every binding energy in these chapters is a floor: more searching can only make it stronger. Doubling Xater's pair didn't break the census's conclusion that element 9's XH₃ and Xater tie as the water. It made both stronger.

What a search can't promise is that it's finished. That has to be argued separately, by these checks:

  • Known answers. The same search, run on our world's pairs whose best arrangements are published (water, hydrogen fluoride, ammonia), must find them.
  • The discovery curve. Starts are added until new basins stop appearing, and the count of basins found only once estimates how many remain unseen.
  • The force field's blind spots. The force field is a smooth fit, and it can blur two of Hartree–Fock's minima into one. So Hartree–Fock itself is relaxed from random placements, without the force field's guidance.
  • The other searches in this position. The census's shapes were built symmetric and then nudged, which shows each is a minimum, not the minimum. The table's electron configurations were chosen among candidates. Each gets the same audit.

The audit, run

On a bigger machine (the compute runner), the first three checks were run.

Known answers: all three found, the third only after a fix. For our ammonia pair the full search finds the best arrangement the census's scan had, 2.18 kcal/mol with the nitrogens 3.49 Å apart. (The real ammonia pair is famously floppy, its hydrogen bond barely holding a shape. Hartree–Fock on a small basis gives a definite one.) For our water it finds the linear hydrogen bond at 4.35 kcal/mol, level with the census's carefully relaxed pair (4.35).

How we got it wrong: hydrogen fluoride. The first audit failed here. The force field's best arrangement is a straight chain, F–H···F–H, and the relaxation then used, small turns along one pose number at a time, never left the line: it stopped at 3.77 kcal/mol, short of the census's bent pair (4.05) and of the measured structure, which is bent. The straight chain isn't a minimum but a saddle. The way down bends the chain while swinging the partner round, a combination of pose numbers, none of them downhill alone, and so gentle that its curvature sits near Hartree–Fock's own noise.

So the relaxation was rebuilt: it measures the whole curvature, steps off a saddle along its downhill direction, and at an apparent bottom probes up to 1.5 radians along every principal direction. From the straight chain it now walks off in six steps and down to 4.10 kcal/mol, bent: the donor's F–H···F nearly straight (171°), the acceptor's F···F–H at 127°, the fluorines 2.83 Å apart (measured: about 170°, 117° and 2.72 Å; Hartree–Fock runs a little long and open here). The census's own pair, relaxed the same way, lands on the same minimum. A search goes where its descent can take it, so the descent is part of what has to be checked.

Hartree–Fock without the force field: nothing deeper. Two molecules were placed at random in close contact and scored by Hartree–Fock itself: 600 placements for our water, 2,400 for Xater, 1,600 for XH₃. None came out deeper than the best pair. The deepest reached 3.5 of water's 4.3 kcal/mol, 24.2 of Xater's 26.9, and 20.1 of XH₃'s 27.5. On the attractive placements the force field is off by 0.5 kcal/mol (rms) for our water and 2.6 and 2.7 for the 4D pair. On single placements it misses up to 12 kcal/mol of the pull, so it does have blind spots in 4D. None hid a deeper pair among these placements.

The census re-baked with the corrected descent: it stands. The best pairs come out at 26.94 and 27.54 kcal/mol, as before. The force field's six "distinct" minima per molecule, which the faulty descent had reported, collapse to one each: the funnel.

The new descent, run on all three. For our water and XH₃ it confirms the best pairs (4.35 and 27.54 kcal/mol). For Xater it set off from the 26.94 pair and, over two runs' time, relaxed it to 27.35 kcal/mol, where it converged: the same arrangement, the two Xs moved from 2.58 to 2.61 Å apart. The tie holds and narrows: XH₃ is ahead by 0.19 kcal/mol, where it led by 0.61. Both gaps are far smaller than what Hartree–Fock leaves out (correlation), so on chemistry the two still tie.

The deepest random placements, relaxed. Water's four deepest ended in the best pair or in shallower minima, never deeper. For the 4D pairs each step of the descent costs over half an hour of Hartree–Fock; their four deepest each ran up to four rounds of five and a half hours. Of XH₃'s, three ended in the best pair (27.54 kcal/mol) and one more is at it, the gradient almost gone; of Xater's, two ended in the best pair (27.35 kcal/mol). The other two are still descending, 0.7 to 1.4 kcal/mol above the best pair and falling by 0.01 to 0.03 a step (Xater's at 26.63, 25.91); their energies are cached, so another round picks up where they stopped. None has gone deeper than the best pair.

So far no check has found a different, deeper arrangement than the 4D best pairs, and hydrogen fluoride is found. But the lesson stands: random placements are thin in ten dimensions, and a search goes where its force field and its descent lead. Still open: the 4D relaxations above, longer cluster searches where the counts haven't settled, giving the force field the lone pairs' directions, and the other searches in this position.
