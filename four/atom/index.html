ATOM⁴

The universe we're building: four dimensions of space, and the force between charges pinned at 1/r. Its consequences, worked out from there and computed wherever they can be. A selftest checks every number. This page is the position. Each chapter shows the working. As in Lumen⁴, 3D is warm and 4D is ice.

New here? Where we are walks the argument in eleven steps, with toys, before the full working below.

What it's made of: electrons (spin-½ fermions), nuclei (heavy point charges, declared stable up to Z = 78; what that takes is chapter 15), the lens vacuum's light, a small fine-structure constant like ours, and the weak force with its neutrino, added so stars can burn hydrogen. Nothing else, and gravity is open. Bulk matter is stable for the same reason ours is, that electrons are fermions: argued from scaling, since the proof for our world leans on the shell theorem. The full list.

1 · Geometry

  • Spheres grow faster. A 4D sphere's surface grows as r³, so anything spreading from a point (light, a field, heat) thins as 1/r³: ten times farther, a thousandth as much. That one fact forces the next chapter's choice, dims 4D starlight, and gives every 4D atom a crowd of neighbours.
  • More room at right angles. Random directions lean toward perpendicular, so aiming is harder: a 30° cone catches 2.9% of directions (ours 6.7%). 24 balls can touch one (ours 12), yet the densest packing fills 62% (ours 74%).
  • Two planes meet at a point, and can be completely at right angles: xy and zw share only the origin. Turning happens in planes, six of them, and a turn can leave no line in place.
  • Lost walkers stay lost: a random walker comes home 19% of the time in 4D (ours 34%), so diffusing molecules meet again less often. And the ways to move fast grow as v³, which gives each atom 2kT and light its T⁵.
  • The 3-sphere can be combed: Hopf's circles fill it without meeting. The hairy-ball theorem, a reason our nebulae flatten into disks, has no 4D version. A torus can be flat, knots untie, and a glove turns over through w.

2 · The force

  • Gauss's law in 4D gives a 1/r² potential, and under it no atom has a ground state. We pin the potential at 1/r.
  • The universe holds electrons (fermions), stable nuclei, the lens vacuum's light, a small fine-structure constant, and the weak force (added for the stars), and nothing else. Matter is stable, by scaling, because electrons are fermions.
  • Maxwell's 1/k² can't give 1/r in 4D: it needs 1/k³. Every way to get it costs one of locality, linearity, relativity, positive probabilities, or four large dimensions.
  • Our vacuum is the lens vacuum, ε(k) = ε₀ℓk and μ(k) = μ₀/ℓk. Light stays Maxwell's, fields still add, energy stays positive. The cost is relativity for the force: there's an absolute now.
  • 1/r isn't harmonic in 4D, so there's no shell theorem and no Faraday cage. A charged shell's centre sits at 3π/8 of its wall's potential.
  • The other way out is to change the electron: an energy growing as p³ or p⁴ makes squeezing cost more than Gauss's pull and gives atoms back, keeping the Faraday cage. It costs relativity for all matter (F = ma goes), where the lens costs it only for the force. Argued, not computed.

3 · Light

  • The light that travels is Lumen⁴'s, exactly: 1/r³, three polarizations, the wake, 1.43 λ/D, ν⁴ and T⁵, the rainbow's conics.
  • Matter feels light through the lens, c/ℓω: long waves push harder, and a flash is felt faintly before it arrives.
  • Small particles scatter as ω³, so the sky is blue, though less steeply than ours.

4 · Motion and spin

  • F = ma. Orbits lie in a plane, and under 1/r they're Kepler's closed ellipses. Under Gauss's force none is stable.
  • Turns happen in planes: six components, double rotations, left and right isoclinic halves (SU(2) × SU(2)).
  • Electrons pair up, two to an orbital, and their spin is handed: it turns with the left half of every rotation and ignores the right. Only half of a magnetic field can flip it.
  • Atoms in a magnet are handed. Every atom is pushed out of a magnet (diamagnetism), but a spin is drawn in by the field's left half only, so 66 of 78 atoms are drawn into a magnet and pushed out of its mirror image.
  • Rigid bodies tumble by Euler's equations in six components, still exactly solvable. Every single bond twists in three angles.

5 · Gravity

  • Gravity meets charge's fork. Under Gauss's law in 4D no orbit is stable (a circle given 1% more speed runs away) and a star of gas is exactly marginal. Pinned at 1/r, orbits are Kepler's closed ellipses again.
  • Gravity's routes mirror charge's: Gauss (no orbits, marginal stars), a nonlocal or ghostly field, a dispersive vacuum, a nonlinear law (keeps the shell theorem, loses superposition: masses don't add), a small extra dimension, or the lens. The lens is assumed, for symmetry with charge. Not yet chosen.

6 · Hydrogen

  • E = −Z²/2(n + ½)²: bound by 6.05 eV, twice our size. Shells hold 2, 10, 28, 60.
  • Its fall to n = 2 glows red to orange, ending at 570 nm. Fine structure splits each l into (l + 1)(l + 2) and l(l + 1).

7 · The periodic table

  • Rows of 2, 10, 10, 28, 28. Noble gases at 2, 12, 22, 50, 78. A ten-electron rule. That's what the generalized Madelung rule predicts for 4D, here confirmed by solving the atoms.
  • Inner electrons screen only partly, so 4D's noble gases hold on about twice as hard as they would with the shell theorem, and a neutral atom still pulls from outside (−Σr²/8s³).
  • The solver (with exchange) gets 32 of our first 36 elements right, chromium and copper included, and ionization within a few percent. 4D's transition metals lean hard toward d and are its least sure entries.
  • 54 of the 78 do what one of ours does and take its name, a letter on: iydrogen, darbon, pxygen, oitrogen. 24 are new, among them elements 6 and 8 on either side of darbon. The big table has them all.

10 · Forces between molecules

  • Neutral atoms attract permanently, as −3 S_A S_B / 64 R⁵ (S = Σ⟨r²⟩). For element 12 at neon's spacing that's about 14 times neon's whole well.
  • Dispersion stays −C₆/R⁶ (London's 3/2 becomes 7/4), computed by coupled response, within 11% on our noble gases. In 4D it's weaker than the permanent pull for every noble gas but the first.
  • Computed end to end, 4D noble-gas pairs from element 12 on sit 4.5 to 5.25 bohr apart. Simulated, element 12 condenses below 4,700 to 8,900 K: they would hardly be gases.
  • Bond springs and angles carry over. A bond twists three ways, and staggered means inverted through the bond.
  • Salt water screens by a power law: an ion is overscreened, with a −r⁻⁷ tail.

11 · Pxygen and Xater

  • Pxygen, element 10: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, two short of a noble gas, so two bonds and three lone pairs. 4D hydrogen holds its electron with 6.05 eV, against Pxygen's 13.1, so their bond is lopsided.
  • Xater, H₂Px, is bent at about 120°, not water's 104.5°, and soft: straightening it costs 0.21 eV (water 1.42). Its lone pairs make a ring around the molecule's plane.
  • Its dipole is 2.1× water's and its hydrogen bond 3.1× as strong, by a Hartree–Fock solver written for any dimension and checked on our water. Its hydrogen bonds are short, halfway to shared, and its liquid should hold together far past our water's boiling point.

12 · Which water

  • Almost every 4D hydride has positive hydrogen: iydrogen holds its electron with only 6.05 eV. Mithium and todium are the exceptions (Mi⁺H⁻, like our LiH).
  • Every hydride of rows 2 and 3 computed: their shapes are what counting says, except that row 2's XH₄ comes out flat, with no dipole.
  • On chemistry, element 9's XH₃ comes first, then Xater, searched over every arrangement and counting correlation: at Hartree–Fock the two tie, each binding a pair at about 28 kcal/mol, 6 times our water's, and correlation (MP2) puts XH₃ ahead by 2.4 kcal/mol in the largest basis tried, and by 2.2 to 2.7 in every basis. Their best pairs lie antiparallel, dipole to dipole. Which fills the oceans is the abundance question, still open.

13 · Ten ways to meet

  • Two molecules meet in six ways in our world and ten in 4D: d moves and d(d − 1)/2 turns. Turning grows as d², so in 4D most of the ways are turns.
  • Chapter 12 first searched two of Xater's ten numbers and missed its best pair by a factor of two. Searched over all ten, a pair's landscape is a funnel: every one of 400 random starts finds the same pair.
  • A search's energy is a floor: more searching can only bind harder. Whether it's finished has to be argued separately, and the audit has run. Our ammonia's, water's and hydrogen fluoride's pairs are found, hydrogen fluoride's bent one only after the descent learned to step off saddles: the first stopped on a straight chain. 4,600 random Hartree–Fock placements found nothing deeper than the best pairs, and chapter 12 stands.
  • Crowds are a maze, and more so in 4D: from two molecules to seven, our water's minima go 1, 5, 5–7, 8–9, 37–45, 138–152 and Xater's 1, 2–3, 8–10, 20–25, 148–268, 342–765 on a pairwise force field, and the 4D counts haven't stopped climbing.
  • In 4D a crowd doesn't add up in pairs. By Hartree–Fock on whole trimers, three Xater in a ring hold 23% of their binding beyond their pairs and three XH₃ 42%, against 8% for our water's. So the pairwise field's 4D clusters stand only as its answers, and the prediction made from them is reopened.
  • Our water's ice is found blind; 4D's ice is open. Random cells find four-connected ice as the lowest crystal. Both force fields pack 4D's waters into dense crystals that Hartree–Fock rejects: summed over 10 bohr, the densest's pairs repel by +6.0 kcal/mol per molecule.

14 · Gases

  • PV = NkT holds in any dimension. A 4D atom carries 2kT, γ = 3/2, and sound runs at 0.61 of the rms speed (ours 0.75).
  • 4D's noble gases are held by the crowd. R⁻⁵ over r³ of space adds up far out: element 12's 24 nearest neighbours give 31% of its crystal's hold, argon's 12 give 80%. Its Boyle temperature is 28 times its well depth (ours 2.8).
  • A throttle cools them up to tens of thousands of kelvin: element 12 below 31,000 to 56,000 K, against argon's 794 K. Even 4D's helium cools at room temperature, so a 4D liquefier needs no precooling. Counted by the whole pull, not the well, the gas is ours rescaled.
  • Element 12 condenses below 4,700 to 8,900 K, simulated (liquid and vapour side by side, checked on Lennard-Jones atoms and our argon). That's where the crowd's rules put it, 6 times the well-depth rule. Element 2 is the one 4D noble gas that might be a gas at room temperature.
  • A hypothesis: a world of liquids. Gas only when hot, wide liquid ranges, glass more often than crystal: element 12's two best crystals nearly tie. Salts are the exception. Computed end to end, 4D's todium gluoride holds together 1.4 times as hard as our sodium fluoride, which the same method gets to 5%.

15 · Nuclei

  • 4D needs a stronger strong force. With ours its deuteron isn't bound (a head-on meeting in 4D carries a barrier), so stars couldn't burn. Two quite different forces agree it must be 23% stronger at least.
  • Helium-4 holds 19–26 MeV per nucleon where the deuteron binds as ours, about three times ours: solved exactly, both forces, checked on our helium-4.
  • Nuclear matter is held by the crowd, as 4D's gases are: 67–90 MeV per nucleon where the model holds (sound under half light speed), against our 16; deeper wells are past it. Big nuclei beat helium by a wider margin than ours do.
  • No gap at mass 8: two helium-4s bind, so helium burns as hydrogen does, with no triple-alpha luck. A periodic table could run to about element 232 (ours about 170) before the innermost electron sinks.
  • The ladder has no gap: every 4D helium-4 capture is downhill, by 7–1372 MeV (ours 5 to 9), more at each rung, checked on our own ladder. Past carbon or oxygen a 4D nucleus is a drop, not helium-4s. How far stars build, and so which element is commonest, is set by how hot their cores get: open.

16 · Stars

  • Pinned at 1/r through the lens, like charge, a 4D star of gas is bound with E = W/2, exactly as ours is, and a star of light is marginal in both worlds: a maximum stellar mass, as we have.
  • Solved, a 4D star is 14 times as dense at its centre as on average (ours 6). With no shell theorem its outer layers press inward: a quarter of the way out, 59% of the pull is from the mass outside.
  • A 4D head-on meeting carries a centrifugal barrier: at the same temperature, nuclei tunnel 15 to 56 times less often. A core makes that up by running hotter or denser (up to about 2.2× hotter if by temperature alone). Not a different regime.
  • The weak force is added, so stars can burn hydrogen and live billions of years. Without it they'd burn leftover deuterium and shine by contracting, for millions.
  • 4D has a Chandrasekhar mass, solved (1.52 α_G^(−4/3) nucleons). G is set by a requirement: with ours, 4D matter is so dense per 4-volume that a world holding air crushes at 50,000 g. Planets like ours need G about 10⁻¹⁷ to 9 × 10⁻¹⁷ of ours, and then stars come out Sun-sized.

17 · Planetary systems

  • Planets orbit as Kepler said, with one twist: a round 4D star doesn't pull like a point, so orbits slowly turn back (about 3 arcseconds per orbit at Earth's distance; zero under the nonlinear law). Two planets can share an orbit's size in orthogonal planes, never closer than √2: in one plane, radii 1 and 1.2 throw one out within 2 orbits. The planes turn through 4D, keeping their angle. Over 100,000 orbits, planes turned equally both ways keep planets safe; tilted our way, about a shared line, they're thrown out: at Jupiter's mass every pair tilted 45° or more.
  • A 4D nebula flattens only if it spins in one plane, simulated against our world's: spinning in two planes it stops part-way, at equal rates it stays round, and a typical cloud ends a thick lens, about 35% as thick as wide. Its planets would form in many planes, not one.
  • Every planet has one safe plane for moons: the one completely orthogonal to its orbit, where the star can't tug. A polar moon, tilted about a shared line as ours can be, is thrown off within 3 orbits.
  • A tidally locked 4D planet keeps turning, and never sees a sunset. Tides lock the spin in the orbit's plane and can't touch the turning in the plane completely orthogonal to it, which moves nothing the star lights: a fixed day side and night side, under a sky that still wheels. Shown with our world's tidal model and with a planet of springs and dampers that knows nothing of it.
  • A planet's temperature falls as r−3/5 (ours r−1/2). Round a star as hot as our Sun, Earth-warm sits at about half Earth's distance in star radii, and a liquid's band of orbits is narrower, but more planets can share it in planes turned apart.

Open questions

  • Open-shell atoms' response. Polarizabilities are computed for hydrogen and the noble gases. Pxygen, darbon and the rest have open shells, which the response doesn't handle yet. Also open: unlike pairs, retardation and three-body forces.
  • Which nuclei exist. Stability is declared for Z up to 78. Deriving it would take the strong force in 4D.
  • Gravity. Under Gauss's law no orbit is stable and a star is only marginal, so gravity has to be pinned too. Its routes mirror charge's (chapter 5). Through the same lens, stars hold together as ours do. A nonlinear law would keep the shell theorem but make masses not add. Which to adopt is open.
  • Correlation. The atom solver has exchange but not correlation, and averages open shells into spheres. 4D's transition metals are the least sure. The molecular solver is Hartree–Fock, which misses about a third of a bond's energy, in both worlds.
  • Spin and statistics. Electrons pairing up is taken as part of the universe's definition. The theorem that would derive it assumes full relativity, which the lens vacuum gives up.
  • A world of liquids? 4D's noble gases are held by the crowd: element 12 condenses below 4,700 to 8,900 K, simulated (chapter 14), thousands of kelvin above what its well suggests. Still open: whether other liquids follow (wide liquid ranges, few gases at room temperature), whether cold matter crystallizes or freezes to glass, and the crystals of salts, the likely exception, such as bluminium pxide, Bl₂Px₃, which could be 4D's sapphire. A hypothesis, partly simulated.
  • Which crystal a 4D water makes. Our water's ice is found from random starts; 4D's waters can't be, yet: a pairwise force field misses their rings' cooperativity (a quarter to two-fifths of a ring's binding, by Hartree–Fock) and crowds them too close. A polarizable field fitted to Hartree–Fock trimers now gets the rings within a quarter, and its crystal search is under way, its crowded crystals still to be checked against Hartree–Fock (chapter 13).
  • Which water fills the oceans. On chemistry element 9's XH₃ leads Xater by about 9% once correlation is counted, in every basis tried. Which is commoner needs to know which elements stars make: chapter 15 shows every rung of the helium-4 ladder is downhill, with element 10 on it and element 9 off it, which leans toward Xater; how far stars climb is open. The liquids themselves (structure, boiling points, the mixture), and their refractive indices (the rainbow's 42° is our water's), wait on simulation.
