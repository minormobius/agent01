How it's computed

The solvers behind every chapter, what each was checked against, the compute they ran on, and how to run them yourself.

How this book is made

Everything in these chapters is computed by code anyone can read, and most of it can be re-run in a browser. Six rules make that hold:

  1. Our world is the control. Every solver is written for any number of dimensions and run on ours first, against measured or published answers. Only then is it run in four.
  2. No dependencies. Plain JavaScript modules, the same ones in a browser and in node. The live views on these pages run the same code that produced the numbers.
  3. Every number comes from the code. The calculations write their results to data files, and the pages are generated from those files: nothing on a page is typed by hand.
  4. Everything stated is checked. A self-test of 49 groups of checks, about a minute and a half, recomputes samples of every result and checks each method against known answers. It runs on every change.
  5. Long runs go to a free compute runner, below, and every run is logged.
  6. Mistakes stay in the text. When a check fails, the fix and the failure are both written up (the search chapter's hydrogen fluoride).

The compute runner

A calculation longer than a few minutes runs on GitHub's free runners: 4 cores, 16 GB and up to 6 hours each, up to 20 at once. A run is a short file listing commands, each a tool and plain words, grouped four to a runner. Pushing it starts the run. Each tool saves its progress every few seconds to a data branch, so a run cut at its deadline resumes where it stopped, and every run's logs are kept there with the commit it ran. The results come back as data files, and the pages are regenerated from them.

The methods

Each method: what it computes, how, what checks it (the self-test's numbered checks, by name), its code, where it's used, and its limits.

Atoms: the self-consistent field

Every atom from hydrogen to element 78, in both worlds: its electron configuration, energies, sizes and ionization.

The radial equation in d dimensions has the centrifugal term of l + (d − 3)/2, so one solver serves every dimension. It is solved by Numerov's method on a logarithmic grid, energies found by Sturm bisection on node counts. Many electrons are a self-consistent field: each feels the nucleus, the others' charge through the sphere-averaged kernel (1/max in 3D; k(min/max)/max in 4D, which has no shell theorem), and a local exchange term from the d-dimensional electron gas, with each electron's own share removed and Hund's rule for spins. The ground configuration is searched among candidates, and only converged fields count.

Response and dispersion

How easily each atom's cloud is pushed (polarizability at imaginary frequency), and from it the dispersion pull C₆ between two atoms.

The coupled response of the solved atom to a field at imaginary frequency, then Casimir and Polder's integral for C₆. Exact for hydrogen in both worlds; for helium it is time-dependent Hartree–Fock.

Atom pairs from their clouds

The energy between two atoms at every distance, from their solved clouds.

Gordon and Kim's method: the two frozen clouds overlap, and the energy is their electrostatics (summed shell by shell, so the large nuclear terms cancel exactly) plus the electron gas's kinetic and exchange energy at each point (correlation left out, as in the atoms). Dispersion is added from the response above.

Hartree–Fock in any dimension

Molecules: their shapes, dipoles and hydrogen bonds, our water first as the check.

Hartree–Fock on Gaussian functions, with McMurchie and Davidson's integrals rebuilt for d dimensions: the Boys function F_n is replaced by G_n(T) = ∫₀¹u²ⁿ(1 − u²)^((d−3)/2) e^(−Tu²) du, which is F_n at d = 3. Geometries are relaxed; pairs are scored by their binding energy.

Correlation: MP2

What Hartree–Fock leaves out of a pair's binding: whether the tie between Xater and XH₃ survives it.

Møller–Plesset perturbation theory to second order on the closed-shell Hartree–Fock result, the cores frozen: E₂ = Σ (ia|jb)[2(ia|jb) − (ib|ja)]/(εᵢ + εⱼ − εₐ − ε_b), over occupied i, j and empty a, b, the integrals turned from basis functions to orbitals by a four-index transform (one pass over the unique integrals for every occupied orbital at once). Nothing in it depends on the dimension; that lives in the integrals. Pairs are counterpoise-corrected, each molecule alone in the pair's whole basis, as the Hartree–Fock ones are. The basis is grown by one recipe in both worlds (a second d, diffuse s and p, a second p on hydrogen, an f), and in our world checked against the standard aug-cc-pVTZ and aug-cc-pVQZ, computed at the same arrangements by PySCF.

Force fields fitted to Hartree–Fock

A cheap energy for two molecules, so they can be searched over every pose and simulated by the thousand.

Charges on the nuclei, an exponential repulsion between every pair of sites and a damped dispersion between heavy atoms, fitted to hundreds of Hartree–Fock pair energies: random placements, scans along the hydrogen bond, and close contacts so the repulsion is seen.

Pose search, and its audit

The best way two molecules can sit together: 6 numbers to search in 3D, 10 in 4D.

Many random starts, each descending on the force field by BFGS until its slope is gone (a stall isn't a minimum), turns re-anchored as they wind. Then Hartree–Fock scores the force field's minima and relaxes them by a quasi-Newton descent that measures the whole curvature, steps off saddles along their downhill direction, and probes up to 1.5 radians along every principal direction at an apparent bottom. The audit runs the same pipeline on our pairs with published answers, places thousands of pairs at random for Hartree–Fock alone, and relaxes the deepest.

Cluster minima

How fast the minima multiply as molecules are added: two to seven, in both worlds.

Thousands of random starts per cluster size, each descended to its minimum on the force field; minima told apart by energy and shape, counted, and their unseen number estimated by Chao1. Each start has its own seed, so a search splits across machines and the page can replay any start live.

A polarizable force field, and crystals

Whether a crowd of 4D molecules holds the way its pairs say, and which crystal our water and 4D's make.

Each molecule's charges, and in 4D a cloud term per atom (the 1/r³ potential a round cloud has outside it once the shell theorem fails), fitted to its own Hartree–Fock electrostatic potential; site polarizabilities fitted to its Hartree–Fock tensor by finite field, coupled by Thole's damped dipoles and solved for every molecule at once; only the overlap repulsion fitted to pairs. Trimers scored whole by Hartree–Fock in their own basis, so the three-body part is measured. Crystals: rigid molecules in a cell of any shape, relaxed with the cell from random starts; Ewald's split for each power of r the field uses, in any dimension.

Liquid and vapour, side by side

Where a gas condenses: its critical point.

Gibbs-ensemble Monte Carlo: two boxes trade volume and atoms until one is liquid and one vapour, at one temperature per run; the critical point is fitted to the two densities across temperatures. The 4D pull reaches far, so the boxes are big and the tail is added for a uniform fluid.

Crystals and salts

Which lattice a crystal takes, and how hard a salt holds together.

Lattice sums over candidate lattices (fcc and the 24-cell's among them); salts from closed-shell ions solved above, with Gordon–Kim between charged clouds and Evjen's neutral cubes for the sum. In 4D the numerical tail would diverge over the r³ shells, so past 14 bohr the exact far field from the clouds' moments is used.

Stars

Whether a ball of gas holds itself together under each law of gravity, its structure, and how often its nuclei meet.

The virial theorem in d dimensions for a 1/r^k pull; the self-consistent polytrope, iterated on each law's shell kernel (Hachisu's method); and WKB tunnelling with Langer's centrifugal term, the 4D head-on meeting's built-in barrier.

Atoms in a magnet

Whether an atom is drawn into a magnet or pushed out, how the field's two halves differ, and the field of a spinning ball.

Pauli's equation as the square of a first-order one (Lévy-Leblond's), built in 4D from quaternion units: the spin couples to the field's left half only. Langevin's diamagnetism from the solver's ⟨r²⟩ (−Σ⟨r²⟩/2d per atom), and Curie's paramagnetism for the spin Hund's first rule gives the solver's configuration. A spinning ball's field from its moment, A = Ωx/r³ under the pinned kernel.

Orbits and planets

Kepler's orbits under each law, precession round a real star, planets in planes turned apart, moons, starlight on a planet, the stability map, and the orrery that puts them together.

A leapfrog n-body integrator in any dimension (symplectic, so energy doesn't drift over hundreds of thousands of orbits), orbits' planes compared by their principal angles, a round star's outside potential summed from its shells. The orrery builds a system from the results: planes from their halves of turning as the nebula leaves them, spacings from the stability map, moons in the completely orthogonal plane.

Nebulae

Whether a rotating cloud of gas falling onto a star flattens into a disk.

Tens of thousands of particles pulled as 1/r² (Newton in 3D, the pinned law in 4D, so only the dimension differs), with pairwise collisions that remove part of each approach speed along the line between the pair: momentum and spin kept exactly, energy taken. The same code in 3D is the control.

Run it yourself

The code is public. With node (version 20 or later) and git, and nothing else installed:

git clone https://github.com/minormobius/agent01
cd agent01
node four/atom/test/atom4.selftest.mjs        # every check, about 90 s
node four/atom/tools/orbits.mjs               # re-bake the orbits, 40 s
node four/atom/tools/clusters.mjs 4 5 100     # 100 starts of five Xaters

Each tool's first lines say what it does and how to run it. A fork's own GitHub Actions can run the compute queue (.github/compute/atom4.json) the same way. Packaging the solvers as a standalone library, free to use, is planned; until a licence is published the code is open to read but not yet licensed for reuse.
