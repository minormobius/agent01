A planetary system in four dimensions

A star, its planets and their moons, moving as they would in four dimensions of space with gravity pinned like charge (the pull falls as 1/r², so orbits close, as ours do). Nothing here is drawn on rails: every body is pulled by every other, stepped forward live. What it's built from comes from the planetary-systems chapter, and where it had to assume, it says so below.

Each orbit's plane has two halves of turning. Every planet's left half lines up with the nebula's; only its right half differs, a point on this sphere. The ring marks where the nebula's spin leaves them on average (a mean cosine of – to its own, ✦). Two planets whose points are φ apart orbit in planes tilted φ/2 in both of 4D's angles at once.

Distances in star radii; the star is as hot as our Sun. Temperatures are a black planet's, from starlight alone. Periods in the innermost planet's years. Tilt: the two angles between a planet's plane and the nebula's main plane, and to planet b's (always equal: isoclinic). e: how far from round each orbit is now, live. Moons' distances are drawn eight times larger than they are.

What it's built from

  • computed The motion. Leapfrog n-body under the pinned law, star, planets and moons all pulling (moons too light to pull back). The same integrator the chapter's orbit runs use.
  • computed The planes. A 4D nebula spinning at two rates, 1 and q, doesn't flatten to one plane. It ends a thick lens, and every orbit's left half of turning lines up with the cloud's while its right half lines up only to a mean cosine just under (1 − q)/(1 + q) (measured). Each planet's plane is drawn that way. A random spin's q is typically about a fifth.
  • computed The spacing. Each planet sits out past the one inside at the closest ratio the stability map found stable for 100,000 orbits at their tilt and mass, with a random margin. Tilted planets disturb each other less, so 4D systems pack closer than ours. Then the whole system is run before it's shown, and redrawn if anything strays.
  • computed The moons. Each in its planet's completely orthogonal plane, the one plane the star can't tug (the moon runs), a third of the way to where the star would take it.
  • follows The temperatures. Starlight thins as 1/r³ and a planet glows as T⁵, so temperature falls as r−3/5: our water's liquid band, 273–373 K, runs from 57 to 96 star radii here.
  • a choice The star. A 4D star's surface temperature isn't known yet, so it's set to our Sun's. How many planets, and their masses (a tenth of Jupiter's or Jupiter's), aren't derived either: this is a possible system, checked by running it, not a predicted one.
  • open Days and seasons. A 4D planet spins in two planes at two rates, and how that sits against its orbit decides what seasons even are. Not worked out yet, so the planets here don't turn.
