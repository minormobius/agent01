What the first stars made

A four-dimensional universe starts, as ours did, with hydrogen and helium. Its first stars burn them, and the heavier ones explode. This page shows what those stars give back, element by element, on 4D's periodic table and then on ours. Their metals sit next to our Sun's, each 4D element placed where its counterpart sits in our table.

Revised (2026-11-24). This page used to put almost half the first stars' metals on one element, 137, and most of the rest in the rock row. Both were wrong. A 4D core reaches its ash's ignition temperature only at the Chandrasekhar mass itself, and there it burns almost wholly to equilibrium. The rock-row-heavy answer came from cores the model let stand above the limit, which cannot. And equilibrium doesn't make one nucleus: it makes a band of proton-rich ones that decay to 4D's stable line, around elements 116–122. Where that band sits is the least certain thing on the page (below).

On our table

Each square is one of our elements. Its top half is that element's share of the Sun's metals, by mass. Its bottom half is the share of the first 4D stars' metals in the element that does what this one does: 4D's counterpart, named by place in its table (pxygen answers to our oxygen). 4D elements with no counterpart are in the tray below. Point at or tap a square for the numbers.

What kind of element

Both mixtures by the row of our table they fall in, 4D's through its counterparts. Our Sun's metals are mostly in our second row: oxygen, carbon, neon, nitrogen. The first 4D stars put almost nothing there.

On 4D's own table

The same metals on the table they belong to: all 140 elements of the table of four dimensions, shaded by their share of the first stars' metals (log scale). The dashed-gold squares are new: elements with no counterpart in ours.

How this was made

The 4D side is computed up to two declared choices. The stars are aged on the book's own nuclear network, from zero age to the end of their central hydrogen, in mass ladders at a gravity of 10⁻¹⁷ of ours (paper 2, section 4). A star returns what its envelope holds, plus a share of its core's ash on the wind if its core stays under the Chandrasekhar mass. A core that reaches the Chandrasekhar mass explodes and returns everything. How far an explosion burns is computed the same way for both worlds, calibrated on our white dwarfs: some of the core reaches equilibrium, some burns to the rock row (the ash fused with itself), and some stays as it was. In 4D almost all of it reaches equilibrium. A 4D core gets hot enough to light its ash only at the Chandrasekhar mass itself, so it ignites far denser than our white dwarfs do. The rock row would take over only if the core expanded a hundred to a thousand times more before burning than ours do. Then a mass function weights the stars, and the net yield p is the metals returned per mass locked away in remnants and long-lived stars, the standard measure.

Declared, not computed. How much a core grows after its hydrogen runs out decides which stars explode, so it is the main control above. The first star is being followed past exhaustion now, to compute it. Also declared: the winds and the explosion's expansion (both calibrated on ours, carried over), the mass function, and which product each ash element burns to ().

"Iron" is not iron here. In our world an explosion's equilibrium ends at the iron peak, nickel-56, which decays to iron. In 4D, equilibrium (computed on the liquid-drop model of nuclei, at the exploding core's density and as it cools) makes . Like nickel-56, those are proton-rich; each decays along its mass number to 4D's stable line, releasing about MeV a nucleon, and ends . The band is the least certain number on the page: the liquid drop is a fit, far past the nuclei the book computes directly, and held to our own masses and to the book's light 4D nuclei it could sit anywhere from about element 100 to 250 (the nuclei chapter's check).

The comparison is not like for like. The Sun's metals are what about nine billion years of our galaxy's stars made before it formed, every generation and every process (massive stars, white dwarfs, merging neutron stars). The 4D side is one generation, the first. The like-for-like comparison is our own first stars, which can only be read from the oldest, most metal-poor stars; that is a next step. So read this as where the first 4D metals sit, not as a forecast of a 4D sun.

Ours. The solar mixture is Asplund, Grevesse, Sauval & Scott 2009 (ARA&A 47, 481), table 1: the present-day photosphere, or the meteorites where the photosphere gives no value. It is turned into mass fractions with NIST's standard atomic weights, and checked against the paper's own bulk composition: Z = 0.0134, as theirs.
