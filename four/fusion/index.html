How our stars burn

Before four dimensions, our own world. A star is a ball of gas squeezed by its own weight until its centre is hot enough for nuclei to fuse. Here is what happens at that centre, stage by stage, from the Sun's hydrogen to the silicon at the heart of a star about to explode. The nuclei are cartoons, but which reactions happen, in what order and how often, come from the real reaction rates astrophysicists use.

Why fusion is hard

Nuclei are positively charged, so they push each other away. To fuse, two of them must get close enough, a few femtometres, for the strong force to take over, and to get there they have to climb a hill of electric repulsion. In the Sun's centre the hill is about a thousand times higher than a typical proton's energy. Classically, nothing would ever fuse.

Quantum mechanics lets a nucleus tunnel through the hill instead of climbing over it, with a chance that falls steeply the lower its energy and the higher the hill. So fusion happens in a narrow window: fast enough nuclei to have a real chance of tunnelling, but not so fast that hardly any exist. That window, the Gamow peak, decides everything below. Heavier, more charged nuclei face a higher hill, so each stage of a star's life needs it hotter than the last.

The hill one nucleus sees coming at another: the electric repulsion outside, the strong force's well inside. The dashed line is the energy they typically fuse at; the orange band is the stretch they have to tunnel through.

A star's core, stage by stage

Pick a stage, or watch them in order: each starts from the ash the one before left. Protons are red, neutrons blue. Wavy lines are gamma rays, yellow dots positrons, dashed lines neutrinos (which leave the star at once). Reactions are drawn one at a time at a steady pace, chosen at random in proportion to how often each really happens at that moment, so common ones show up often and rare ones rarely.

What's real here, and what isn't

  • Real: every reaction and its rate, from the REACLIB library (the snapshot that the pynucastro project ships; Cyburt et al. 2010, ApJS 189, 240), 149 reactions among 38 nuclei, integrated as a network; which reactions happen at each moment and how often, relative to each other; the composition, the energy released, and how long each stage lasts at its conditions.
  • Simplified: each stage is held at one round temperature and density, typical of the Sun's core for the first and of a star of about twenty Suns for the rest, and run until its fuel is gone, so the times are for those conditions held fixed, not a real star's stage lengths: a real core is stirred, feeding its centre from a larger store, and heats as it goes, and the Sun only burns its hydrogen halfway in its lifetime. Left out: screening by the electrons (which speeds the Sun's reactions by 5–20%), the neutron-capture side channels, partition functions in the reverse rates, and the energy neutrinos carry away (counted in the energy here).
  • Cartoon: how nuclei move, and how many are drawn. A real core holds about 10⁵⁰ nuclei, not 64, and rare ones (the CNO cycle's carbon, one in a thousand) are drawn even when they'd be fewer than one. The clock skips, and the page says by how much.
