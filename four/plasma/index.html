Magnetic fields in four dimensions

A star is plasma, and plasma carries magnetic fields. In our three dimensions a magnetic field is drawn as an arrow at each point. That's a coincidence of three: the field is really a turning in a plane, and in 3D every plane has one direction at right angles to it. In 4D there's no such direction, and the field has to be met as what it is, a bivector: six numbers, one for each plane xy, xz, xw, yz, yw, zw. This page is about what that does to the textbook physics of magnetized gas (the stars chapter uses it).

1 · A 4D field is two planes

Six numbers look like a lot. But every 4D bivector, whatever its six numbers, is two perpendicular planes, each turning at its own rate, a and b. In 3D only one plane fits, so there's only a. The two rates come from the field's two halves, its left and right parts, each a 3D arrow (the same split that divides 4D rotations, and the left half is the one an electron's spin feels: fields): a = |left| + |right| and b = |left| − |right|.

Three kinds of field, then. Simple: one rate is zero, a single plane, the halves equal in size. That's the only kind our world has. Two-plane: both rates non-zero, the point's path winding round a torus. All left or all right: the rates equal, every point turned round a great circle, the circles of Hopf's fibration (geometry). The one number that says how far a field is from simple is F∧F = 2ab, the Pfaffian doubled: zero exactly when the field is a single plane.

One consequence to play with: a charge in a two-plane field has no field line to run along, so with nothing pushing it, it can only circle, and stays put for ever. In ours a magnetic bottle leaks out of its ends. Pushed, it drifts at right angles, slowly in a stiff plane and fast in a weak one: drift golf is a course of such fields.

2 · Frozen in, and what's frozen

In a plasma that conducts perfectly, the gas and the field move together: Alfvén's theorem, the reason the Sun's field is dragged out into the solar wind and wound up by its spin. It holds in 4D too. It needs only Faraday's law, which the lens vacuum leaves alone (it changes how currents make fields, not how fields change), and Ohm's law for a perfect conductor. Then the field is carried by the flow as a bivector:

∂F/∂t = −d(ivF) = −ℒvF   (dF = 0)  ⇒  along a parcel: F → J⁻ᵀF J⁻¹, ρ → ρ / det J

In ours, "frozen in" means field lines are threads the gas carries. In 4D a simple field's "lines" are sheets: through every point runs a whole surface the field turns within, and the gas carries the sheets. A two-plane field has no sheets at all. And now something our world doesn't have: the Pfaffian of J⁻ᵀF J⁻¹ is Pf F / det J, and the density goes as 1/det J too, so

F∧F / ρ is carried unchanged by every parcel of gas

A parcel's field can grow, shrink and turn its planes anywhere, but it can't change whether it has sheets. A simple field stays simple. A two-plane field stays two-plane, and when the flow stretches one of its planes the other starves: a times b is pinned to the density. A tangle of two planes can't be made or undone by moving the gas; only resistance can (or, where a star's field is frozen for 1025 years and more, the collisionless physics at the smallest scales: the stars chapter). In 3D, ideal flow leaves no comparable number in each parcel from the field alone; the nearest thing is helicity, which isn't local, and which 4D doesn't have (below).

3 · Helicity has nowhere to live

Our plasma physics leans on one more conserved quantity, magnetic helicity, ∫A·B over a volume: how much the field lines link and twist round each other. Perfect conduction conserves it exactly, and a little resistance destroys it far more slowly than it destroys the field's energy. That's why a disturbed lab plasma relaxes to a calm twisted state (Taylor's relaxation), why the Sun throws off twisted clouds of field, and how the dynamos that make stars' fields are thought to work.

follows In 4D it doesn't exist. ∫A·B is really the integral of A∧F, a 3-form, over three-dimensional space. In four dimensions A∧F is still a 3-form, so it can only be integrated over a three-dimensional surface, not over a region of space: there's no helicity of a volume. Its one candidate replacement, F∧F, is a 4-form that can be integrated over space, but it's d(A∧F), a surface term: over a closed region it's only what crosses the boundary.

ours: A∧F (3-form) over 3-space → helicity  ·  4D: A∧F over 4-space → nothing; F∧F = d(A∧F)

What 4D has instead is section 2's local number, F∧F/ρ in every parcel, and with it a whole family of conserved totals, ∫ρ f(F∧F/ρ) dV for any function f. open Whether those do helicity's jobs. Taylor's relaxation and the dynamo theory built on helicity have to be rebuilt.

4 · Can a 4D star make a field?

A field frozen into a star for 1025 years can be a fossil from the star's birth. Or the star can make it: its turning, churning gas stretching and folding a weak field faster than resistance unmakes it, a dynamo, as our Sun's does every eleven years. Our textbooks warn that it isn't easy: a flow that's too symmetric can't do it (Cowling's and Zeldovich's anti-dynamo theorems), and the flows that can are twisted, with the helicity section 3 says 4D lacks.

computed The textbook test. G. O. Roberts's (1972): a fixed flow of cells, periodic in x and y, and the question whether any field grows. The field follows ∂F/∂t = −d(ivF) minus its resistive decay, ηk² for each wave in ours and η|k|³ in 4D (the lens: the stars chapter), solved pseudo-spectrally for each of the field's wavelengths along the directions the flow doesn't vary in, and the fastest growth kept. The magnetic Reynolds number Rm = 1/η measures how much faster the flow moves the field than resistance smooths it. The checks: with no flow every field decays at exactly ηk² or η|k|³; our Roberts flow grows a field with an optimal wavelength, as Roberts found; a flow confined to a plane never does (Zeldovich).

Yes: on the textbook test 4D makes fields, and more easily than ours. Roberts's helical cells grow a field in 4D at every Rm tried, from 1 to 14, about a fifth to three-fifths faster than in ours, because the lens's resistance is weaker on large scales (|k|³ against k²), and a dynamo of this kind works on scales larger than its cells. A flow in a plane still can't: Zeldovich's theorem holds in 4D. And there's a new direction to fail in: a field that varies only along w, where the Roberts flow doesn't move, never grows; the two-helix flow, its flow along w in a different pattern from the one along z, grows such fields only above Rm ≈ 5.

open Roberts's flows vary along two directions only, so in 4D they're 3D-like flows with room around them. A 4D star's convection turns in two planes and varies along all four; whether the missing helicity matters there, and how a 4D star's dynamo saturates once its field pushes back on the gas, are the next questions. And the magnetized wind: how fast a 4D star spins down.
