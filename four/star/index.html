How to read it
  • Colours inside are temperature, from the surface's (deep red) to the centre's (white), on a log scale. The bright glow near the middle is where the star makes its light: 90% of it comes from inside the radius named.
  • Moving cells mark gas that convects, stirring the layer; smooth bands are radiative, where light diffuses out and the gas stays put. A pale blue ring near the surface is a wafer-thin convective layer, drawn far thicker than it is.
  • In four dimensions a star is a 4-ball, and a screen can only show a 3D slice of it. The slider moves the slice through the fourth direction, w: off-centre slices are smaller balls, and each layer of 4D radius r shows at √(r² − w²), so the inner layers vanish from slices far out. The piece cut away is one of the sixteen orthants (x, y, z, w all positive), so the cut only opens on slices with w > 0. On the others the ball is whole.
  • Mass and age. Temperature isn't a free choice: mass and age set it. Most stars here are zero-age, just settled onto their main sequence, and their core clock is the time the centre, as it is then, would take to burn half its hydrogen if it stayed as it is: a zero-age estimate, which runs short, since a real core heats as it burns (the 4D stars aged here halve theirs 2–32 times later than that clock's convention said). The stars marked aged have been run through their lives (the book's evolve.mjs: every shell burnt at its own temperature and density, stirred regions mixed), and the age slider walks them from zero age to the end of central hydrogen: watch the core heat and densify, the burnt hydrogen spread in a profile, the surface brighten. For our stars a chart sets each life against MIST's published track for the same mass (Choi et al. 2016), a model built independently with fuller physics. In 4D, gravity G and the strong force s are the dials that matter; mass is in Chandrasekhar masses at that G.
Where it comes from, and what to trust

Each star is a zero-age model from the book's star solver (atom/js/mainseq.js: hydrostatic balance, energy transport, Fermi–Dirac electrons; in 4D, pinned 1/r gravity summed over the whole profile, the lens vacuum's opacity, and the burning of the 4D reaction network itself). The convective core is read off the solver's own gradient. The outer layers come from the surface model behind paper 2's surface test (atom/js/surface.js: Saha ionization in d dimensions, bound–free, free–free and H⁻ opacity from the book's own atoms), checked on our own stars: free–free against Bell & Berrington's tables to within 36%, and our stars' outer convection switching off between 1.25 and 1.4 solar masses, as textbooks give. The 4D Sun-like star's photosphere is ionized, so it has no convective envelope; the light, cool stars of each sequence do.

Aged stars (ours): REACLIB burning shell by shell with screening and neutrino losses, initial hydrogen 0.707 (set so our Sun shines at today's luminosity at 4.57 billion years, as standard solar models set theirs), metals 1.43% (MIST's), OPAL's opacity tables as measured input, a mixing-length envelope at the surface with α fixed on our Sun's radius, no diffusion and no overshoot (a stirred core stops exactly where the gradient says). Checked against standard solar models in paper 2's section 4, which also checks the book's own opacity against OPAL's. MIST differs on purpose in diffusion and overshoot, so the comparison shows where those matter.

Not in it: rotation, mass loss, and molecules (our coolest stars' surfaces are outside the model, and say so). Envelope depths of deep zones are read from a model that keeps the star's mass fixed, so trust where convection starts more than how much mass it holds.
