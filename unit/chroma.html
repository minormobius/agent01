What a color sounds like

Visible light runs 384–789 THz. That is a ratio of 2.05 — the entire rainbow is one octave. Drop it by forty octaves and it lands almost exactly on F4 → F5, close enough to play.

Two whites

identical to the eye, nothing alike to the ear

Your screen makes white from three narrow emitters. The second spectrum below is one smooth hill — and it is not nearly the same colour, it is exactly the same colour: three broad humps solved so their XYZ matches the screen's to the last decimal place. Your eye reduces any spectrum to three numbers and discards the rest, which is the whole trick that lets screens work at all. Your ear discards nothing. Play them.

What is honest here. The pitch really is the light's own frequency, divided by 240 — nothing is transposed to taste. What is not honest is any claim that this is what a color "is": the ear and the eye do not work alike. The eye integrates a whole spectrum into three numbers and throws the rest away, which is why metamers exist; the ear resolves partials and keeps them, which is why the two whites above sound different. Mapping one onto the other is a choice, not a discovery.

Why it sounds rough. One octave is 1200 cents, and the visible band is 1245. Chop that into 48 and the partials sit 26 cents apart — a quarter of a semitone, well inside the range where the ear hears beating rather than separate notes. A broad spectrum therefore arrives as a cluster, not a chord, and no amount of care in the mapping avoids it. The stretch control buys resolution by lying about the frequency; the roughness meter is Plomp & Levelt's dissonance curve, computed over a fixed bank so it measures the colour and not the setting.
