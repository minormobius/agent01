Both halves of that sentence matter, so here is exactly what is running.

The physics

Each residue is one bead at its α-carbon. The beads obey Langevin dynamics under a structure-based potential — a Gō model, in the Clementi–Onuchic form: harmonic bonds and bond angles, a two-term torsion potential, a 10-12 well for every native contact, and a repulsive r⁻¹² core for every other pair. Nothing is scripted. The chain finds its way there by integrating forces, and every force in it is checked against a numerical gradient of the energy before the build ships.

The cheat

The reference bond angles, torsions and contact distances are all read off the deposited structure. The native state is the global energy minimum by construction. So this model can reproduce a fold; it cannot predict one. Real de-novo folding from sequence alone is not something a browser — or, with physics, anything else — currently does. That gap is why AlphaFold happened.

What the model does earn you is the shape of the process: the collapse, the search, the order contacts form in, how a β-sheet is more frustrated than a helix bundle, and what happens as you push the temperature toward the folding transition. Those are real, and they are what you are watching.

What the numbers mean

Q is the fraction of native contacts currently made — the standard reaction coordinate for folding. A contact counts as made inside 1.2× its native distance. RMSD is the C-alpha deviation from the deposited structure after optimal superposition. States visited accumulates every (Q, energy) pair the run has passed through: the funnel that textbooks draw as a cartoon, drawn instead from the trajectory in front of you.

Where it came from

It started as a question about a cryo-EM paper — Ji et al., Orthoflaviviruses use diverse binding modes to engage LDLR family receptors — and how much of protein physics a browser could actually afford. Two of the proteins here are from that paper's world: the LDLR LA5 module, and the tick-borne encephalitis virus envelope protein.

Structures from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Engine in Rust, compiled to WebAssembly with no bindgen; renderer is hand-written WebGL2.
