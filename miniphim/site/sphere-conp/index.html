Is it a 3-sphere? Short proofs of "no", and what they rest on

The paper: John A. Baldwin and Steven Sivek, 3-manifold decision problems and Riemann hypotheses, arXiv:2611.06705, posted 2026-11-04, math.GT. It's a short note. This page says what it claims, what that claim stands on, and where the abstract and the body read differently.

The question

You're handed a knot drawn as a diagram, or a 3-dimensional space cut into tetrahedra. Is the knot really unknotted? Is the space really the 3-sphere? Computers can decide this, but the question here is about certificates: if the answer is yes, is there a short proof you can check quickly (that puts the problem in NP)? And if the answer is no, is there a short proof of that (coNP)?

For "yes, it's the unknot" and "yes, it's the 3-sphere", short proofs have been known for years. For "no", the known short proofs needed the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH), starting with Kuperberg on the unknot.

What GRH was doing

The certificate for "no" is a piece of algebra: a representation of the space's fundamental group into a group of 2×2 matrices over a finite field of size p. For these problems, a nonabelian one exists when the answer is "no" (that part is the topology: Kronheimer–Mrowka for knots, Zentner for the 3-sphere). The matrices are easy to check. The catch is p: if it has too many digits, the certificate isn't short. In the authors' words, "GRH is used to ensure that the certificate has polynomial length in the input." It does that through an effective Chebotarev density theorem (Lagarias–Odlyzko), which promises a small enough prime.

Try it: a certificate for "no", found slowly, checked fast

A small working model of the idea, with knots instead of 3-spheres because their groups fit on one line. Pick a knot and a prime p. The page searches for two 2×2 matrices of determinant 1, entries mod p, that obey the knot group's relation and don't commute. If it finds them, that pair is a proof the knot isn't the unknot: the unknot's group is ℤ, and nothing built from ℤ can fail to commute.

What to notice. Checking is a few dozen multiplications; finding is a search through every matrix mod p. And not every prime works: for the cinquefoil the strip shows 5, 11, 19, 29, 31, the primes that are 5 or ±1 mod 10, as far as this page counts. That is a Chebotarev pattern in miniature: a fixed share of primes is good, but nothing in the pattern alone says how far you must go to meet the first one. For these toy knots it's always close. For a knot or a 3-manifold drawn with thousands of crossings or tetrahedra, the good prime must still have few enough digits for the certificate to be short, and that bound is what GRH used to supply and what Hypothesis 1.2 now supplies, more loosely.

What the model leaves out: real certificates come from big presentations read off a diagram or triangulation, not two-letter relations; for the 3-sphere the representation is Zentner's into SL(2,ℂ), brought down to a finite field; and the topology that guarantees a certificate exists is the deep part, which no widget shows. What the note does

It shows a weaker hypothesis is enough. Their Hypothesis 1.2, the quasi-Riemann hypothesis for Dedekind zeta functions: there is one constant ε > 0 such that no Dedekind zeta function of any number field has a zero with real part above 1 − ε. (GRH says: none above 1/2.) Frei, Sofos and Widmer showed this gives an effective Chebotarev theorem with worse bounds than GRH, and the note checks they are still good enough to find the prime. Their Theorem 1.3: each of the problems below is in coNP, modulo Hypothesis 1.2.

Abstract vs. body

The abstract says the note leads "for example to the first unconditional proof that 3-sphere recognition is in coNP." The body is more careful. Hypothesis 1.2 now has a claimed proof: Cureton and Thorner communicated a Lean-formalized proof with ε = 1/32, "building on the formalized proof announced by OpenAI" for Dirichlet L-functions. And then:

"at the time of writing it is not clear to us that any human has understood the proof"
… "we will continue to describe these results as being conditional on Hypothesis" 1.2.

So both readings are honest. If you trust a machine-checked proof that no one has yet explained, 3-sphere recognition is unconditionally in coNP. If you want a proof a person has understood, it's conditional on Hypothesis 1.2, which is still a big step down from needing GRH. (The note's own AI statement: "No generative AI was used at any point in the writing of this note." The proof it leans on is another matter.)

Why it's worth a look

  • A topology result where the bottleneck is analytic number theory: how small can a prime with a given property be.
  • A live case of a mathematical fact whose formal check came before anyone's understanding of it, and two authors deciding in print how to label that.
  • Still open, from the table: is cusped hyperbolicity in NP? Hyperbolic knot recognition in NP is announced as forthcoming (Baroni and Lackenby).
