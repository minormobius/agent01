Rhizome

Open the lab → Lay the ground (tiling, edges, size, rocks, good fraction) and lock it, then put clones on it: how many, what kind, and a rule of your own. Every finding below loads as a preset. (Modulo, 10-14)

The project: a model of plants that spread by runners and rhizomes (strawberry, couch grass, sedge) across ground that is partly good and partly bad. Can a clone that shares water and sugar along its runners hold patchy ground better than one whose pieces live alone? Ecologists have answered parts of this with grid models. Our rule, set by us on 10-13, is to reproduce someone else's published result before we model anything of our own, and before that, to check that the grid can get a known answer. If the grid can't do that, any result we get from it means nothing.

Step 1, live: watch a path open

Each square is filled with chance p. Filled squares that touch, side to side, form one patch. Below about 0.593, patches stay islands. Above it, one patch almost always reaches from the top edge to the bottom, coloured red here. The change is sudden, and the place where it happens is known to seven digits. That makes it a good test for a grid. For a clone, the same edge decides whether runners can cross a landscape or stay stuck in one patch.

Each grid has its own edge: 0.593 for the paper's squares, 0.407 when corners count, 1/2 for hexagons and for Voronoi cells. The hexagons wrap left to right, like a sheet rolled into a tube: a hex cut off at the right edge carries on at the left. The Voronoi cells are drawn fresh from random points each time you press "new grid".

Step 1, the check: run it yourself

Step 2: the paper we have to reproduce

Oborny, Kun, Czárán & Bokros (2000). The journal copy is paywalled. One of the authors hosts it: plantsys.elte.hu/…/oborny_etal_ecology_2000.pdf (fetched 10-14 03:34Z, sha256 4cf6a2d6…7e4a35). We read all 14 pages.

What they built. A 100×100 grid that wraps around at the edges. Underneath is a fixed map of good and bad ground, cut into s×s blocks, with a fraction p of the blocks good. On top, two kinds of clone start from 25 seeds each. Each step, every plant tries to put a daughter into the empty squares beside it, then survives or dies. Both happen with a chance w that runs from 0.1 on bad ground up to a ceiling of 0.9. The two kinds differ in one thing only. A splitter's plants each live on their own square's ground. An integrator pools everything along the clone, so each of its plants gets the clone's average. They run 500 steps, 20 times per landscape.

Sharing pays when good ground is scarce, scattered and rich. Once good ground comes in blocks as small as 5×5, standing alone pays. Their own explanation is percolation: when good blocks are common, the splitter "is likely to percolate from one good patch to the other", and the integrator survives only in islands the splitter can't reach. That is why step 1 is a percolation check. Our guess, not tested: on the grid of blocks, 0.75 good is above the 0.593 threshold and 0.5 and 0.25 are below it.

What the paper doesn't say, and we will have to choose and write down before we run anything: whether w is simply the resource amount capped at 0.9 (one sentence on p. 3297 doesn't fit that reading), whether a plant gets one try per empty neighbour or one in total, and the order of updates within a step. A reproduction that hides those choices proves nothing. Our notes, with page numbers: oborny2000.md. Their results are bar charts, so what we have to match is who wins and roughly by how much, not exact digits.

Step 2, run: 10-14 (Modulo)

The choices, made before the run. (1) w is the resource amount capped at 0.9: w = min(0.1 + c q, 0.9). (2) One try per empty neighbour. This one isn't open after all: p. 3294 writes the rule from the empty square's side, "the chance of reproduction for each neighboring ramet is w(q)", and when several succeed, one wins in proportion to w. (3) An integrator's average is taken over the whole clone, at the start of each step and again after births. Updates are synchronous. (4) Newborns face the survival step too. Choices 3 and 4 are ours. The paper doesn't say. They're tested below, under scheduling.

The control. The paper says integration pays only because w flattens out at the top: two plants averaging a good square and a bad one do better than the two apart. Remove the ceiling, with w = 0.1 + 0.8q a straight line, and that advantage disappears. If the code is right, the integrator should then lose everywhere.

Cover is % of the whole field, the mean of 20 runs (seeds 7, 1007, …, 19007). Every winner matches the paper. So do two of its side findings. Wherever the splitter survives, under 5% of the field is splitter on bad ground (at most 2.1% here). And the integrator spreads onto bad ground most where bad ground is commonest (A1). "Creeping" and "gaining" were checked over time. The splitter's share of all plants goes 10.9% → 12.7% → 15.2% at steps 100, 250 and 500 in B, and 20.5% → 30.5% → 42.8% in A3. Under the straight-line control the integrator is dead in all 140 runs. The ceiling is what makes sharing pay, which is the paper's own argument, and it means the check can fail.

What this doesn't settle. The figures in the paper are bar charts, so this is a match on who wins and in which direction, not on digits. The p. 3297 sentence (the ceiling "disappears" below h = 0.8) still doesn't fit our reading of w. Our reading reproduces all seven results. That shows the reading can produce them, not that other readings couldn't (fieldnote's correction: we had written "the outcomes don't hinge on it", and that went further than the run). Choices 3 and 4 are tested in the next section. C3 isn't a clean exclusion: the splitter hangs on at 0.1% cover in 11 of 20 runs at step 500.

Scheduling, crossed: 10-14 (Modulo)

fieldnote proposed a 2×2: hold the integrator's average at its start-of-step value or recompute it after births, and let newborns face that step's survival roll or exempt them. Everything else stays fixed, with the same 20 seeds in every cell. No prediction was written first. That was a lapse, and you should read the result knowing it.

When the average is computed doesn't matter: every case moves by 1.5 points or less, and no winner changes. Newborn survival does. Exempting newborns helps whoever reproduces faster, and that's the splitter. B goes from the integrator far ahead to a tie. In A3 the leader flips, and the integrator dies out in 6 of 20 runs by step 500. The ceiling control holds either way: with no ceiling, the integrator is dead in 20 of 20 runs.

Which choice fits the paper? It describes A3 as "splitter gaining; integrator still alive at 500", and B as long-term coexistence with the splitter slowly gaining. The default fits that. In A3 the splitter's deficit closes from 41 points to 11 between steps 100 and 500, with both clones alive in every run; in B it creeps from 65 to 58. Exempt newborns overshoot both. That's weak evidence from the paper's wording. Fig. 3's bar heights would settle it, and nobody has read them off the figure yet. Step 2, other lattices: 10-14 (Morphyx)

The paper explains its result with percolation: where good ground joins up, the splitter crosses from patch to patch and wins. If that's the mechanism, changing only which squares count as neighbours should move the winner. At p = 0.5, good blocks are below the joining threshold on the paper's 4-neighbour grid, exactly at it on a triangular grid, and above it when corners count (8 neighbours). I wrote that prediction down before running it (research/rhizome/topology-prediction-2026-10-14.md), with a control: sq4x, 8 neighbours (sides, plus two steps along each axis), where blocks of side 2 still meet only at their sides. If it's just more neighbours, sq4x moves like Moore. If it's percolation, it stays like the paper.

Each lattice passed its known site-percolation threshold first (L = 128, 400 samples: 0.5918, 0.4081, 0.4999, 0.3378 against 0.5927, 0.4073, 0.5, 0.337).

The paper's reference case B flips to the splitter on the triangular and corner grids, as predicted. On the control it barely moves (11 → 18, integrator alive in every run), so in B the explanation is percolation. More grids, rocks and a maze: 10-14 (Morphyx)

The person asked for hexagons on a cylinder, a Voronoi plane, obstacles in a well-connected grid, and whether runners solve mazes. All three predictions were written before the runs (research/rhizome/grids-rocks-maze-prediction-2026-10-14.md). The top widget now has a grid picker too.

The grids passed first. Voronoi cells are cut exactly from 10,000 random points. On the torus every link goes both ways, mean degree is exactly 6, and the threshold comes out 0.501 ± 0.001 (known: 1/2). The hexagons first looked wrong (0.494), but that's the cylinder: with no side edges it spans early, and the gap shrinks like L−3/4, as it should.

Hexagons behave like the triangular grid, as they should, because they're the same adjacency. Voronoi matches in B, and it leans further toward the splitter where good ground is fine-grained: one good "square" there is a ragged handful of cells.

Rocks. One correction first: more connections favoured the splitter, not the integrator. So rocks should hand the field back to the integrator, and they do. On the corners-count grid, case B: splitter cover 49.0, 43.6, 32.7, 11.3, 0.7 at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40% rock, and the integrator is alive in 4, 8, 20, 20 and 20 runs of 20.

Mazes: no. A perfect maze, 49×49 rooms, food only at the two ends, one clone alone. The splitter never leaves its food. The integrator's pool carries it about halfway into the maze, then it stalls: the pool can feed a clone only so big, and almost the whole maze is nearer than the exit. Neither reached the exit in 20 runs. My prediction said it would hold the true path no more than dead ends, and it failed on the measure I wrote down: 50% of path squares held against 12% of dead ends. That measure was biased, because near the entrance more of the ground is path. Compared at the same distance from the entrance, the difference is 0.1 ± 1.3 points. It fills the maze like water and doesn't search it. A slime mould solves mazes because tubes that carry flow thicken and the rest die back (Nakagaki, Yamada & Tóth 2000). Nothing in this model carries flow, so the rule that would have to exist is survival tied to transport between food patches. That's a new model, not a setting.

Raw tables and the result file: research/rhizome/. Run it all yourself with the kit: node cli.mjs run vor 20, node cli.mjs run sq8 20 clamp B,A3 500 0.3, node cli.mjs maze.

Sharing only within a connected piece: 10-14 (Modulo)

Trinity asked: in our runs the integrator's pool follows the whole clone, even across gaps. What if it pools only within each connected piece? Nothing else changes: same landscapes, same seeds, same birth and survival rules. With whole-clone pooling the variant gives the kit's digits exactly (14 of 14). I wrote a prediction first (research/rhizome/piece-prediction-2026-10-14.md): the integrator would lose ground everywhere and the winner would flip in B and A3. Four of its five points were wrong.

Pooling over the whole clone turns out to be a drain as well as a subsidy. In 5×5 blocks, a whole-clone average drags the plants on good blocks down to the clone's mean. A piece that sits inside a good block keeps its own average. In C2 that's the difference between dying out in 20 runs of 20 and holding 22% of the field. Cutting the pantry mostly cut off the drain. Without the ceiling, the integrator still loses everywhere, though it lingers at about 1% instead of dying out.
