k-space

mri · part two · the encoding

Part one ended on a problem. The sensor is one coil of wire; it integrates the entire body into a single time-varying voltage and has no spatial resolution whatsoever. An MRI scanner is a one-pixel camera. Every pixel you have ever seen in an MR image was manufactured by making the precession frequency depend on where you are.

1 · Frequency becomes position

Switch on a gradient — a field that varies linearly across the bore — and a spin at position x precesses at γ̄·G·x. Now the single voltage the coil induces is a sum of every location's contribution, each at its own frequency:

S(t) = ∫ ρ(r) · exp(−2πi k(t)·r) d²r     where     k(t) = γ̄ ∫₀ᵗ G(τ) dτ

Which is to say: the voltage at time t is one sample of the object's Fourier transform, at whatever spatial frequency the gradients have driven you to. The gradients are a steering wheel, k is where the car is, and the readout is a drive through frequency space. Twieg and Ljunggren, independently in 1983, are the people who put it that way — and once you do, reconstruction is an inverse Fourier transform and nothing else. No model, no prior, no network. Sixty lines of Cooley–Tukey.

3 · The middle is the picture, the edges are the detail

k-space is not a picture of anything. Its centre holds the low spatial frequencies — the bulk, the contrast, where the signal energy is — and its edges hold the high ones, which is to say the boundaries. Delete a part and see what that part was carrying. Paint on it directly, or take a preset.

The centre of k-space is a few per cent of the samples and almost all of the signal energy. That imbalance is what every acceleration method in MRI is exploiting.

4 · Sample too coarsely and the body folds onto itself

The spacing between k-space samples, Δk, sets the field of view: FOV = 1/Δk. Skip every other line and you have doubled Δk, halved the FOV, and anything outside it does not vanish — it wraps, and lands on top of the anatomy that does fit. The furthest sample, k_max, sets the resolution: Δx = 1/2k_max. Both are the sampling theorem, not a failure of the reconstruction; the transform is doing exactly what it was asked.

Turn the undersample slider in §2 up and watch it happen. This is also the point where modern MRI earns its keep: parallel imaging — SENSE, GRAPPA — un-folds those copies using the fact that a coil array has several sensors with different spatial sensitivities. Which is the correction to part one: the sensor has no spatial resolution *individually*, and an array of them has a little, and that little is enough to fill in the lines you didn't acquire.

5 · When you visit a point of k-space, not just whether

Here are two acquisitions that cover exactly the same k-space samples, of the same object, with the same off-resonance. The only difference is the order and the clock.

Spin-warp excites once per line, so its phase clock restarts sixty-four times and never accumulates. Single-shot EPI excites once for the entire image, so a spin that is off-resonance by Δf keeps gaining phase for the whole readout — and since the phase-encode axis is sampled only once per echo spacing, its bandwidth is thousands of times narrower than the readout's. The image slides along that axis by Δf · N · esp pixels. This is why EPI brains come out bent near the sinuses, and why fat lands somewhere its owner is not. At 3 T, fat precesses about 440 Hz from water; take the slider there and watch a whole head slide off its own outline.

The engine's test suite asserts this rather than illustrating it: it reconstructs, measures the image's centroid, and compares it with Δf · N · esp. Agreement is better than a tenth of a pixel across the range. And spin-warp's shift, for the same Δf, comes out at about four thousandths of a pixel.

6 · What the speed costs

Spin-warp waits a full repetition time — hundreds of milliseconds — for each of its lines, so a 128-line image takes about a minute. EPI takes all 128 lines after one excitation, in about 65 milliseconds: roughly a thousand times faster. That single fact is why functional MRI and diffusion imaging exist at all; you cannot watch a brain respond to a stimulus at one image a minute. And the price is everything in §5 — the distortion, the T₂* blur, the sensitivity to every imperfection in the field, all of which follow from the clock never restarting.

Where this stops. Reconstruction here is an inverse FFT and nothing more. Real scanners add coil-array combination, parallel-imaging unfolding, off-resonance correction and, lately, learned reconstruction. The radial mode grids with a nearest-neighbour kernel rather than a proper Kaiser–Bessel one, so some of its softness is the shortcut rather than the physics — the code says so where it does it. Contrast — what makes tissues differ rather than where they are — is a third part, and is not written.

Where this comes from

  • Lauterbur PC (1973). Image formation by induced local interactions: examples employing nuclear magnetic resonance. Nature 242:190–191. A gradient makes frequency mean position. Everything on this page is downstream of two pages.
  • Mansfield P & Grannell PK (1973). NMR "diffraction" in solids? J Phys C 6:L422–L426. The same idea from the reciprocal-space side, independently and simultaneously.
  • Twieg DB (1983). The k-trajectory formulation of the NMR imaging process. Med Phys 10:610–621. k-space named and formalised: the pulse sequence is a trajectory. §1 and §2 are this paper.
  • Ljunggren S (1983). A simple graphical representation of Fourier-based imaging methods. J Magn Reson 54:338–343. The same formulation, independently. Cite both.
  • Edelstein WA, Hutchison JMS, Johnson G & Redpath T (1980). Spin warp NMR imaging and applications to human whole-body imaging. Phys Med Biol 25:751–756. Constant-duration, variable-amplitude phase encoding — the robust practical form, and the default in §2.
  • Mansfield P (1977). Multi-planar image formation using NMR spin echoes. J Phys C 10:L55–L58. EPI: all of k-space in one shot. The thousand-fold speed-up and every artefact in §5.
  • Shepp LA & Logan BF (1974). The Fourier reconstruction of a head section. IEEE Trans Nucl Sci 21:21–43. The phantom. Its interior sits at ±0.01 against a 1.0 background because the paper's point was that low contrast is the hard case; the contrast-boosted variant here is Toft's (1996), and the engine carries both.
  • Guerquin-Kern M, Lejeune L, Pruessmann KP & Unser M (2012). Realistic analytical phantoms for parallel magnetic resonance imaging. IEEE Trans Med Imaging 31:626–636. Why the k-space here is analytic instead of an FFT of a picture: the inverse crime, and how to avoid it.
  • Pruessmann KP, Weiger M, Scheidegger MB & Boesiger P (1999). SENSE: sensitivity encoding for fast MRI. Magn Reson Med 42:952–962. Un-folding §4's aliasing with a coil array — the correction to part one's "the sensor has no spatial resolution".
  • Griswold MA et al. (2002). Generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions (GRAPPA). Magn Reson Med 47:1202–1210. The k-space-domain counterpart, and the one in widest clinical use.
