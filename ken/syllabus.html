A Curriculum in the Measurement of Delegated Work

Six units, with the primary sources, the model to master, and the experiment to reproduce in each

Standing document · revised as units are completed
Abstract

The problem of knowing whether unattended work was any good is not new and not unstudied; it is old, rigorous, and scattered across five fields that do not cite each other. This curriculum assembles it. Unit I takes the measurement of unobservable quantities from psychometrics, where it has been formalised for a century. Unit II takes the design of experiments, including the sequential designs that a budgeted loop needs. Unit III takes the economics of delegation under unobservable effort, which supplies the only closed-form model in the sequence and, with it, an exact prediction of the failure this programme has already suffered. Unit IV takes proxy failure — Goodhart's law, made quantitative. Unit V takes comparative judgment, which is how a judge becomes an instrument rather than an opinion. Unit VI takes scalable oversight: what remains possible when the supervised party is more capable than the supervisor.

Each unit names a small number of primary sources, one mathematical model to be understood well enough to derive, and one foundational experiment to be reproduced from scratch. The replications are deliberately cheap: five of the six need no model calls and no budget, only an afternoon and an honest plot.

0How to use this

Read in order. The dependencies are real: Unit IV's formalisation of proxy failure is unreadable without Unit I's distinction between a construct and its indicators, and Unit VI's oversight results are just anecdotes without Unit V's machinery for turning verdicts into a scale with error bars.

Each unit is done when its replication runs and its result is written up — not when its reading is finished. The replication is the exam. Where a unit's replication touches this repository's own loop data, it is stated; those can be run today, at zero model cost, because the data already exists.

On the shape of this field

There is no department, no canonical textbook, and no agreed name. What there is instead is five mature literatures whose intersection nobody has occupied. That is the opportunity and it is also the hazard: nothing here will tell you that you have assembled the pieces correctly, and the failure mode of an autodidact in a pre-paradigmatic field is to reinvent, badly, something that was settled in 1955. The reading lists below are weighted toward old papers for exactly that reason.

Unit I

The measurement of unobservables

≈ 5 weeks

If quality cannot be observed directly, on what grounds may a number be said to measure it?

This is the oldest rigorous part of the field and the part most completely ignored by current practice. Psychometrics has spent a hundred years on precisely one problem: inferring an unobservable quantity from fallible indicators, and establishing when you are entitled to. Spearman began it by noticing that the correlations among unrelated school subjects had a structure that implied a common cause nobody could measure. Everything since is the discipline of not fooling yourself about that inference.

The load-bearing idea is construct validity : a measure is not validated by being precise, reproducible, or agreed upon. It is validated by an accumulating argument that it stands in the relations to other things that the construct would stand in. Campbell and Fiske sharpen this into a two-sided test — a measure must converge with other measures of the same construct and diverge from measures of different ones. Almost every published evaluation of agent output tests neither.

Primary reading
  1. Cronbach & Meehl, Construct validity in psychological tests . The unit's centre. Short, and it will change how you read every benchmark paper afterwards.
  2. Campbell & Fiske, the multitrait-multimethod matrix . Convergent and discriminant validation. The design that catches a measure of "how long the output is" wearing a quality costume.
  3. Spearman , for the founding argument; Lord & Novick ch. 2–4 for classical test theory stated properly. Skim Spearman for the move, study Lord & Novick for the algebra.
  4. Cronbach on coefficient α , then Krippendorff ch. 11 and Cohen on agreement. α measures internal consistency, not validity. Learn the distinction here rather than in a review.
  5. Messick on consequential validity. Where the act of measuring changes the thing measured — the bridge into Unit IV.
  6. Optional, if the unit goes well: Rasch ; Embretson & Reise . Item response theory. The right frame if you ever want a gate whose difficulty is calibrated rather than asserted.
The model to master

Classical test theory, and reliability as a variance ratio. An observed score decomposes into a true score and independent error:

X = T + E,   E[E] = 0,   Cov(T, E) = 0 so that σ²X = σ²T + σ²E

Reliability is the fraction of observed variance that is signal:

ρXX′ = σ²T ⁄ σ²X equivalently, the correlation between two parallel measurements

Master three consequences. First, attenuation: the observed correlation between two unreliable measures is bounded by √(ρXX′ρYY′), so a weak judge–human correlation may indicate a noisy judge rather than a wrong one — and you cannot tell which without knowing both reliabilities. Second, the Spearman–Brown relation: lengthening a test raises reliability predictably, which prices the question "how many rubric items do we need?". Third, and most immediately: a measure with a ceiling has σ²T crushed toward zero among the cases near the top, so its reliability collapses exactly where you needed it — among your best turns.

The replication

A rater study on our own data, and the ceiling it will find. Draw a sample of loop artifacts. Write a five-item rubric. Have three raters score every artifact independently — at least one of them human, at least one of them you. Then compute, from scratch and not from a library: inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff's α, which tolerates a ragged panel), Cronbach's α across the five items, and the item–total correlation of each item.

The findings you are looking for are unflattering and specific. Any item whose item–total correlation is near zero is measuring something else and should be cut. If α across items is very high, the five items are one item and you have not measured five things. And the existing probes signal — which took three distinct values across eighty-nine turns and sat at its maximum in two thirds of them — should be shown, formally, to have almost no discriminating power in the region that matters. That demonstration is the unit's exam.

Unit II

The design of experiments, and when to stop

≈ 5 weeks

What must be arranged in advance for a comparison to license a conclusion — and how do you spend a fixed budget across it?

Fisher's insight was that randomisation is not a hygiene measure but the thing that creates the reference distribution against which a result is judged. Without it you have a story; with it you have a calculable probability. The lady tasting tea is the whole argument in one page, and it is still the cleanest introduction to inference ever written.

The second half of the unit is the part a budgeted loop actually needs. A run that checks its results as it goes and stops when they look good has an error rate far worse than it believes. Wald solved this in 1945; the sequential probability ratio test lets you stop early and keep an honest error rate, provided the stopping rule was fixed in advance. Then the allocation question — given a budget, which arm gets the next unit of it — is the bandit literature , of which the relevant chapter for us is best-arm identification rather than regret minimisation .

Primary reading
  1. Fisher, The Design of Experiments , ch. 1–3. Read the original, not a summary of it. It is short and unusually well written.
  2. Box, Hunter & Hunter , ch. 1–5, then the factorial chapters. The working text. Blocking, confounding, and factorial designs — which is how you test four gate settings without running four separate studies.
  3. Neyman and Rubin on potential outcomes. The vocabulary for saying precisely what a causal claim claims.
  4. Wald on sequential tests. The budget governor's missing theory. Read at least the setup and the error-rate argument.
  5. Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn ; Ioannidis ; Nosek et al. . Researcher degrees of freedom, and the remedy. Read the first immediately before writing your own analysis plan; it is uncomfortable and it should be.
  6. Student , for the pleasure of it. A brewer solving a small-sample problem because his employer forbade him to publish under his own name.
The model to master

Randomisation inference, then power. The permutation test needs no distributional assumption at all: under the null that treatment does nothing, the observed assignment is one of the C(n, k) equally likely assignments, and the p-value is the fraction of them giving a statistic at least as extreme. For the tea experiment, eight cups and four of each:

P(all four correct | H₀) = 1 ⁄ C(8,4) = 1⁄70 ≈ 0.014

Then the design question, which is where most studies are lost before they start. For a two-arm comparison of means at significance α and power 1−β, the required sample per arm is approximately

n ≈ 2(z1−α/2 + z1−β)² ⁄ d² d = the standardised effect size you care about detecting

Master this well enough to run it backwards: given the budget you actually have, what is the smallest effect the study could detect? If that number is larger than any effect you believe in, the study is not worth running, and knowing this beforehand is the single highest-return skill in the unit.

The replication

The lady tasting tea, exactly — then our own power analysis. Implement the exact null distribution by enumeration, not simulation and not a library call: all 70 assignments, the statistic on each, the p-value by counting. Confirm 1/70. Then extend it honestly: recompute for six cups and for ten, and see how fast the smallest achievable p-value moves. This is why the eight-cup design was chosen and it is a design decision, not a detail.

Then turn the machinery on the programme itself. Given a turn budget, and a quality measure with the reliability estimated in Unit I, what is the smallest per-turn improvement the curve could detect? Produce the number before running anything. If it is embarrassing, that is the unit working correctly.

Unit III

Delegation under unobservable effort

≈ 6 weeks

What contract should a principal offer an agent whose effort cannot be seen, only inferred from a noisy outcome?

This is the unit that unifies the two halves of the subject, and the reason to place project management and alignment on one continuum rather than at two ends of a ladder. Both are instances of the principal–agent problem: a principal wants outcomes, an agent chooses actions, and the principal observes a signal that is informative about those actions but not equal to them. Everything interesting follows from that gap .

Two results carry the unit. Holmström's informativeness principle : any signal that carries incremental information about the agent's effort should enter the contract, however noisy — which is a formal argument against reducing evaluation to one headline metric, and in favour of the multiple imperfect signals that Unit I taught you to combine. And then the result this programme has already paid for:

When an agent's effort is divided among several tasks and only some of them are measurable, effort flows to the measured tasks. The optimal response is frequently to weaken the incentive on the measurable task rather than to strengthen it — and where the unmeasured task matters enough, to offer no performance incentive at all.

the multitask result, Holmström & Milgrom 1991

Baker completes the picture by separating two distinct defects a metric can have: it can be noisy, which costs the agent risk, and it can be distorted, meaning that maximising it does not maximise value. These call for opposite remedies, and confusing them is the most common error in gate design. Kerr is the same lesson without algebra, and the right thing to hand a colleague. Gibbons and Prendergast survey the field.

Primary reading
  1. Holmström, Moral Hazard and Observability . The informativeness principle. Derive it; do not merely read it.
  2. Holmström & Milgrom, Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses . The centrepiece of the entire curriculum. §2 is the part to know cold.
  3. Holmström & Milgrom for why linear contracts are optimal in the tractable setting. The justification for the model below. Heavy; take the result if the proof resists.
  4. Baker on distortion versus risk.
  5. Kerr ; Gibbons . Read Kerr first if the formalism is cold. It is four pages and it is devastating.
  6. Ross ; Grossman & Hart ; Prendergast . Origins and survey. Reference rather than front-to-back.
The model to master

The linear-exponential-normal (LEN) model. The one closed-form model in this curriculum, and worth knowing by heart. The agent exerts effort e at cost ½ce²; output is observed with noise, x = e + ε with ε ∼ N(0, σ²); the contract is linear, w = α + βx; the agent has constant absolute risk aversion r. Maximising certainty equivalent gives effort e* = β/c, and the principal's optimal incentive intensity is

β* = 1 ⁄ (1 + r c σ²)

Read what it says. Incentive intensity should fall as the measure gets noisier (σ² up), as the agent is more risk-averse (r up), and as effort gets more costly to vary (c up). A noisy gate should be weighted less, not compensated for by being weighted more — which is the opposite of what a failing loop usually does to itself.

Now extend to two tasks with efforts e₁, e₂, where only task 1 is measurable and the cost function has a positive cross-partial (the tasks compete for the same effort). The optimal β₁ is pushed below its single-task value, and as task 2's marginal value rises, β₁ → 0. Derive that. It is the exam question, and it is also a formal statement of why turns 36–50 of our own loop produced seven backend modules and no page.

The replication

Solve the LEN optimum numerically, then reproduce the multitask incentive-weakening result. Implement the single-task model, verify β* = 1/(1 + rcσ²) against a numerical optimiser, and plot β* against σ². Then build the two-task version and produce the paper's qualitative result: the surface of optimal β₁ over (value of task 2, cost cross-partial), showing the region where the optimum is to switch the measured incentive off entirely.

Finish by fitting the model to us. Take task 1 as "passes the gates", task 2 as "a stranger would want to use it", estimate what the loop's implied β₁ was, and state what the model says it should have been. This is the first point in the curriculum where the theory makes a falsifiable retrodiction about our own run.

Unit IV

Proxy failure, made quantitative

≈ 4 weeks

Exactly how, and how fast, does optimising a measure destroy its relationship to the thing it measured?

Goodhart's original remark was narrow and about monetary aggregates; Campbell stated the general version better and earlier than the slogan suggests, and Strathern supplied the familiar phrasing along with an ethnographer's account of what a measured institution turns into. The essential modern move is Manheim and Garrabrant's : "Goodhart's law" names at least four distinct mechanisms, and they have different fixes.

The one to internalise first is regressional Goodhart, because it requires no adversary, no gaming, and no intent — only noise and selection. If a proxy equals the true quantity plus independent noise, then selecting the top-scoring item selects partly for quality and partly for luck, and the expected true value of the winner falls short of its measured value by an amount that grows with selection pressure. Nothing has gone wrong. Everyone behaved correctly. The number still lies.

Then the quantitative anchor for this entire programme: Gao, Schulman and Hilton measure gold-standard quality as a function of how hard a proxy is optimised, and fit functional forms in which quality rises, peaks, and then declines with continued optimisation. Our own question — does turn 20 improve on turn 19 — is a special case of theirs, with turn count standing in for optimisation pressure. Pan, Bhatia and Steinhardt add the alarming refinement that the transition can be abrupt rather than gradual. Skalse et al. give "unhackable" a formal definition and show how restrictive it is. Amodei et al. §3 stated the agenda in 2016; Krakovna's specimen list is the cabinet of examples to read before designing any gate.

Primary reading
  1. Manheim & Garrabrant . The taxonomy. Learn to diagnose which of the four you have; the remedies do not transfer.
  2. Gao, Schulman & Hilton . The quantitative core of the unit and the closest published relative of our own study.
  3. Campbell ; Strathern ; Goodhart . The originals, in decreasing order of usefulness and increasing order of fame.
  4. Pan, Bhatia & Steinhardt ; Skalse et al. .
  5. Amodei et al. §3; Krakovna et al. . Agenda and specimens. The example list is genuinely funny and should still change your gate design.
The model to master

Regressional Goodhart, as an order statistic. Let the true quality be T and the measured proxy P = T + E with E independent noise. Select the best of n candidates by P. Then the selected item's expected true quality is

E[T | P = max] = E[P | P = max] · σ²T ⁄ (σ²T + σ²E) in the jointly normal case — the measured score, shrunk by the reliability

Note what the shrinkage factor is: it is exactly the reliability from Unit I. The two units are the same algebra seen from different ends, and that is the moment the curriculum locks together. As n grows, the measured maximum keeps climbing while the true value behind it flattens — the gap between what your gate reports and what you actually got widens with every additional candidate you screen. No malice required.

The replication

Produce the overoptimisation curve on a synthetic, with no models involved. Draw T from a normal; form P = T + E at several noise levels; for each n in a geometric sweep, select best-of-n by P and record the mean true quality of the winner. Plot true quality against log n for each noise level.

You will get the qualitative shape of the published curves — rise, flatten, and, once you let the proxy be distorted rather than merely noisy (make E correlate negatively with T beyond some threshold), turn over and fall. Then state the result in the form that matters: for our measured reliability, at what selection pressure does additional optimisation stop buying real quality? That number is a budget policy, derived rather than guessed.

Unit V

Judgment as an instrument

≈ 4 weeks

How do you convert a pile of subjective verdicts into a scale with standard errors, and how do you establish that the judge is measuring the work rather than its length?

Thurstone established that people are far better at "which of these two" than at "score this out of ten", and gave the model that turns the former into the latter. Bradley and Terry supplied the form still in use; Elo made it famous in a setting where the predictions get checked every weekend.

The modern half is a warning. Zheng et al. document that model judges exhibit position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias, and — crucially — report judge agreement against the human–human agreement ceiling rather than against an imagined perfect standard. That comparison is the correct one and is usually omitted. Dubois et al. then show the professional response to a known bias: measure it, and regress it out. You do not ask a biased instrument to stop being biased; you characterise the bias and correct for it, which is what Unit I would have told you.

Primary reading
  1. Bradley & Terry ; Thurstone . The model and its psychophysical motivation. Both short.
  2. Zheng et al. . The methods and limitations sections are the template for calibrating our own judge. Note how they report agreement.
  3. Dubois et al. . Debiasing by regression. The most directly copyable technique in the unit.
  4. Chiang & Lee ; Elo .
The model to master

Bradley–Terry, fitted by hand. Each item i has a latent strength θᵢ, and

P(i beats j) = eθᵢ ⁄ (eθᵢ + eθⱼ) equivalently, logistic in the strength difference θᵢ − θⱼ

Fit by maximum likelihood — the minorisation–maximisation update converges in a few dozen iterations and is about forty lines. Get standard errors from the observed information, and note the structural fact that a comparison graph which is not connected has no unique fit: an item nobody compared to anything has no rating, and your scheduler must be told to keep the graph connected.

Then extend it the way Unit IV demands: add covariates. A Bradley–Terry model with a length term estimates the strength difference holding output length fixed, which is how you find out whether your judge was ever measuring anything else.

The replication

Fit Bradley–Terry to real verdicts, then measure your judge's position bias. Take a set of artifacts, generate pairwise comparisons, fit the model, and produce a ranking with error bars. Then run the bias experiment from : present every pair twice, in both orders, and measure the rate at which the verdict flips. A judge that flips on a material fraction of swapped pairs has an effect size of "which slot the answer was in" that you can estimate — and compare against the effect size of the quality difference you were trying to detect.

The unit passes when you can state your judge's agreement with human raters as a fraction of the human–human agreement ceiling, and not before. If the humans only agree with each other 75% of the time, a judge at 72% is close to the measurement limit and further tuning is chasing noise.

Unit VI

Oversight beyond your ken

≈ 6 weeks

What supervision remains possible when the supervised party is more capable, at the task, than the supervisor?

This is where the curriculum's two halves meet, and where the ordinary management intuition turns out to be the technically load-bearing one. The reason supervision is possible at all is an asymmetry: judging is easier than doing. A manager need not out-code the engineer; a referee need not out-play the players. Wherever verification is cheaper than generation, a weaker party can supervise a stronger one — and the research programme consists of finding how far that asymmetry stretches and what to do where it snaps.

Christiano et al. is the hinge: it takes the comparative judgment of Unit V and uses it as a training signal, so that a supervisor who cannot demonstrate the task can still shape it. Debate and amplification are two proposals for stretching the asymmetry deliberately; self-critique is the most immediately usable, since critiques demonstrably help humans find flaws they would otherwise miss.

The unit's experimental centre is Bowman et al. and the sandwiching design: place the system between a non-expert supervisor and a ground-truth expert, and measure whether the non-expert, working with the system, reaches the expert's answer. It converts a philosophical worry into a measurable quantity, it is the only design here that directly tests "management beyond your ken", and it is runnable at our scale. Burns et al. examine the case where the supervisor is definitively weaker; Bai et al. show a working system built on the asymmetry.

Primary reading
  1. Bowman et al. . The sandwiching paradigm. The unit's experimental template; read the design section twice.
  2. Christiano et al. . Preference learning. The bridge from Unit V, and the reason Unit V comes first.
  3. Amodei et al. §4 on scalable supervision.
  4. Saunders et al. ; Burns et al. . The two most empirically grounded results in the unit.
  5. Irving et al. ; Christiano et al. ; Michael et al. ; Bai et al. . Proposals and one deployed system. Read for the shape of the argument.
The model to master

The verification–generation gap, stated as a decision problem. Let a supervisor's probability of correctly ranking two outputs be a function of the quality gap between them and of the supervisor's own competence. Supervision succeeds when that probability stays above chance in the region where the candidate outputs actually differ. Formalise it, then notice the two ways it fails: the gap shrinks (all candidates are near-equally good, and the judge is reading noise — Unit I's ceiling, again), or the supervisor's discrimination decays faster than the quality difference grows.

This is not a settled model with a canonical citation, and you should treat building one as part of the unit's work rather than as background. It is also the most likely place in this curriculum to produce something genuinely new, because the measurement-theoretic framing of Units I and V is not standard in the oversight literature.

The replication

A sandwiching trial, on a domain where you are honestly the non-expert. Choose a task in this repository where ground truth exists and you do not personally have it — a proof in the geometry pack, a claim in the crypto stack, a numerical result with a published reference. Establish the expert answer independently. Then measure three conditions: you alone, you assisted by the system, and the system alone. The quantity of interest is the fraction of the gap between "you alone" and "expert" that the assisted condition closes.

Report it even if it is negative. A trial where assistance made you more confidently wrong is the most valuable result the unit can produce, and it is the one this curriculum exists to be able to recognise.

7What the sequence adds up to

At the end, the claim you are entitled to make is narrow and worth having: that you can state what quality means for a class of delegated work, measure it with a known reliability, detect a stated effect size with a stated power, recognise the point at which further optimisation stops buying real quality, and supervise work you could not have produced yourself — with a number attached to how far that last one stretches.

Nobody is going to hand you that. The five parent literatures are mature and the intersection is empty, which is the only reason a project of this size is worth attempting from outside an institution.

The order is not negotiable, but the pace is

Thirty weeks is the honest estimate at a serious part-time pace, and units III and VI will each take longer than budgeted. Units I, II and IV can be compressed if the replications are done and the reading is skimmed; units III and V cannot, because their models must be derivable rather than merely recognised. If the schedule slips, cut Unit VI's proposals (debate, amplification) and keep its replication.
