The Harness

A design calculator, and what it says about the runs we have already paid for

ken/lab/design.mjs · 92 known-answer checks · node-only, not served
Abstract

Replication is the only defence against model variance and replication is what costs money, so the useful work happens before any of it is spent. This note describes a small node library that answers three questions offline: how many runs an effect of a given size would need, what the smallest detectable effect is for a budget already fixed, and which design changes make the same claim cheaper.

Applied to the bake-offs, it gives a number worth having early. With two samples per cell, a two-cell contrast could only have detected a difference of about 2.8 standard deviations, which is the gap between total success and total failure. It also produces a result that runs against intuition and against how the existing rig is usually described: for a fixed run budget, repeats do not buy precision. They buy a variance estimate. Beating the noise takes more distinct tasks.

1Scope and coverage

The library sits at ken/lab/design.mjs, imports its statistics from the shared packages/dataviz rather than reimplementing them, and is covered by design.selftest.mjs, which checks 92 values that can be derived by hand or looked up. The standard it is held to is borrowed from bakeoff/CLAUDE.md, which puts it well: a rubric nobody has watched fail is not evidence of anything. So every assertion in that file is an independently known answer rather than a snapshot of what the code returned: z(0.975) = 1.959964, C(8,4) = 70, and a variance decomposition worked out longhand.

Three questions, in the order they are usually needed:

  1. Given a budget, what could this run possibly detect? Nearly always the more useful direction, and the one that stops a study before it wastes anything.
  2. Given an effect worth detecting, what would it cost?
  3. What design change makes the same claim cheaper? This has real answers, and they are worth more than any amount of careful reporting afterwards.

2Power of the bake-off design

Six cells with two samples each gives twelve runs, so a contrast between any two cells has two observations per arm. Feeding that to mde():

So the twenty-three-of-twenty-three result recorded in the two bake-offs has a second reading alongside the saturated gate described in the house standard. Even with a perfectly discriminating instrument, that design had no power to separate the cells. Both problems were present, and fixing the gate alone would not have produced a comparison.

What this does not say

None of this shows the bake-offs were a mistake. Their stated purpose was a floor and a side-by-side arena for a person to judge, and two samples per cell was chosen deliberately so that within-cell spread could be seen at all, which §4 below shows was the right call for that purpose. The claim here is narrower: those runs could not have supported a statistical comparison between cells, and nothing in the reports says otherwise or claims they could.

3The cost of a comparison

The second question, and the one that makes the balance concrete. Pairing is the lever: run every condition on the same tasks, and whatever makes a task hard cancels out of the difference. The required observations fall by a factor of (1 − ρ), where ρ is the correlation between conditions on a shared task.

Read the d = 0.5 row. Unpaired, a medium effect needs 126 runs. Paired at a plausible ρ = 0.7, it needs 38. At ρ = 0.85, which is not unreasonable when both conditions attempt the same brief, it needs 20, which is inside what the bake-offs already spend. The same claim, a sixth of the bill, from a design choice that costs nothing to make.

The two rows above it are the warning. Small effects are brutally expensive whatever you do, and a programme that needs to detect d = 0.2 should decide that in advance rather than discover it after spending. Which is the argument for asking question one first.

4Repeats, tasks, and where precision comes from

This one surprised me, and it is the reason the note exists rather than just the library.

The intuition that running the same prompt into the same situation several times should beat the noise is half right. Write the variance of a condition mean under an allocation of tasks × repeats:

Var(mean) = σ²between ⁄ tasks + σ²within ⁄ (tasks · repeats) and with the run budget fixed at R = tasks · repeats, the second term is σ²within ⁄ R

The second term does not depend on the split at all. Every run spent repeating a task you already have is a run not spent on a task you do not, so maximising tasks wins at every value of the intraclass correlation. allocate() returns one repeat as the optimum at ICC 0.1, 0.35, 0.6 and 0.9 alike, and the selftest asserts it at each.

The paired two-condition version reaches the same place. Substituting tasks = R/(2·repeats) into the variance of a paired difference gives a quantity increasing in repeats, flat only when the task-by-condition interaction is exactly zero.

What repeats do buy is the variance estimate itself. At one repeat per task the within-task degrees of freedom are zero and σ²within is not estimable, so you have no idea how noisy the model is and no input for any of the calculations above. allocate() therefore returns the optimum and a finding string saying what that optimum costs, and flags varianceEstimable: false.

This also settles a question the existing rig raised. Two samples per cell is not a way of averaging out noise, and would be a poor one if it were. It is a way of seeing the noise, and bakeoff/CLAUDE.md justifies it in exactly those terms: the spread within one cell across two runs can exceed the gap between two models, and one draw cannot tell you which you are looking at. Right call, right reason.

5The variance pilot

Not a comparison. A variance pilot: one condition, several tasks, several repeats, which produces the σ² components every calculation above takes as input. Until it exists, every cost estimate here rests on an assumed ρ and should be read as a range.

Feed the result to varianceComponents(), which returns σ²within, σ²between and the ICC, clamping a negative between-task moment estimate at zero because a negative variance is not a finding. That ICC then sets ρ in Table 2, and the cost of every claim the programme wants to make becomes a number rather than a worry.

6Sequential stopping and exact tests

A run that watches its own results and stops when they look good has an error rate far worse than it believes. Wald's sequential test gives boundaries on the accumulated log-likelihood ratio that permit early stopping while holding the stated error rates, provided the rule was fixed in advance. sprtBounds({ alpha: 0.05, beta: 0.2 }) returns an upper boundary of ln 16 ≈ +2.77 and a lower of ≈ −1.56.

The library also carries the exact randomisation machinery from Unit II's replication: ladyTastingTea() enumerates the null distribution rather than simulating it, and permutationTest() refuses above a stated assignment cap rather than silently switching to sampling, because a p-value from an undisclosed Monte Carlo is a different object. The design lesson falls straight out of the enumeration: at six cups the smallest achievable p-value is exactly 0.05, so six cups cannot produce a significant result at the conventional threshold and eight is the smallest design that can.

7Known gaps

  • Every ρ here is assumed. Nothing in the repository has measured a task-level correlation between two conditions, so Table 2 is a menu rather than a quotation. The pilot in §5 is what turns it into one.
  • Normal approximation throughout. Sample sizes run a little light at small n; the exact: true flag adds the conventional correction rather than pretending to more precision than a normal approximation has.
  • No cost model in tokens or dollars. costToDetect() takes a costPerRun and refuses to assume one. Bake-off cells ran between about 800 and 5,100 seconds of wall clock, which is too wide a spread to convert into a unit price without measuring it.
  • No runner yet. This is the design half. Executing a design against real cells still goes through bakeoff/, and connecting the two is the next piece of work.
