the yen leash

why washington would step into someone else's currency — modeled stage by stage

Short version: probably not imports, and not really oil — those move an exchange rate over months, not in a single "historic" intervention. The urgent reason lives in a loop that starts with a trillion-dollar bet built on cheap yen, and ends in the US government's own borrowing costs.

For years, investors could borrow yen for almost nothing, convert it to dollars, and buy higher-yielding US assets — Treasuries, tech stocks, anything paying more than Japan's near-zero rates. That's the yen carry trade, and by most estimates it's worth trillions. It only works while the interest gap holds and the yen doesn't move against it too fast.

In August 2024 the Bank of Japan raised rates and the yen snapped stronger in days. That erased the trade's cushion for a lot of investors at once, so they unwound together — sold the US assets, bought back yen to repay the loans — and it hit global equities on the way through. That's the real precedent for what "historic intervention" is trying to prevent: not a slow drift, a stampede.

The part that pulls the US in directly: Japan is the largest single foreign holder of US Treasuries, north of a trillion dollars of them. Japan's usual tool for defending a falling yen is to sell dollar assets — often those Treasuries — and buy yen with the proceeds. Fine at small scale. At the size a real currency crisis would require, that selling itself pushes US bond yields up, which is a Tokyo currency problem landing as an American borrowing-cost problem. The loop closes.

Stepping in early, with its own resources, is how the US avoids reaching that step. The Treasury's actual intervention fund — the Exchange Stabilization Fund — holds a mix of dollars, euros and IMF reserve assets for exactly this kind of move, so financing it partly by rotating other reserve holdings, euros included, is a plausible piece of the mechanism, not a stretch.

The import-cost read isn't wrong, it's just downstream: a stronger yen does make everything Japan imports cheaper, oil included, and that matters more with a war pushing energy prices around. But that's a slow-burn benefit that shows up over quarters. It's not what makes an intervention "historic" on the day it happens — the carry-trade unwind and the Treasury-selling loop are the fast-moving reasons, and the cheaper oil is closer to a side effect.

what this leaves out

This is a five-node teaching model, not the real transmission mechanism, which also runs through the US–Japan rate differential itself, other central banks' own positioning, general risk-appetite at the time, and — not modeled here at all — the politics of whether G7 partners would back the move publicly the way they did in 2022. Treat every number above as "which direction, roughly how much," not as a prediction of what any real intervention would move.
