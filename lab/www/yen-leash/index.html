the yen leash

why washington would step into someone else's currency — modeled stage by stage

Short version: probably not imports, and not really oil — those move an exchange rate over months, not in a single "historic" intervention. The urgent reason is a trillion-dollar bet built on cheap yen unwinding all at once, which pushes straight back into US borrowing costs. Everything below walks that chain, and the toggle underneath goes deeper on the bet itself.

For years, investors could borrow yen for almost nothing, convert it to dollars, and buy higher-yielding US assets — that's the yen carry trade, worth trillions by most estimates. Japan is also the largest single foreign holder of US Treasuries. Put those together and you get a loop: a falling yen pressures Japan to defend it by selling those Treasuries, large-scale selling pushes US yields up, and if the carry trade unwinds in a hurry — as it briefly did in August 2024 — that hits US markets too. A stronger yen also makes Japan's imports (oil included) cheaper, but that's a slow, months-long effect — not what makes an intervention "historic" on the day it happens.

the carry trade, properly

Is this "borrow infinite free money and dump it into the S&P 500"? Not quite, on both words. Not free: you still pay Japan's interest rate on the loan — it's just been close to zero, not zero, so the profit is the gap between that and what the dollar asset pays. And you're exposed to the exchange rate the whole time you hold the trade: if the yen strengthens against you, that loss can wipe out months of the interest-rate gain in days, which is exactly what forces everyone to sell at once. Not infinite either: it's borrowed, so it's capped by how much lenders and brokers will extend against your collateral, and that ceiling drops fast the moment the trade looks risky — which is part of why an unwind feeds on itself.

Why was Japan's interest rate so low for so long? Japan's asset bubble burst in the early 1990s and the economy spent three decades fighting deflation — prices flat or falling, which discourages spending and investment. The Bank of Japan cut its policy rate to effectively zero in 1999, later added large-scale bond buying, and in 2016 pushed the rate slightly negative while pinning 10-year government bond yields near zero by buying whatever it took to keep them there ("yield curve control"). All of it aimed at one goal: force inflation back up in an economy that couldn't generate any on its own.

Why is this changing now? Inflation finally showed up in Japan — partly the ordinary post-pandemic kind, partly imported, because a weak yen makes everything Japan buys from abroad (energy especially) more expensive, which itself pushes prices up. That let the BOJ end negative rates and yield curve control in March 2024 and start raising rates gradually — the same rate rise that triggered August 2024's carry-trade unwind. Every further hike narrows the US–Japan rate gap that made the trade profitable in the first place, so more of this is the mechanism working as intended, not a one-off shock.
