Honeyflow Chess

The board is a shallow pan of honey. Play a normal game — moving a piece doesn't just relocate it, it drags a laminar impulse through the fluid underneath, scaled to how far the piece travelled. The piece that made the move always lands exactly on its target square and stays there, immune to the very current it just released, for as long as it's the other side's move to make. Every OTHER occupied square is sampled against the live velocity field each frame and nudged along it, tethered back to its own square by the honey's stiffness — heavier pieces resist more, and the pull now takes a good few seconds to build, so there's real time to watch the field develop before anything commits to a new square. Drag a piece far enough off its square's centre and it doesn't just sway back: it actually slides into the neighbouring square, a real change to the position, not a wobble — blocked if that square is already occupied. High viscosity keeps everything creeping and smooth: no turbulence, no vortices, just honey settling back to rest. The grid stays put; the arrows plotted over it are a live vector field, sampled fresh each frame — not a sketched line — sized and coloured by local speed.

White to move

Move shapes follow standard chess (pawns, knights, bishops, rooks, queens, kings, forward promotion to queen). Check, checkmate, castling and en passant are not implemented — this board is about the fluid, not the tournament rules — so a king can be captured like anything else. A piece the current carries into a neighbouring square is a real, unannounced change to the position — it isn't your move, and it doesn't pass the turn — so glance at the board before you play, not just at whose turn it says.

Reduced motion is on: the fluid still simulates (it's the whole point of the page) but the vector field redraws less often, and pieces stay locked to their squares — no dragging, and no flow-carried slides — instead of moving in the current.
