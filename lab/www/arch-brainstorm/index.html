the mechanic, live

Tap empty foam to plant a Node (its cell gets carved out of its neighbours, with a random floor height — the bigger its dot and the brighter its cell, the higher). Tap a Node's dot to pull it (deconstruct — its cell is absorbed back into whichever Nodes are left). Tap an Edge — the line between two cells — to toggle it: solid means wall, dashed means you've opened it. But an open Edge is just an unlocked door; whether you can actually walk through it depends on the Edge's own grade — not the two rooms it separates, but the slope of the boundary segment itself, from one Vertex to the other: the height carried at each corner (blended from every cell that meets there), divided by the corner-to-corner distance, same rise-over-run a trail sign uses, measured along the threshold you'd actually cross. Over the slider's threshold below, gravity blocks the step regardless of the toggle, and the doorway renders as an amber hazard dash instead of a walkable white one — open, but a cliff. try2path runs after every change: it searches from the green source Node to the red sink Node using only Edges that are both open and under the grade threshold, and reports whether a route exists, how many hops, and the steepest grade the route actually climbs. You're on the map too — the yellow dot is a guy standing right on this same tiling, and gravity pulls him straight toward the bottom of this picture, not toward some node's floor number in a separate view. He falls through any open, walkable Edge below him, same rule as everything else here — but he isn't only falling: hold climb (▲, W, or the up arrow) and he walks straight up through any open, walkable Edge above him too, fighting gravity for as long as that Edge allows it. Move him left/right with the arrow keys, A/D, or the close-up window below, which zooms in 10x on him alone and has its own left/climb/right buttons; there's still no jump — no arc through open air — so a route needs an actual open, walkable Edge at every step, level or climbed, not a gap hopped over.

Foam & Seam

an architecture brainstorm, not a pitch deck

The ask: a big puzzle platformer where the level itself is a Voronoi foam, modelled as three entity classes — a Node is the centre of a cell, a Vertex is a point equidistant from a set of Nodes (where three or more cells meet), and an Edge is the wall between two Vertices, shared by exactly two Nodes' cells. The puzzle mechanic is edge transparency: every wall starts solid, and you tap one to make it walkable, trying to open a route from a source cell to a sink cell without the whole foam collapsing into one undifferentiated room. Now there is gravity, too — and it belongs to the Edge, not the rooms either side of it. Every Node still has its own floor height (a room's floor is flat), but nobody walks the straight chord between two room centres — you cross the boundary between them — so it's that boundary's own slope, rise between its two Vertices over its own length, that decides whether a step is a stair or a cliff. Opening an Edge just unlocks the door; the door itself can still be too steep to use, whatever either room's floor height happens to be. Your tools are still only these two — plant/pull a Node, toggle an Edge — but a route now has to be both open and shallow enough along the threshold itself to actually walk. Below is a working miniature of exactly that, then the challenges it has to clear, then what it would take to give it Factorio's depth.

what this needs to solve

1. navigability is a moving target

Every Node planted or pulled reshapes neighbouring cells, and the change is not local: pulling one Node on the far side of the map can sever a corridor nowhere near it — same risk now sits one level down, in toggling a single Edge, since the sandbox above makes Edge transparency the primary lever rather than Node add/remove. A full game needs a live, legible signal for this (the try2path status line here is the crudest version of one), or players will thrash trying to hold a mental model the game already discarded.

2. an abstract diagram is not a platformer

A Voronoi cell is a convex polygon with edges at arbitrary angles. "Walkable floor" is a platformer concept; "cell boundary" is a geometry concept, and they don't map onto each other for free — and steepness in particular belongs to the boundary, not the rooms it separates: nobody walks the straight chord between two room centres, so a room's own floor height was never the figure of merit. The sandbox above now has a firmer version of the rule this needs — every Edge's grade is computed from its own two Vertices (each corner's height blended from every cell that meets there), rise over the boundary segment's own length, refused by gravity above the slider's threshold whether or not the doorway's unlocked. That's a real fix over an earlier version of this sandbox, which read grade off the two flanking rooms instead of the boundary itself — the two aren't the same number, since an Edge's corners are shared with whichever other cells happen to meet there too, not just the pair it divides. Still a graph-level stand-in, though, not an actual traversal aid (ladder, grapple point) for the boundaries gravity vetoes, which a real platformer would need instead of just forbidding the step.

3. recomputation has to be incremental

The sandbox recomputes every cell from scratch on every edit, which is fine at a few dozen seeds and falls over at a few hundred. A shipped game needs an incremental structure (an updatable Delaunay/beach-line, or a bounded-radius recompute that only touches cells near the edit) rather than a full rebuild each time — otherwise performance collapses exactly when the foam gets dense enough to be interesting.

4. deconstruction is a real-time physics question, not just a delete

Pulling a seed merges its cell into its neighbours, but who wins contested area, what an actor standing on the vanishing cell does mid- merge, and whether a half-absorbed cell is walkable during the transition are all questions with no single obvious answer — "delete the point" is the easy 10%, the transition is the other 90%.

5. legibility of a genuinely chaotic structure

Real Voronoi foam from scattered points is visually noisy — cells of wildly different size sitting next to each other, which is exactly what the sandbox above looks like once you add more than a handful of seeds. A puzzle needs the player to read state at a glance: what changed since my last move, where's safe, where's the goal. That likely means constraining placement (a coarser snap lattice, a minimum cell size) or a shading layer (age, type, distance-from-goal) rather than raw unconstrained scatter.

6. softlocks are a first-class risk

Because the player edits the very terrain they stand on, it's possible to wall yourself into a shrinking cell or sever the only exit — the two hardest questions in any terrain-editing puzzler are whether to block edits that would do this (validate against a live connectivity check before committing) or allow it and provide rewind. A hard-fail is much worse in a puzzle game than in a sandbox toy, so this isn't optional polish, it's a core design decision to make early.

7. edits need a cost, or it isn't a puzzle

Unconstrained "click anywhere to add a Node, tap any Edge to open it" — which is literally what the sandbox above offers — is a sandbox, not a puzzle: no scarcity, no wrong answer, every Edge can just be opened until source and sink are trivially connected. A puzzle needs edits to be moves with a budget (a fixed number of Node placements, a cost per Edge toggled open, a turn limit) so that a solution — the specific minimal set of Edges to open — is something to find rather than something to stumble into by tapping everywhere.

turning it into Factorio

Factorio's loop, reduced: extract → craft by hand → automate the hand-craft with a machine → chain machines into a line → optimise the line's throughput → unlock deeper chains → defend the base against the pressure that automation attracts. The question is what each step becomes when the "factory floor" is a shape you're also walking around in.

cells are resource nodes, not just rooms

Type each cell by a property of its own geometry — few neighbours and near the map edge yields raw ore, many-sided interior cells yield power, distance from a "vein" running through the foam sets richness. Planting a seed stops being pure terrain-sculpting and becomes prospecting: where you carve determines what you get, same as choosing where to drop a mining outpost.

the core move — automate what you just did by hand — becomes automating the foam-editing itself

Early game: the player manually plants and pulls seeds to solve one navigation puzzle at a time, exactly like the sandbox above. Mid-game: unlock a placeable seeder machine that inserts/removes sites by a rule ("keep exactly six neighbours around this vein," "grow toward the richest untapped region"), so the foam starts sculpting itself and the player's job shifts from solving one puzzle by hand to designing the rule that solves a whole class of them. That shift — from doing the thing to building the thing that does the thing — is the entire Factorio feeling, aimed at geometry instead of item flow.

production chains ride the cell walls

Instead of belts on a grid, a resource flows along a shared Voronoi edge between adjacent cells, and a production line is a path through the cell-adjacency graph — which is the exact same graph the platforming layer already needs for its own connectivity check (the one the sandbox above computes for the start/goal path). That's the load-bearing idea: the level's navigability graph is the factory's logistics graph, so laying pipe and laying a walkable path are the same action seen from two angles. It's also where Factorio's signature tension shows up for free — a belt running through the one room you also need to walk through.

the tech tree unlocks edit types, not items

Where Factorio's tree unlocks assemblers and belt tiers, this tree unlocks new classes of foam edit: batch-inserting several seeds at once, "protected" cells that resist a neighbour's deconstruction, directional growth rules, partial merges instead of atomic add/remove. Each new tool changes what shapes of foam — and therefore what puzzles and production layouts — become buildable, which is what keeps the mid-and-late game from being the same mechanic repeated at larger numbers.

base growth needs something pushing back

Factorio's biters punish unchecked expansion so automating everything and walking away is never quite safe. Here that pressure could be a rival automatic seeder eating into your foam from one edge — you either wall it off (spend edits defensively) or out-grow it (build a smarter rule than its rule) — which supplies the "why can't I just automate everything and relax" tension the genre depends on for pacing.

the payoff is the same shape, just geometric

Factorio's dopamine is watching a small hand-built line become a sprawling automated base you can still trace by eye. The foam version: a level that starts as the dozen cells you placed by hand in the sandbox above and ends as thousands, grown by rules you wrote, still legible because the shading/constraint work from challenge 5 scales with it — the base doesn't just get bigger, it stays readable, which is that challenge's answer turning into the genre's actual late-game pleasure.
