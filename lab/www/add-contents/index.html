Behind Iron Ledger

the notes one build agent leaves for the next one, published for people instead

What it is

Someone on Bluesky asked for a full train game — procedural terrain, cities, a rail budget, track you draw at cost, switches you throw by hand. Someone else, mostly joking, asked for Bluesky login bolted onto it so a build could be saved and shared. The joke worked. Iron Ledger is the game that came out of that thread.

What actually works

  • A procedurally generated map of terrain and cities, different every game.
  • Laying track by tapping — priced by the ground it crosses and how far it runs, against a starting budget.
  • Trains that start running the moment two cities connect, and pay you back over time.
  • Junctions with real switches: a train on the wrong pair of rails waits until you throw the switch.
  • Tearing up a bad piece of track for a full refund.
  • A "copy share link" button that packs your whole map and build into the URL — no account needed to show someone what you built.
  • Autosave to this browser, so closing the tab doesn't lose your progress.

What's still missing

Real "Sign in with Bluesky." It was asked for directly, and it isn't there yet — not skipped, blocked. The site this game lives on only allows network calls to a short allowlist of hosts, and the shared login service isn't on it yet. Rather than ship a login button that always fails, the game got a handle-lookup instead: type your Bluesky handle to tag a build with your name and avatar for sharing — real identity, no password, no login. Saving to your actual Bluesky account is next, once the allowlist is widened by someone with access to that file.

Why no login yet, in full

Two separate blockers, either one alone would have sunk it. First, the shared login code lives outside this game's own folder, and the site serves each game's assets from inside that folder only — so the login library 404s if you just try to import it. Second, even past that, the page that serves this game sends a security policy that only allows network calls to the public Bluesky read API and a couple of identity lookups — not the login service itself. A "Sign in" button would compile, render, and then have the browser itself refuse every request it made. Writing that up plainly felt more honest than shipping a button that lies.

Share links carry the whole build

Rather than wait on login to make sharing possible, a build's terrain seed, every piece of track, every switch setting and the revenue earned so far gets packed into the URL itself. Opening that link regenerates the exact same map and the exact same build, client-side, no server and no account. That turns out to be the better mechanism for "show off a build" even after real login exists — it still works for a build nobody ever chose to save to their account.

Money

Revenue a train earns funds more track, not just a one-time budget — a fixed budget with no income read like a puzzle with a ceiling, not a train game where a busy line pays for its own expansion.

Removing track

Pulling up a segment refunds its cost automatically and immediately — the amount you've spent is calculated live from what's currently on the map, so tearing up a piece of track is the refund, nothing extra to keep in sync. Any train that needed that piece either finds another way through or stops if that was its only route.

What a future pass would tackle first

  • Widen the site-wide allowlist so real Bluesky login can reach the login service, then wire an actual save-to-account.
  • A gallery of builds once accounts can save more than one.
  • A real balance pass on prices and payouts — the numbers in there now are first guesses, never tuned against a person actually playing.
  • Someone else in the thread asked whether cities could be named after your actual friends on Bluesky — a real feature, just not this one, and not something anyone but the person who asked for the game can request.
