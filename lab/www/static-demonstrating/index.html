Hats on a Book

a proper-coloring hat-guessing game on the book graph, played live

Two spine vertices, mutually adjacent to each other and to every one of n independent page vertices. An adversary properly colors the whole graph — adjacent vertices get different colors — from a palette of q colors. Every vertex sees only its neighbors' colors, then all five guess their own color at once. The team wins if at least one guess is correct.

This page demonstrates arXiv:2607.25274, "Proper Hat-Guessing on Two-Spine Book Graphs" (Yulin Zhai, 2026). Its Theorem 5.4 gives an explicit strategy that wins with q = 7 colors on 3 pages — the demo below runs that exact strategy, not a simplified stand-in.

Play it — B2,3 at q = 7

Colors are the seven elements of 𝔽₇ = {0,…,6}. Pages guess with the fixed affine formula gi(x,y) = x + λi(y−x) mod 7, for λ = (2, 3, 6) — one formula per page, no lookup table. Spines guess by solving, live, which of their possible tuples the pages left uncovered (the paper's "coverability" decomposition, computed here with a real bipartite-matching algorithm, not the paper's precomputed orbit table).

Why not 8 colors?

A short union-bound argument in the paper (Lemma 2.3) already kills q = 8 for n = 3 pages: on a restricted family of colorings, 5³ = 125 exceeds 2·7² = 98, more colorings than the two spines could possibly cover between them. That is why the demo above is capped at seven colors — eight is provably too many for three pages, matching HGP(B2,3) = 7 exactly.

Why 11 is the hard ceiling, for any number of pages

However many pages you add, the two spines can never be forced to cover more than a bounded amount of adversarial structure — that bound is C₂ = 11, proved by reducing the whole game to a bipartite-graph question: a set of "surviving" spine-color pairs is coverable by the two spines exactly when it is a pseudoforest (every connected component has at most one cycle). The smallest graph that *isn't* one is K2,3 — two rows, three columns, all six edges present, needing e + s = 6 + 5 = 11.

Try it yourself: assign each of the six edges below to Spine 1 (which may take at most one edge per column) or Spine 2 (at most one edge per row). You cannot assign all six without a conflict — that impossibility, at exactly this size, is the reason the paper's bound sits at 11 and not lower.

This page implements the paper's exact page-guess formulas and a general bipartite-matching decomposition for the spines — it does not reproduce the paper's asymptotic-probability argument (Theorem 4.5) or its full extremal classification (Section 7); those are stated here in prose only.
