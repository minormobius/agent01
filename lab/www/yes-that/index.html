Stallpoint

A low-temperature-differential Stirling engine can run on a warm hand and a glass of ice water — until it can't. The moment it stops isn't a fuzzy threshold, it's a bifurcation of the engine's own equations of motion. This page computes that bifurcation live, from the parameters you set, and simulates the flywheel obeying it in real time.

—
the model

Flywheel angle θ, driven by thermal torque, resisted by friction and by the displacer's own geometry fighting the crank once per turn:

I·θ̈ + b·θ̇ + κ·sin(θ) = α·ΔT

Nondimensionalized (b̂ = b/√(Iκ), γ = αΔT/κ) this is the driven-damped-pendulum equation, the same one that governs synchronous-motor pull-out and Josephson junctions. For small damping the boundary between "settles at a fixed angle" (stalled) and "keeps turning forever" (running) is a homoclinic bifurcation — the driving torque exactly matches the energy the friction bleeds off over one full swing. Melnikov's method gives the small-b̂ asymptotic for that boundary:

γ_c ≈ (4/π)·b̂   →   ΔT_c = (4b)/(πα) · √(κ/I)

That's a genuine approximation, accurate for small friction — not exact for every parameter combination, and it can drift from the true numerical boundary as b̂ grows past roughly 1. α (thermal-torque gain from working-gas properties) is held fixed this turn; see BRIEF.md for what that leaves out.
