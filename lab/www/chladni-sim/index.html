Chladni Plate

Ernst Chladni's 1787 trick made sound visible: bow or drive a flat plate at one of its resonant frequencies, scatter fine sand on top, and the sand bounces off the parts that are moving hard and settles along the parts that barely move at all. What's left is a map of the plate's own vibration.

A flat plate can vibrate in many distinct shapes, called modes, each with its own resonant frequency. Every mode has a fixed pattern of nodes — lines that stay still — and antinodes — regions that swing furthest up and down. Sand cannot sit still on an antinode; it gets thrown off with every cycle. On a node there's nothing throwing it, so it stays, and after enough shaking every grain has either walked to a node or bounced there by chance and stuck. The pattern you get is the geometry of the standing wave itself.

Reading the mode number

Each preset is labelled (n, m) — the two integers that define the standing-wave shape used here, cos(nπx)cos(mπy) − cos(mπx)cos(nπy), a classic idealised approximation for a square plate. Bigger n and m mean a shorter wavelength packed into the same plate, which means more nodal lines, a finer pattern, and — on a real plate — a higher frequency. The "relative frequency" number above is illustrative only: it scales roughly with n²+m², the way a simply-supported plate's frequencies do, but a real plate's actual resonances depend on its material, thickness, and how its edges are clamped. This page isn't calibrated to any physical plate or to Hz.
