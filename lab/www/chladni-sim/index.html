Chladni Plate

Ernst Chladni's 1787 trick made sound visible: bow or drive a flat plate at one of its resonant frequencies, scatter fine sand on top, and the sand bounces off the parts that are moving hard and settles along the parts that barely move at all. What's left is a map of the plate's own vibration — and every one of those resonances shows up as a peak if you sweep the driving frequency and watch how hard the plate answers back.

A flat plate can vibrate in many distinct shapes, called modes, each with its own resonant frequency. Every mode has a fixed pattern of nodes — lines that stay still — and antinodes — regions that swing furthest up and down. Sand cannot sit still on an antinode; it gets thrown off with every cycle. Drive the plate between resonances and nothing organises — the sand just sits wherever it lands, because no single mode is strong enough to push it around. Drive it exactly at a resonance and one mode dominates so hard the sand walks straight to its nodal lines.

Reading the plot

Frequency in Hz comes from the standard thin-plate formula, f = (π²/2π)⋅√(D/ρh)⋅((n/a)²+(m/a)²), where D = Eh³/12(1−ν²) is the plate's flexural rigidity, a is the span, h the thickness, and ρ, E, ν come from the material — that's why a thicker plate rings higher and a wider one rings lower. That formula is derived for a simply-supported edge; the mode shapes drawn here use the free-edge cosine approximation real Chladni plates need, because that is what actually produces the classic figures. Mixing the two is a simplification, disclosed rather than hidden: the numbers are physically scaled, not a lab measurement of this exact plate. The response curve sums all ten modes as damped oscillators driven from one fixed point — a textbook modal superposition, with the phase relationship between modes simplified for clarity. "Damping" is an illustrative Q knob, not a modelled material property.
