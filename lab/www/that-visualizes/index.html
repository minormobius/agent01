Why the formula looks like that

Turn this on and the plot above grows a dashed crosshair at (x̄, ȳ) — the mean point — plus one rectangle per dot. Each rectangle's width is that point's x−x̄, its height is y−ȳ, so its area is exactly the product term (x−x̄)(y−ȳ) the numerator sums. Orange means the point is above the mean in both x and y, or below in both — the deviations share a sign, so the point agrees with an upward trend. Blue means they disagree — the point sits on the "wrong" side for that trend. A point far from the mean in both directions casts a much bigger rectangle than one that's merely far in one, which is why a single outlier, moved far enough, can outweigh eleven ordinary points.

numerator  Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)0.00

The sum of every rectangle's signed area above — orange rectangles add to it, blue rectangles subtract. This one number is "do x and y move together", and its sign is the sign r ends up with.

x-spread  Σ(x−x̄)²0.00

How spread out the x-values are. Squaring means a point far left counts the same as one far right — nothing about spread cancels out.

y-spread  Σ(y−ȳ)²0.00

Same idea for y. Multiply the two spreads and take the square root, and you get the biggest the numerator could possibly be for these exact points — that's the denominator.

What r is telling you

r ranges from −1 to +1. Near +1, the points sit close to a line that rises left to right — as one variable goes up, so does the other. Near −1, the line falls — one goes up as the other goes down. Near 0, there's no consistent straight-line trend, in either direction. The faint line drawn through the points above is the best-fit line r is actually measuring the tightness of.

Where it lies to you

Hit Curved (fools r). The points trace a clean, obvious parabola — a textbook relationship, easy to predict — and r reads close to 0. That's not a bug: r only measures linear agreement. A perfect curve has no consistent straight-line slope, so the coefficient that only checks for straight lines reports nothing there. Then hit One outlier: eleven points with no relationship to each other at all, plus a single point dragged out to an extreme, and r swings hard toward 1. One point, moved far enough, can dominate a statistic computed from twelve. Neither of these is r malfunctioning — it's r doing exactly what it's defined to do, which is narrower than "are these related."

And whatever r says: a correlation, however strong, is not by itself evidence that one variable causes the other.
