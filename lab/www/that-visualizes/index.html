Twelve points on a plane. Drag any of them and the correlation coefficient below — r, the standard measure of how well two variables move together in a straight line — recalculates on every move. Try to drag the points into a tight line and watch r climb toward 1 or −1. Try to make r read exactly 0.

What r is telling you

r ranges from −1 to +1. Near +1, the points sit close to a line that rises left to right — as one variable goes up, so does the other. Near −1, the line falls — one goes up as the other goes down. Near 0, there's no consistent straight-line trend, in either direction. The faint line drawn through the points above is the best-fit line r is actually measuring the tightness of.

Where it lies to you

Hit Curved (fools r). The points trace a clean, obvious parabola — a textbook relationship, easy to predict — and r reads close to 0. That's not a bug: r only measures linear agreement. A perfect curve has no consistent straight-line slope, so the coefficient that only checks for straight lines reports nothing there. Then hit One outlier: eleven points with no relationship to each other at all, plus a single point dragged out to an extreme, and r swings hard toward 1. One point, moved far enough, can dominate a statistic computed from twelve. Neither of these is r malfunctioning — it's r doing exactly what it's defined to do, which is narrower than "are these related."

And whatever r says: a correlation, however strong, is not by itself evidence that one variable causes the other.
