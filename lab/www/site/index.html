A standoff zone the crowd can't enter. The barrier itself takes the load instead of the real wall behind it — but the room gets smaller, so whoever is left gets tighter.

How strongly people cling to their own small group instead of spreading out evenly. Cohesive clumps redistribute the crush — they don't remove it.

At 0% everyone is a fixed circle that only ever pushes straight back — turn one's Social Force Model. Turn this up and a compressed body reorients: it turns side-on to whatever's squeezing it (drawn as an ellipse), yielding along that axis in exchange for needing more room on the other. Nobody is pinned to a spot — what's elastic is a person's facing and shape, at the position they already occupy, easing back to round over roughly a third of a second once the load clears.

Reading it

Both numbers are model units, not newtons or pascals — comparable to each other and across settings on this page, not to a real incident. Push the buffer out: the back wall's pressure drops toward zero because the barrier is absorbing it, while the worst individual load usually climbs, because the same number of people now share less floor. Pull cohesion up: groups clump, so the room's density gets lumpy — pressure moves around rather than vanishing. There is no pair of settings here that drives both numbers down at once; that absence is the point.

Elastic coupling changes how a body resists, not where it stands. Watch the ellipses: each one's long axis is current facing, and it swings to line up with whatever direction is squeezing that person, while the body thins along that axis and thickens across it — like turning shoulder-first to get through a gap. Nothing here moves a person to a different point in the room; the shape and facing change at the position they're already at, and relax back toward a circle once the load lifts. Expect the reorientation readout near 0% at low density or with coupling off, climbing toward 30–50% once a packed room meets full coupling — it's capped well short of 100%, since the long-to-short axis ratio this model allows tops out around 1.25:1 by construction. That cap, and the ~⅓ second it takes to reorient, are tuned for a stable, legible toy, not fitted against the paper's own elastic constants — read them as "this much give," not as a measurement.
