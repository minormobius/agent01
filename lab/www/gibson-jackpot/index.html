The mathematical model

A toy model, not a forecast and not medical or financial advice — this tab exists so the model can be checked rather than taken on faith. Everything below is the literal arithmetic the calculator runs; nothing here is simplified for the reader.

Baseline aging hazard

Standard Gompertz–Makeham, the shape actuaries use for adult mortality: h(age) = A + B·eC·age, with A = 0.0005, B = 0.00003, C = 0.093. This is the hazard at socioeconomic percentile 100, resilience 1, minimum country risk — i.e. before any multiplier is applied.

Proportional-hazards multipliers

Country risk, state resilience and standing each contribute a relative-risk factor — the same log-linear structure a real Cox proportional-hazards model uses:
countryFactor = 0.6 + 0.8 · risk
resilienceFactor = 1.3 − 0.6 · resilience
sesFactor = 1.4 − 0.8 · (SES percentile ⁄ 100)
Country risk and resilience scores (0–1, per country) are illustrative estimates written by hand for this build, not sourced statistics — they set the ranking roughly right, not the absolute numbers. Standing runs 0–100 as a within-country percentile: 0 is the bottom of that country's distribution, 100 the top, independent of the country's own risk level.

These three factors combine into two separate multipliers, not one: mbase on ordinary aging, qJ on the jackpot excess hazard (see below) —
mbase = qJ = countryFactor · resilienceFactor · sesFactor
h(age) = mbase · (A + B·eC·age) + qJ · jackpotRate(t)
Today mbase and qJ are computed from the same formula and so carry the same number — the fix this turn was that qJ existed at all: earlier builds added jackpotRate(t) straight into h(age) with no multiplier, so a visitor's country, resilience and standing changed their ordinary-aging risk but not their exposure to the jackpot itself. Kept as two named terms, not one, so a future turn can give qJ its own sensitivity — say, personal wealth buying less protection against a systemic collapse than it buys against ordinary illness — without restructuring the hazard integral again.

Income → percentile

When you give an income instead of a percentile, it is converted against your selected country's own median, not a single global figure: percentile = 50·(1 + erf(z ⁄ √2)), z = (ln(income) − ln(countryMedianIncome)) ⁄ 1.1. The erf term uses the standard Abramowitz–Stegun rational approximation, not a lookup table. The per-country median incomes are illustrative order-of-magnitude estimates written by hand for this build, not sourced statistics — same caveat as the risk/resilience scores above — and the spread parameter (σ = 1.1) is still shared across every country, since there's no data here to estimate a per-country dispersion from. Switching country now visibly moves the same income to a different percentile, which is the point: $20,000/yr sits near the median in the US and far above it in Ethiopia.

Survival curve

Ages are integrated year by year to 100 (the numeric cutoff — true remaining-life integrals run to infinity, but this does not materially move the result for realistic ages) using the trapezoidal rule on cumulative hazard: H(a) = Σ (h(ai) + h(ai+1)) ⁄ 2, S(a) = e−H(a). Expected years remaining is the discrete integral of S(a) over that same grid.

The jackpot: population decline

A single global scenario, the same for every visitor, given as a normalized Weibull cumulative distribution: with x = clip(t − 2039, 0, 40),
D(t) = 0.8 · (1 − e−(x⁄24.3)2.2) ⁄ (1 − e−(40⁄24.3)2.2), and P(t) = P₀ · (1 − D(t)), with P₀ fixed at 2026's population. It sits flat until 2039, ramps through the 2040s–70s, and lands at 20% of 2026's population by around 2079, holding there through 2099 — a smooth stand-in for "not a war, not a single event, a slow-motion accounting," not a prediction of how it would actually happen. Because x is clipped rather than smoothly saturating, the decline rate below has a real kink at t = 2079: it tapers toward zero approaching from below, then is forced to exactly zero for t ≥ 2079. That is an accurate reading of the formula as given, not a smoothing bug.

How the jackpot enters your own curve

The world-level excess-mortality rate is the instantaneous rate implied by D(t), taken as a central difference rather than an analytic derivative (the clip makes the true derivative piecewise, easy to get the sign or scale wrong on by hand):
jackpotRate(t) = ( ln[popFraction(t−ε)] − ln[popFraction(t+ε)] ) ⁄ (2ε), ε = 0.02 years, popFraction(t) = 1 − D(t).
That rate itself is one global scenario, the same for every visitor — the population chart above reads it unscaled. Your personal exposure to it is qJ · jackpotRate(t), added into your hazard alongside mbase · h0(age): h(age) = mbase · h0(age) + qJ · jackpotRate(t). Country risk, state resilience and standing move both terms now, not just the ordinary-aging one — "world population" and "your survival curve" still read off the same underlying jackpotRate(t), just scaled differently per visitor before it enters the integral.

Why standing still won't move the number as much as the sesFactor range implies

Even with qJ applied, sesFactor's own range (1.4 down to 0.6 — a 2.3× spread end to end) never becomes a 2.3× spread in expected age at death, for two structural reasons that have nothing to do with the jackpot fix. First, sesFactor multiplies a hazard, and expected lifespan is a nonlinear function of cumulative hazard (via e−H) — doubling a hazard that was already small most of your life does not double the years lost. Second, for a low-risk, high-resilience country the countryFactor·resilienceFactor product is already well below 1 before SES is applied at all — for the United States (risk .30, res .70) it's ≈0.84 × 0.88 ≈ 0.74 — so both mbase and qJ are scaling hazards that were already discounted, and sesFactor's swing rides on top of that, not on top of 1.0. Moving standing end to end now visibly moves expected age at death by more than it used to (qJ used to be silently fixed at 1), but it still won't hit the raw multiplier ratio — that was never going to happen for a quantity built from an integral over a lifetime, not a single instant.
