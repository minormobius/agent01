Porefront

A square lattice of pores linked by throats, each with its own random resistance. Raise the pressure and the invading fluid claims every throat it can reach at that pressure — but some pockets of the fluid it's displacing get walled in as the front passes, and no amount of extra pressure ever gets them out.

What this actually shows

This models pressure-controlled drainage as bond percolation with trapping: throat resistances are drawn uniform at random, the invading fluid enters from the left and can only cross a throat once the pressure exceeds that throat's resistance and the throat is reachable from fluid already inside — and a throat is never crossed if doing so would seal off defending fluid with no path left to the right-hand drain. That sealed-off fluid is residual saturation: stuck at full drainage, not almost-invaded.

One grid here is one noisy sample. The paper this is drawn from finds that as the network grows, the trapped fraction settles onto a fixed limiting value — but slowly, with the gap shrinking as a power law (exponent ≈0.25 in 2D, ≈0.75 in 3D — small 3D samples land much closer to the true answer than small 2D ones do). A single run below can't show that curve; it can only give you one point on it. See the plan at the bottom for turning many visitors' runs into that scatter plot live.

how to read it

Blue is defending fluid that hasn't been touched. Orange is fluid that got invaded at or below the current pressure. Purple is fluid that's proven stuck — its whole remaining pocket lost every path to the drain, so it can never be pushed out no matter how far you raise the pressure. Drag the slider slowly near a purple patch forming and you can watch the exact throat that seals it.
