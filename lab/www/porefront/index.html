Porefront

A square lattice of pores between packed grains, linked by throats of random resistance. Water rises from the bottom under applied pressure, working against gravity, and claims every throat it can reach — but some pockets of the air it's displacing get walled in as the front passes upward, and no amount of extra pressure ever gets them out.

What this actually shows

This models pressure-driven imbibition into a granular pack as bond percolation with trapping, in a gravity field: throat resistances are drawn uniform at random, water enters from the bottom row and can only cross a throat once the applied pressure exceeds that throat's resistance plus the buoyancy cost of lifting water to that throat's height — and a throat is never crossed if doing so would seal off air with no path left up to the top row. That sealed-off air is residual saturation: stuck at full fill, not almost-displaced. The gravity slider sets how steep that height penalty is — at zero it's plain invasion percolation; turned up, the water front is forced flatter and more compact, the way a real water table rises through sand, because reaching high ahead of the bulk of the front costs more than filling in behind it first.

One grid here is one noisy sample. The paper this is drawn from finds that as the network grows, the trapped fraction settles onto a fixed limiting value — but slowly, with the gap shrinking as a power law (exponent ≈0.25 in 2D, ≈0.75 in 3D — small 3D samples land much closer to the true answer than small 2D ones do). A single run below can't show that curve; it can only give you one point on it. See the plan at the bottom for turning many visitors' runs into that scatter plot live.

how to read it

Blue is air that hasn't been touched. Orange is water that reached that pore at or below the current pressure and height. Purple is air that's proven stuck — its whole remaining pocket lost every path up to the top, so it can never be pushed out no matter how far you raise the pressure. Drag the pressure slider slowly near a purple patch forming and you can watch the exact throat that seals it. The gravity slider changes the run itself, not just the view — raise it and re-run to see the front flatten out as climbing costs more than filling sideways and below.

network is the node-and-edge graph above. field is the same run as a solid mosaic of tiles — easier to read as one continuous shape rather than individual pores. throats stops showing pores altogether and instead bins every throat by its own resistance, so you can see which resistances have been crossed, which are walled inside a trapped pocket, and which the front simply hasn't reached yet — all three colored the same way as the pores, and all three still move as you drag the slider.
