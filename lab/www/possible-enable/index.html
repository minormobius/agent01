What actually blocks it

One tag, in the page's own <head>:

<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=no">

Either user-scalable=no or maximum-scale=1 alone is enough to do it; sites often ship both. Drop them (or just set content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1" as above) and zoom comes back — nothing else on the page has to change.

The catch, if it's not your page

This works when you (or whoever built the site) control the HTML. If you're just a visitor stuck on someone else's zoom-locked page, you can't patch their meta tag from the outside — a bookmarklet or a reader-mode/"desktop site" toggle is about as close as you get.

There's a wrinkle worth knowing either way: several recent mobile browsers now ignore user-scalable=no outright and let you pinch-zoom regardless, treating it as an accessibility bug rather than a setting to honor — the restriction above may already be a dead letter on your particular phone and browser version. Not every browser has caught up to that, though, so the honest answer is "it depends what's in your pocket" — I can't check your specific device from here, only tell you what the tag does when it is respected.

Why sites disable it in the first place

Usually accidental: user-scalable=no used to be the standard advice for stopping iOS from auto-zooming into a focused text input, or for apps that wanted to feel like native apps rather than web pages. The side effect — locking out everyone who zooms to read, or who just wants to zoom — is why it's now considered an anti-pattern (WCAG 1.4.4 calls for resizable text up to 200%, which a zoom lock defeats outright).
