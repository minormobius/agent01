afterimage

A light moves around the grid, one square at a time. Hit match whenever the current square is the same one it was N steps back — you choose N. That's the whole game; it gets harder because remembering exactly how many steps back is the actual work.

This trains attention and short-term recall in the moment, not general intelligence. It's modelled on the n-back task used in memory research since the 1950s — whether practising it makes you sharper at anything else is genuinely contested among researchers, so treat this as a focus exercise, not a brain upgrade.

Compare with someone

Type a handle to see their best score at your current difficulty, if they've saved one here. There's no general leaderboard — only people you name.

Keep your scores

Your best on this device is already remembered. Sign in with Bluesky and finished runs can be saved to a record in your own repository instead — nowhere else, and nothing is sent unless you choose to save.

How it's scored

Every trial is one of four things: a real match you caught (a hit), a real match you missed, a false alarm (you hit match when it wasn't one), or a correct rejection (you correctly did nothing). Accuracy is hits plus correct rejections, divided by every trial in the run — a run of all correct rejections and no real matches still scores 100%, so it's a measure of judgement, not just reaction.

Nothing here is a diagnosis or a clinical test. It's a small, honest version of a real research task — nothing more is claimed for it.
