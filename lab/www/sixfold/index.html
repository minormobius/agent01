Sixfold

Build a triangulated sphere by hand, one unit triangle at a time — the editor refuses any attachment that would let a vertex touch more than six triangles. A mesh built entirely under that rule is called a 6-net. A theorem says every closed 6-net folds, uniquely, into one rigid shape made of flat unit triangles — no stretching, no gaps — which is why the paper behind this toy calls the result a neoplatonic solid: it earns the name without being one of the five ancient ones, since it can be as irregular as the mesh allows.

Six is the magic number because of a fixed budget: for any closed triangulated sphere, Σ (6 − degree(v)) over every vertex always equals exactly 12 — a fact of the sphere's topology (Euler's formula in disguise), not a choice. A vertex with degree under six is carrying some of that budget as curvature; a vertex at exactly six is locally flat and carries none. Close a net with the budget unspent and it cannot be a sphere at all — which is the real reason nothing here is allowed past degree six: overshoot even one vertex and the total can never balance back to 12.

A flat patch cut from this lattice always has a boundary — that number above can shrink but never reaches zero here, because closing a net into a sphere means taping non-adjacent boundary edges together, which this tap-to-attach editor doesn't do. 3 — solids, one of them actually solved

Five closed 6-nets whose unit-triangle shape comes from closed-form trig (a regular base plus an apex placed so every edge is exactly 1) — that's arithmetic, not the paper's realization method. The sixth is different: it's an irregular, non-vertex-transitive 6-net (a pentagonal bipyramid with one face split into three), handed to the page only as a face list — no coordinates supplied anywhere — and its shape below is what a real numerical solver found. Hit "Solve this net" above to add your own drawn mesh here too. Drag to look around; the solver's own numbers are printed under it.

How do you know a solved shape is right? Two numbers, both printed below, and neither is trusted alone. Edge error is how far every edge landed from length exactly 1 — near zero means the springs stopped pulling. That alone isn't enough: springs only push connected vertices apart from each other, so nothing stops two unconnected corners from drifting close together or the whole net folding back on itself — which is exactly what a "squished, overlapping" result looks like. So the solver also tracks the closest gap between any two non-adjacent vertices; a small number there is the warning sign, flagged in red below. This turn's fix adds a short-range repulsion between non-adjacent vertices to actively push the solver away from that failure mode, not just report it after the fact.

Turn three: the solver can now be pointed at your own net, and it now fights its own worst failure mode instead of just reporting it. Hit "Solve this net" under the editor and the same relaxation runs on whatever you've drawn — labelled clearly as a flexible embedding, not the rigid solid, because your net still has an open boundary (see the note there for why that's unavoidable with this editor). The relaxation itself changed too: edge-length error alone can't catch a shape that's folded back on itself — springs only pull connected vertices to length 1, nothing stops two unconnected ones from landing on top of each other — so non-adjacent vertices now repel each other at close range, and the closest surviving gap between any two of them is printed next to the edge error for every solved shape, flagged in red if it's tight. Five of the six precomputed solids are the classic convex deltahedra, built from closed-form trig, not solved. The sixth is genuinely solved: given only its face list, the relaxation starts every vertex at a random point in space, run three times from independent starts with the lowest-error run kept. That is not the paper's own method — the paper's is a numerical homotopy continuation from a hyperbolic starting point, which needs circle-packing machinery this build doesn't have — so convergence plus a clean non-adjacent gap is evidence the shape is right, not a proof the way the paper's algorithm would be. It has not been tried on a genuinely non-convex net, which is the paper's actual headline claim.
