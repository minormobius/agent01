Sixfold

Build a triangulated sphere by hand, one unit triangle at a time — the editor refuses any attachment that would let a vertex touch more than six triangles. A mesh built entirely under that rule is called a 6-net. A theorem says every closed 6-net folds, uniquely, into one rigid shape made of flat unit triangles — no stretching, no gaps — which is why the paper behind this toy calls the result a neoplatonic solid: it earns the name without being one of the five ancient ones, since it can be as irregular as the mesh allows.

Six is the magic number because of a fixed budget: for any closed triangulated sphere, Σ (6 − degree(v)) over every vertex always equals exactly 12 — a fact of the sphere's topology (Euler's formula in disguise), not a choice. A vertex with degree under six is carrying some of that budget as curvature; a vertex at exactly six is locally flat and carries none. Close a net with the budget unspent and it cannot be a sphere at all — which is the real reason nothing here is allowed past degree six: overshoot even one vertex and the total can never balance back to 12.

The editor and the gallery here work on the mesh's combinatorics — which triangles touch which, and the live degree cap — because that part is checkable with nothing but integer arithmetic. The five solids in the gallery above are the classic convex deltahedra: their coordinates come from closed-form formulas (a regular n-gon base plus an apex at the height that makes every edge exactly 1), not from solving anything. Solving an arbitrary net a visitor builds into its actual 3D shape is not done here — the paper's real method is a numerical homotopy continuation from a hyperbolic starting point, which is a genuine next-turn project, not something to improvise in one pass. So a net you build joins the gallery as the flat pattern you drew, not as a solved solid, and says so. Every edge on every rendered solid is measured after construction, not assumed — the number shown under each one is the true min/max edge length across all its triangles. Built with three.js, runs entirely in your browser; nothing here is sent anywhere. Honours "reduce motion" by turning off the idle auto-rotate on the solid viewer.
