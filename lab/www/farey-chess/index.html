Farey Chess

The board is the modular group's own tiling of the upper half-plane. Each side fields three pieces, and each piece is restricted to its own subset of the group's generators — a Flipper only knows S, a Slider only knows T/T⁻¹, a Rider knows all three. Tap one of your pieces to select it, then tap a dashed tile (or a move button) to move it. Land exactly on an enemy tile and you capture that piece; wipe out the other side's army to win.

what's actually going on

PSL(2,Z), the modular group, is generated by two Möbius transformations of the upper half-plane: S: z ↦ −1/z and T: z ↦ z+1. Its standard fundamental domain is the "keyhole" — |z| ≥ 1 and |Re(z)| ≤ ½ — and every copy of it in the picture above is M(domain) for some group element M, an integer matrix of determinant 1 built from words in S and T. A piece's tile is the matrix it stands on; a move right-multiplies that matrix by a generator, which is exactly how the Cayley graph of the group is built. Two different words can land on the same tile — the group has relations, most famously (ST)³ = 1 — and when that happens here it shows up honestly, as a loop back to a tile you've already visited.

The three piece types are literally three different generating sets of the same group. S has order 2 (S² = 1): a Flipper can only toggle to its one S-neighbour and back, so it barely moves but is nearly impossible to pin down for long. T has infinite order: a Slider walks one fixed strip of the tiling, forward or back, forever. A Rider carries the full group and can reach either kind of neighbour. None of this is a separate rulebook bolted onto the math — legalMoves() just restricts which generators a piece is allowed to right-multiply by.

One simplification, said outright rather than left implicit: this only draws the orientation-preserving half of the classic picture. The familiar two-colour "modular tessellation" you may have seen also includes mirror images, generated by an orientation-reversing map this board has no generator for. What's here is the honest PSL(2,Z) tiling on its own, which is why every tile is the same shape rather than alternating with its reflection.

Every tile here is one image of the fundamental domain under a matrix in PSL(2,Z), built live in your browser — nothing is precomputed or fetched.
