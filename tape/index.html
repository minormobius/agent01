A book you can put on the table.

A four-year-old picks a card off the shelf, puts it on the box, and hears their dad read Where the Wild Things Are. Takes it off, playback stops. Puts it back tomorrow, it carries on where it stopped. No screen, no account, no subscription, no internet.

Commercial versions of this exist and they are good. They are also priced like razor blades: the player is $80–$130 and every story after that is $10–$20, forever, and the catalogue is somebody else’s. The part that does the magic — the chip in the card — costs about twenty cents.

This surface is where that gets designed and built, in the open. Hardware, firmware, the phone-side recording pipeline, and the enclosure. Everything here is either a finished decision with the reasoning attached, or an open question marked as one.

The one fact that shapes everything. An NFC tag cannot hold the audio. The cheapest sticker (NTAG213) has 144 bytes of user memory; the biggest tag anyone sells cheaply (NTAG216) has 888. One minute of speech is about 180,000 bytes. So the card holds a 64-bit pointer and the audio lives on an SD card inside the box. Everything nice about this design falls out of that — including “many books on one card”, which stops being a feature and becomes the length of an array. The full argument.

The design is not finished

Three questions are open and are deliberately being left open until parts are chosen. They are written up properly in the design record:

  1. Bluetooth or wire? Bluetooth was the starting assumption. It is probably the wrong one for a four-year-old, for reasons that are specific rather than aesthetic.
  2. Which brain? An ESP32 boots in a second and costs $6 but fights itself over WiFi and Bluetooth; a Pi Zero 2 W does everything and takes 25 seconds to boot.
  3. Battery, or a cable? A cable is honest, cheap, and safe. A battery is what makes a child carry the thing to bed.
