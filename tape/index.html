A book you can put on the table.

A four-year-old picks a card off the shelf, puts it on the box, and hears their dad read Where the Wild Things Are. Takes it off, playback stops. Puts it back tomorrow, it carries on where it stopped. No screen, no account, no subscription, no internet.

Commercial versions of this exist and they are good. They are also priced like razor blades: the player is $80–$130 and every story after that is $10–$20, forever, and the catalogue is somebody else’s. The part that does the magic — the chip in the card — costs about twenty cents.

This surface is where that gets designed and built, in the open. Hardware, firmware, the phone-side recording pipeline, and the enclosure. Everything here is either a finished decision with the reasoning attached, or an open question marked as one.

The one fact that shapes everything. An NFC tag cannot hold the audio. The cheapest sticker (NTAG213) has 144 bytes of user memory; the biggest tag anyone sells cheaply (NTAG216) has 888. One minute of speech is about 180,000 bytes. So the card holds a 64-bit pointer and the audio lives on an SD card inside the box. Everything nice about this design falls out of that — including “many books on one card”, which stops being a feature and becomes the length of an array. The full argument.

Where things are

Design record this is the meat

The product architecture, the landscape survey, and every decision with its reason. Read this first — four of the obvious assumptions about this project turn out to be wrong, and it is cheaper to find out here than at the soldering iron.

Hardware chosen & linked

Every part with a checked link to buy it, what it beat and why, the schematic and the power chain, and a live power budget. The check is not “does it return 200” — it is whether the page still says the part’s name.

Record your first story start here

Fifteen minutes, most of it spent reading a book out loud. Written for a parent with a phone, not an engineer with a bench — including how to read aloud for a recording, which matters more than any of the electronics.

Assembly structure only

Eight stages, each ending at a test you can fail, ordered so that a fault is always in the one thing you just changed. The steps wait on a box actually existing.

Firmware specified

What runs on the box: the card-presence state machine, the manifest index, the ingest server, and the audio pipeline behind a swappable output stage.

Studio works today

Record a book in the browser. Runs here for trying out, and ships inside the box so that the recording and the upload share an origin — which they have to.

Cards works today

What is actually on a card, byte by byte, and a writer that puts it there over Web NFC. Every card’s URL lands here, so a card found under the sofa can say what it is.

Simulator works today

The whole box, in a tab. Tap a card, hear it play, press the buttons. The point is to get the interaction wrong here, where being wrong is free.

What is settled, and what is not

The three questions this page carried last round are answered, and the answers are in the decision log with their reasoning:

  • Bluetooth or wire? Wire. The wireless speaker turned out not to matter, and dropping it freed the brain choice entirely — there is no longer any reason to be stuck on 2016 silicon.
  • Which brain? ESP32-S3. It boots in about a second and decodes every audio format a phone can produce, which is what lets a parent just upload a voice memo.
  • Battery or cable? Battery. ~20 hours of stories, ~8 days between charges.

Still open: the enclosure — though it now has five requirements that arrived from other decisions, including a card nest shaped by the read range. And nothing has been built yet. Everything on this site works; none of it has been soldered.

The cards are a deck of playing cards. A poker deck, an NFC sticker on the back, the id written on in biro — and the card lives in the book it belongs to, which is a better filing system than anything a product designer would have reached. Cards are consumable at 14¢; the recordings are the asset, and they never move.
