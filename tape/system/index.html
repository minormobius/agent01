The system

The tooling is one part of it. This is the rest, written down for the version of you that comes back in two months having forgotten why any of it was decided — which is the only readership this page has.

This page is not about tape. It is the house standard for building things like tape, and it happens to live here because tape is the first one. When there is a second project, lift it out to a repo-level doc — scripts/rehome.mjs exists for exactly that move. Until then, keeping it next to a real build is what stops it becoming aspirational.

1 · The bench

Ordered by what is missing, not by what is impressive. The greyed rows are already on the bench.

The Fluke changes one recommendation. The case against buying a crimper was never the money — it was that a marginal crimp fails intermittently, months later, and you would blame the firmware. That argument assumes crimps cannot be checked. With a good meter each one can be: tug it, then beep it end to end, then beep it to its neighbours to catch a whisker. Verified individually, a crimp is no longer a thing you trust. So: buy the crimper if you intend to keep building. Buy the pre-crimped kit as well, so the first box is not waiting on a new skill.

2 · What a Perma-Proto is

A circuit board wired to look exactly like a solderless breadboard: 30 rows of double five-hole columns, four power rails down the sides, the same silkscreen numbering, 51×81 mm, about $4.50. Thru-plated and gold-finished, so it takes rework without lifting pads.

Why it matters more than it sounds: the usual route from breadboard to permanent is to look at a working breadboard and rebuild the circuit on plain perfboard, which has no layout at all. That rebuild is a transcription, and transcriptions are where errors come from — you are copying a working thing into a different topology by eye, at night. A Perma-Proto has the same topology, so the move is hole-for-hole. What worked keeps working.

3 · The interconnect standard

Three tiers. The rule is that you never think about this again — you look it up here and do what it says.

Tier 1 · board to module

0.1″ headers. No cable.

Female sockets soldered to the board; modules plug in. A dead module is a swap, not a desoldering job, and the reader can be lifted out to test read range through different lid thicknesses without unwiring anything.

Tier 2 · board to panel

JST-XH 2.54 mm.

Latching and polarised. Dupont is friction-fit only and explicitly not for anything that moves; this is a toy that gets shaken and carried by the wrong end. Polarised also means you cannot plug it in backwards at eleven at night, which you will otherwise eventually do.

Tier 3 · power

JST-PH 2.0 mm.

Not a preference — it is what the battery ecosystem already ships. Cells and chargers arrive with it fitted, so the highest-current connection in the box needs no work and no decision.

And on every cable that leaves the board: a zip tie through two holes, before the connector. A tug then pulls on the board and not on a solder joint. It is the step people skip, it costs nothing, and it is most of the difference between a box that survives a four-year-old and one that comes back in pieces.

4 · Tricks that replace tools

Written down because they are the kind of thing you work out once and then forget you knew.

Soldering a header in square, with no third hand

Plug the header’s pins into a solderless breadboard and set the board face-down on top. The breadboard holds everything square and level. Tack one corner pin, pick it up, check it is flush, reheat that single joint to correct it if not, then solder the rest. Correcting one joint is easy; correcting thirty-six is a new board.

Holding a wire while you tin it

A zip tie round the wire and a heavy mug on the tail. You have the zip ties.

Checking a crimp, now that there is a Fluke on the bench

Three checks, in order, every time: tug it firmly by the wire — a bad crimp comes off in your hand and is cheap to find now; beep it end to end; beep it to its neighbours to catch a whisker bridging two pins in the housing. Ten seconds, and it converts crimping from a thing you trust into a thing you know.

Before powering anything for the first time

Beep between the power rails with everything unplugged. A short found by a meter costs nothing; a short found by a battery costs a module and possibly the board.

5 · The build loop

Breadboard → transfer → box, with a gate between every stage.

  1. Breadboard one subsystem at a time and prove it against its gate before adding the next. The whole reason the assembly page is staged that way is so a fault is always inside the one thing you just changed.
  2. Never add two things between tests. The moment you do, debugging becomes a search instead of a look.
  3. Transfer to the Perma-Proto hole-for-hole once the whole thing works loose on the bench. Not before — soldering a design you have not seen run is how an evening becomes a weekend.
  4. Box it last, and only after the full first-story flow works with wires everywhere.

6 · How this gets written down

This is the actual system, and it is the part that makes a second project fast. Four habits, each of which has already paid for itself here:

HabitHereWhat it bought
Parts as data, not prose parts.json → rendered by two pages One place to edit. A link checker that can verify the whole BOM in one command — and which caught that a 200 response proves nothing, because vendors reuse product ids.
Pin map as code, diagram generated from it lib/pinmap.js → the schematic The drawing cannot lie about a pin number. It also holds the reserved list, so GPIO33–37 being PSRAM is caught by a test instead of by a multimeter at midnight.
Every hard-won constraint becomes a test a full card still fits NTAG213 · no origin can both record and upload · the multimeter stays high on the bench list A comment saying “don't do X” gets skimmed. A failing test gets read. Three times now a fact learned the hard way has been frozen this way, and one of them caught a real modelling error the same afternoon.
Decisions logged with their reasons the decision log — eighteen rows, each with a because In two months you remember what was decided and never why. Without the why you relitigate it, and usually arrive somewhere worse, because the reason was load-bearing and invisible.

To start project two

Copy the shape, not the content: a parts.json, a pinmap.js, a selftest that enforces the constraints you have already learned, and a design record whose decision log has a because column. Then the bench, the interconnect tiers and the tricks above are already answered and you start at the interesting part.
