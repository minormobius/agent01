A thousand of them

The BOM does collapse, roughly as expected — about $103 of hobby parts becomes about $30 of production parts. And then it stops being the interesting number, because four things that cost nothing at quantity one now cost more than all the silicon put together.

The short version. Landed cost lands near $40–$48 a unit for the US only, of which BOM is about two thirds and compliance most of the rest. The design needs almost no change — one early decision turns out to have been worth five figures. But there is a business problem underneath, and it is not a cost problem. It is at the bottom of this page.

2 · The surprise: the cards cost more than the microcontroller

Shipping a full deck means 52 tags per box, 52,000 tags across the run. Printed PVC NFC cards land somewhere around 10–15¢ each at that volume, so a deck is roughly $6 — twice the ESP32 and second only to the whole power subsystem.

This is the one place the design and the business disagree with each other. Three ways out, and they are genuinely different products:

  • Ship ten cards, sell decks. The obvious move, and it quietly turns tape into the thing it was built to not be — a razor with blades.
  • Ship stickers, not cards. 25 mm NTAG213 stickers are a third the price of printed cards and the buyer supplies a deck of playing cards from a supermarket. Cheaper, more charming, entirely in keeping with the project, and it puts an assembly step on the customer.
  • Ship the full deck and eat it. Honest, and it is $6.

Worth noticing that this decision was invisible at quantity one, where cards are 14¢ and nobody counts them.

A radio, a lithium cell, and a four-year-old. That is three regulated things at once, and it is where garage hardware usually stops.

The decision that already paid for itself. Keeping the ESP32-S3 as a pre-certified module rather than going chip-down means the module’s own FCC ID covers the WiFi radio. The finished product then needs only Part 15B unintentional-emissions testing on the self-declaration path — no FCC filing, no fee. Published estimates put the difference at $5,500–$14,500 and roughly half the timeline, and modules win on total cost below about 50,000 units. That choice was made back when the reason was “ESP-ADF decodes whatever the phone recorded”; it turns out to have been worth more than the entire BOM of the first hundred units.

The mirror image: going chip-down on the PN532 is the opposite trade. It saves perhaps $3 a unit and buys an intentional-radiator certification, an antenna you now have to tune, and a re-test every time the enclosure changes. At 1,000 units it is close to a wash and probably the wrong way round. At 10,000 it is obviously right.

4 · Tooling, and why you would not injection-mould at 1,000

A simple two-part moulded box is roughly $3,000–$15,000 of aluminium tooling. Across 1,000 units that is $3–15 each, on a part that costs $1.50 to mould — the tool costs more than the parts. Moulding starts paying back somewhere around 5,000–10,000 units.

So at a thousand you do what the one-off build does, industrialised: an off-the-shelf enclosure with a laser-cut front panel, or CNC-routed plywood. It costs more per unit and nothing up front, which is the right shape of risk for a first run. The enclosure routes.

5 · Things that do not exist at quantity one

  • A test jig. You cannot plug in a thousand boards by hand. A pogo-pin bed that powers the board, flashes it, exercises the reader with a known card, plays a tone into a microphone, and reads the battery divider — a day of work and it defines your yield.
  • A tracking label. Not optional: CPSIA requires permanent marking with manufacturer, location and batch. That is a design requirement, not paperwork — it needs a place on the moulding or the label artwork.
  • Serialisation. Each box wants an identity for support, and each SD card an image. Both are production-line steps.
  • Returns and support. At a 3% return rate that is thirty boxes coming back, and someone reads the emails.
  • Product liability insurance. A lithium cell in a toy for a four-year-old is the sharp end of the consumer-goods risk pool. Get a quote before committing; it can change the arithmetic on its own.

7 · The problem, and it is not cost

The thing that makes tape good is that a parent records the story themselves. That is the whole point, and it means there is nothing to sell afterwards.

A Toniebox is $129.99 and every figure after it is $19.99. Over two years a modest collection runs $610–$850, and almost all of that is content. The hardware is not the business — it is customer acquisition for the business, and the margin lives in a catalogue we deliberately do not have.

So a thousand tapes is a hardware company with one transaction per customer, competing against two companies for whom the hardware is a loss leader. That is a real and specific disadvantage, and it is worth being clear-eyed about before a tool is cut. It also has a shape of answer — sell the thing whose margin you are structurally good at, which here is cards and enclosures and possibly nothing else, or do not sell it at all and publish the design so that a thousand people build one.

Which is what the repository is already for.

8 · What to do differently now, at quantity one

Very little, which is the useful finding. Three things, all cheap today and expensive to retrofit:

  1. Keep the ESP32-S3 as a module, forever. §3. It is already the plan; the point is not to talk yourself out of it later for $2 of BOM.
  2. Put test points on everything — the rails, I²C, I²S, the battery divider. On protoboard they are a pad and a label. They are what a pogo-pin jig lands on, and adding them to a laid-out board later means a respin.
  3. Never let the audio format become the box’s problem. The manifest names the file and ESP-ADF plays what arrives. That is what keeps a firmware update from orphaning somebody’s recordings, and at a thousand units in the field that stops being a nicety.
