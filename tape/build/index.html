Build it

Eight stages, each ending at a test you can actually fail. The order exists so that when something does not work you know which one thing you just changed.

Status: structure written, steps not. Every stage below is real and in the right order, and the parts and pin assignments it refers to are chosen and linked. What is missing is what only building one produces: photographs, torque-and-fit details, and the list of things that went wrong the first time. No box has been built yet. Saying so is more useful than a confident guide to an assembly nobody has performed.

Before you order anything

  • Read the design record, or at least §3. It is short and it will stop you buying a 125 kHz reader or a foil-backed deck of cards.
  • Decide branded or unbranded on the bill of materials. If you buy one branded part, buy the PN532 — the antenna is the part that decides whether a four-year-old’s aim is good enough.
  • Buy one deck of cards and test a single sticker on it before you sticker fifty.

Starting from one soldering iron

The tool list matters more than the parts list if you do not already have a bench, and the order below is not the order you would guess. A crimper is ninth.

The first box costs more than the box. Roughly $100 of that is bench, not project — you buy it once and every build after this is just parts. Worth knowing before the total on the checkout page is a surprise.

How things connect

Most of the wiring is not wiring

Every module in the parts list — the ESP32 board, the PN532, the amplifier, the SD breakout — comes on 0.1″ pins. Solder female headers to the board and plug them in. The four fiddliest interconnects stop being cables at all, a dead module becomes a swap rather than a desoldering job, and you can lift the PN532 out to test read range through different lid thicknesses without unwiring anything.

Six short runs. That is not a harness, it is an afternoon — and the highest-current connection in the box, the battery, arrives already connectorised.

JST-XH, and no crimper

Two decisions, and the second one is the useful one.

JST-XH rather than Dupont, because Dupont is friction-fit only and is explicitly not for anything that moves. This is a toy a four-year-old will shake, drop and carry by the wrong end. JST-XH latches, and it is polarised — you cannot plug it in backwards at eleven at night, which you will otherwise eventually do.

Buy the kit with the pins already crimped on. You push the pre-crimped pin into the housing by hand and solder the bare end to the board. You get the latch and the polarisation and you need no crimping tool at all. Do this for build one; find out whether you actually resent the fixed lengths before spending on a tool to fix a problem you may not have.

When you do buy a crimper — not now — do not buy the SN-28B. It is the one every guide recommends and its anvil is too wide: it bites into the insulation rather than wrapping it. IWISS IWS-2820M or SN-025 instead, about $35. This matters more here than in most projects, because a marginal crimp fails intermittently, months later. It will pass your tug test, work all through the bench build, and then start dropping out in a bedroom — and you will spend a weekend suspecting the firmware.

Strain relief is the part people skip

Every cable that leaves the board should be anchored to something structural before it reaches its connector, so that a tug pulls on the anchor and not on a solder joint. A zip tie through two holes in the perfboard costs nothing and is the difference between a box that survives a four-year-old and one that does not.

The stages

1 · Flash the board on its own

~15 min · nothing but the dev board and a USB cable

Before anything is soldered. Confirm the board enumerates, takes firmware, and prints to the serial console.

Gate: the box says hello over serial. If this does not work, nothing after it will, and you will not be able to tell why.

2 · SD card

~20 min · + microSD breakout

Four wires and power. Format a card, put a file on it, read it back.

Gate: the firmware lists the card’s contents over serial.

3 · Sound

~20 min · + amplifier and speaker

Three I²S wires plus the mute line. Play a file off the card. Do this before the NFC reader, because audio working is what makes every later stage debuggable by ear.

Gate: a story plays, at a sensible volume, with no hiss when it stops. Hiss when idle means the amplifier’s mute line is not wired or not being used.

4 · The reader

~30 min · + PN532

Two I²C wires and the interrupt. Set the module’s DIP switches to I²C — this is the step everyone misses and it looks exactly like a dead board. Then read a card, then write one.

Gate: reads a card at the range you actually need, through whatever thickness your enclosure lid will be. Measure it now, with a ruler. This number decides the enclosure.

5 · Controls

~20 min · + two buttons, pot, LED

Buttons to ground with internal pull-ups. The pot across 3V3 and GND, wiper to the ADC. Set the volume ceiling here, with the pot at maximum and a child in the room, and then leave it.

Gate: the pot at its end stop is loud enough to hear across a bedroom and not loud enough to be unpleasant close up.

6 · Power

~40 min · + charger, cell, buck-boost

Last, and on purpose: everything before this ran off USB, so a fault here cannot be confused with a fault anywhere else. Follow the power chain exactly.

Gate: it plays on the cell, it plays while charging, and the measured current while playing is somewhere near the budget’s figure. If it is double, something is not sleeping — most likely the NFC field is on continuously.

7 · Bench test the whole thing, before it goes in a box

~30 min · all of it, still loose on the bench

Record a real story, upload it, write a card, play it, take the card off mid-sentence, put it back. Leave it idle for an hour and confirm it went to sleep and wakes on a button.

Gate: everything on the first-story page works, start to finish, with wires everywhere. Do not close a box around a system you have not seen work.

8 · The enclosure

unknown · not yet designed

Requirements have accumulated from the electronics and are listed: the card nest sized by the range you measured at stage 4, a screwed battery compartment, the speaker firing up or forward, and small enough that a four-year-old can carry it with two hands.

Gate: a four-year-old picks it up and puts a card on it without being shown how.
