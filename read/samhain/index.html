A deep-read hub · the autumn tales of the north

Samhainar it aursluicthi síde hÉrenn dogrés im samain

“For the fairy-mounds of Ireland are always open at Samhain.” That line is in an Old Irish tale more than a thousand years old, and it is the plainest medieval statement of the belief behind modern Halloween. This hub collects the old spooky stories of the northern autumn: the Irish Samhain tales, the Norse Winter Nights, the Scottish Hallowe'en ballads and the dead who come home at Martinmas. They are read the way Pendragon reads the Arthurian tales: the original text with a facing translation, then the cast, the story graph, the motifs and a storybook. Each tale is graded by how well attested it is, so you always know whether you are reading a medieval manuscript or a Victorian's reconstruction.

01 The tales

Five tales are live: four Irish and one Norse, from different centuries. One of them has no festival in it at all, which turned out to be useful, and one has two. The roster below says what comes next.
I · Old Irish · Connacht · Samhain  A

Echtra Nerai

The Adventure of Nera

On Samhain night at Crúachan a dare sends Nera out to tie a withy on a hanged man's foot, and the hanged man asks for a drink. Then come three houses, a fort in flames, a road into the hill, a fairy wife, a crown kept in a well, the Morrígan's stolen cow, and a hero who has not come out until now, nor will he come till Doom.

II · Old Norse · East Iceland · the Winter Nights  B

Þiðranda þáttr

The Tale of Þiðrandi

At the Winter Nights feast a seer warns that no one must go out, whatever knocks. Something knocks three times. The chieftain's son goes out. Nine women in black ride from the north and nine in white from the south, and the black ones get there first. Then many a hill is opening, and the old powers pack their bags.

Aided Muirchertaig meic Erca

The Death of Muirchertach mac Erca

A king takes home a woman from a grave-mound whose names are Sigh, Storm, Winter-Night. She makes armies out of stones. The Cross shows him the truth, and he goes back to her anyway. On the Tuesday after Samhain she turns the spears inward and burns the house, and he drowns in a vat of wine.

Táin Bó Regamna

The Cattle-Drive of Regamain

Nera's sister tale, from the other side. A cry out of the north throws Cú Chulainn out of bed. At the Ford of the Two Graves he meets a red woman with a one-legged horse, whose names are mockery and cold wind. When he grabs at her she becomes a black bird and tells him how long he has left: I am guarding your death.

V · Old & Middle Irish · Ulster · Samhain, twice  A

Serglige Con Culainn

The Wasting Sickness of Cú Chulainn

At the Samhain assembly two birds on a chain of red gold sing the host to sleep, and Cú Chulainn misses them twice. Against a pillar-stone two smiling women horsewhip him in a dream, and he lies a year without a word. At the next Samhain a stranger sits on his bed. Then come Mag Mell across the water, Fand, a wife with sharpened knives, a cloak shaken between two lovers, and a drink of forgetting.

What the third tale did

After two tales the hypothesis read: the departure is the doorstep, and the ghost story is the wonder-tale that cannot get past it. Aided Muirchertaig was chosen to break it, and it did. Muirchertach gets further along Propp's spine than either hero before him. A saint acts as donor, the Cross over his eyes is the magical agent, and the agent works: the armies turn back into stones and he sees the truth. Then he gives it back. He walks home across the dark field to the woman in his bed, and the return is what kills him.

So direction does not matter. Nera survives by going out and stays out. Þiðrandi dies by going out. Muirchertach dies by coming back in. What all three share is narrower: the danger is at the door of the house on the festival night, in either direction, and the house is never the safe place. Hearth, door, field, mound: every tale is a set of crossings between those four places, and the analysis below maps them.

What the fourth tale did

Táin Bó Regamna was chosen because it is nearly all road, and it broke one word of the hypothesis: there is no festival in it. No Samhain and no Winter Nights, only a night, a sleeping house and a cry out of the north. Everything else held. The summons comes to the bed, the hero is thrown across his own threshold unarmed, and the encounter happens at a ford named for two graves, the threshold on the road. So the hypothesis loses a word. It now reads: the danger is at the threshold, at night, in either direction, and the house is never the safe place. The festival is how three of the four tales date their night. It is not what makes the night dangerous.

It also showed two things the instruments could not say. A crossing can be safe tonight and fatal later: Cú Chulainn comes home unharmed with his death in the Morrígan's keeping. The map now marks those with ⌛, and recoding the earlier tales by the same rule found two in Muirchertach. And the otherworld's names needed their own instrument, because twice now its figures have been named as the wind.

What the fifth tale did

Serglige Con Culainn brought the festival back, twice over. The hero is struck at a pillar-stone during the Samhain assembly, and summoned from his bed on the eve of the next one. The hypothesis held: both injuries come at a threshold, and the second comes to the bed past four guards. It also added a rule. This is the first hero to go into the otherworld, win there, love there and come home alive and whole, and the price is his memory. Manannán shakes his cloak between the lovers, and the druids give Cú Chulainn a drink of forgetting. Across five tales, nobody comes back from the otherworld unmarked. Nera stays in the hill. Muirchertach comes back, remembers, and dies. Cú Chulainn comes back twice: from the ford with a deadline, and from Mag Mell with an empty cup.

It also broke a finding. After four tales, no figure of the otherworld had given a true name. Here they give their names at once, with their fathers' names, and the Names instrument needed a new row for it. See below for what that turned out to mean.

The instruments — five held, and a sixth was added

The five candidate analyses proposed after two tales were tested against the third. All five held, and the third tale sharpened each one, so they are now built. Each tale carries a night.js layer with the same shape, and the section below draws them side by side.

03 The analysis — six instruments, five tales

Drawn live from each tale's night.js. Hover any row for its source movement.

1 · The threshold

Each row is one crossing, drawn on a five-step ladder from the hearth to the far place (the mound, the hill, the cave or the church). The colour, glyph and word all say what the crossing led to.

Read it this way: every tale crosses its door again and again: 4 to 11 crossings each pass through it, and Serglige, with two Samhains, has the most. Nera's one fatal crossing is the dead man carried into somebody else's house. Þiðrandi's two fatal crossings both go out, and Muirchertach's three go back in. Regamna has no fatal crossing, only two marked ⌛. Muirchertach has two ⌛ as well. Serglige is the first tale in which no crossing leads to a death, tonight or later: no fatal crossing and no ⌛. People die in its battle, but nobody dies crossing. What it has are three crossings stopped (a whipping in a vision, undone a year later; Emer's knives; the madness, ended by a drink) and one lost. The one lost is the otherworld woman, going home. The ⌛ tales are both ones in which the otherworld comes as a woman who talks and keeps a death: Sín for seven nights, and the Morrígan until a calf is grown. Serglige shows that a talking woman is not enough. Fand and Lí Ban talk more than anyone, and keep nothing but a year. Direction is still not the variable. The threshold is the constant, and so is the night.

2 · The rules

Read it this way: for four tales the main rule-giver was different every time. Nera's rules come from the dead and from custom: household chores, taught by what happened to the house that skipped them. Þiðrandi's come from a seer, and only virtue breaks them. Muirchertach's all come from the villain, and are designed to be broken. Regamna's come from the hero himself. Serglige is the first to repeat: custom (the sword that turns on a liar), a helper (leave the birds alone) and, like Regamna, the hero, with not on a woman's invitation. The difference is who breaks them. In Regamna the otherworld breaks the hero's rules. In Serglige every rule that breaks is broken by the hero himself, out of pride and then out of love. A rule in these tales always has an author, and the tale is partly about who that author is, and partly about who breaks it.

3 · Dread

Read it this way: two devices are still in every tale: counting and withholding. Every tale counts up to its moment and hides something. Silence, inversion and vanishing are each in four of the five. After three tales, foreknowledge and surprise seemed to split along the truth claim: the legends announced their endings and the tale ambushed you. Regamna spoiled that, because it does both. Serglige brings back a weaker version of the split, and it now holds for all five tales. Every tale ambushes (Nera, Regamna and Serglige all use surprise), every legend foreknows (Þiðrandi and Muirchertach), and neither legend uses surprise. A legend says this happened, and they were warned. A tale says look out.

5 · Names

New with the fourth tale, and given a new row by the fifth. How each tale names its otherworld figures, and whether it names them at all.

Read it this way: in the first four tales no figure of the otherworld gives a true name. The silent ones have none: Nera's fairy wife and king, the hanged man, the nine-and-nine dísir. The ones that speak lie about their names, or name themselves as weather. Sín is Storm, Rough Wind, Winter-Night, and the man at the ford is Cold-Wind-and-Rushes. The one thing named Seer is an ox, and his name is the decoy. Serglige breaks this. Lí Ban, Fand and Óengus give their names freely, each with a father, Áed Abrát. Only the enemy is named in mockery, Senach the Spectral. Put that beside the threshold: it is also the only tale in which no crossing is fatal, tonight or later. An otherworld with genealogies can be visited and come back from. An otherworld that answers in weather kills. That is one tale against four, so treat it as a lead, not a law. The next two tales are Norse hauntings by the named dead, so they will test it.

6 · Truth claims

What the instruments say together

The Pendragon apparatus asks what happens: Propp's functions, Greimas's desire, Thompson's motifs. These six ask where it happens, under whose rule, how it frightens, which belief it carries, what the otherworld is called and whether it wants to be believed. Across five tales from two cultures and four centuries the answers line up. These are stories about a house at night and a rule set at its door. Someone crosses it, and nobody who crosses comes back unmarked: dead, kept, given a deadline, or made to forget. The festival dates the night in four of the five. In all five it is the night, not the feast, that does the work. And the otherworld answers in weather instead of a name, except once, in the one tale where everyone who crosses lives.

Next: the Fróðá wonders (Eyrbyggja saga 50–55). This is the first haunting by the named dead: the drowned come home dripping and sit by the fire under their own names. It tests the names lead directly. It is also a legend, so the dread split says it should foreknow and not ambush. And it is the first tale in which the otherworld moves into the house. The threshold map will have to show the dead coming in and the living driven out.

04 What the night is, in the oldest texts

Before the evidence, here is the night as the tales themselves describe it.
Great was the darkness of that night, and its horror; and demons would always appear on that night.Echtra Nerai §2 — doaidbitis demnoi ind oidqi sin dogrés
This host cannot set out until the next Samhain … for the fairy-mounds of Ireland are always open at Samhain.Echtra Nerai §14

Two things stand out. The first is the tense. The verbs are habitual (dogrés, “always”), so the tale is not describing one night but saying what Samhain is every year. The second is what comes out of the dark. It is the áes síde, the people of the hollow hills, and one dead man. The tales do not describe a festival of ancestral spirits. That idea came later, by way of the Church's All Souls (2 November) and of 19th-century folklorists. Below, the evidence is laid out in order of strength, so the two can be told apart.

The honest version. We have no pagan Irish account of Samhain: every Irish text was written by Christians, centuries after conversion. What survives is (1) a Gaulish calendar month name, (2) medieval Irish tales that treat Samhain as an assembly, a quarter-day of the year and a night when the síde open, and (3) a great deal of modern folk practice recorded from the 18th century on. Ronald Hutton's The Stations of the Sun (1996) is the standard warning against joining these into a single ancient “Celtic festival of the dead”. The series keeps them apart and grades each tale.

05 The evidence, strongest first

Every early witness to the northern autumn festivals, dated and graded.
  • 2nd c. ADThe Coligny calendar (Gaul; a bronze tablet found near Coligny, Ain, in 1897). A month named SAMON[IOS], with a notation usually read as “the three nights of Samonios”. It is the only pre-Christian written witness to the name, but it says nothing about what was done, and scholars still argue about where in the year the month fell.
  • 725Bede, De temporum ratione ch. 15, on the English pagan months: November was Blótmonath, “month of sacrifice, because in it they devoted to their gods the cattle they were about to kill”. This is the earliest secure statement anywhere in the north that the beginning of winter was a pagan sacrificial season. Bede names a slaughter, not the dead.
  • 8th–10th c.The Old Irish tales. Tochmarc Emire names the four quarter-days (Samuin, Imbolc, Beltaine, Brón Trogain), with Samhain beginning winter. Royal assemblies, among them the Feast of Tara, are set at Samhain. Echtra Nerai, Aislinge Óenguso (the swan-maiden who changes shape every other Samhain) and Serglige Con Culainn (set at the Ulstermen's Samhain assembly) all make it the night when the otherworld comes through. The manuscripts are later, from c. 1100 to 1500, but the language of the oldest tales is Old Irish.
  • c. 800–835All Saints moves to 1 November. It was already kept on that day in Alcuin's Northumbrian circle by 800, and made general in the Frankish church under Louis the Pious in 835. The Irish church meanwhile kept its feast of all saints in April. The popular claim that the Church moved the feast to swallow Samhain is not supported by the sources.
  • c. 998All Souls, 2 November, instituted at Cluny under Abbot Odilo. Its spread through the following centuries is what ties the dead to the beginning of November, with bell-ringing, soul-cakes and the belief that the dead visit their old homes. It is the source of most of what people now call “Samhain lore”.
  • c. 1019Sigvatr Þórðarson, Austrfararvísur, contemporary skaldic verse by a Christian Icelandic poet travelling in Sweden. He is turned away from farm after farm because an álfablót, a sacrifice to the elves, is going on inside. It is the only eyewitness account from the Norse world of an autumn rite behind closed doors.
  • 13th–14th c.The Icelandic sagas (written two or three centuries after the events they describe) set feasts and hauntings at vetrnætr, the Winter Nights in mid-to-late October: the dísablót, the haustboð or autumn feast, and in Þiðranda þáttr the killing of a young man by supernatural women on Winter Nights.
  • 1549 →The Scottish evidence begins. The Complaynt of Scotland (1549) names “the tayl of the yong tamlene” among the tales shepherds told. The ballad itself, with Tam Lin rescued from the fairies on Hallowe'en night, is first written down in the 18th century. Burns's Halloween (1785) documents the divination customs of Ayrshire in detail.
  • 19th–20th c.The folklore collections. In Ireland: the bonfires, the guising, the turnip lanterns, foot-water thrown out with a warning call, and the door left unlatched for the dead on All Souls' night. The Irish Folklore Commission's Schools' Collection of 1937–39 recorded many of them in children's own hands. In Wales, the Hwch Ddu Gwta, the tailless black sow that takes the last one home from the Calan Gaeaf bonfire. In Brittany, Anatole Le Braz's La Légende de la mort (1893), with the dead walking on the Night of the Dead.

One thread runs from the top of this list to the bottom. The household rule in Echtra Nerai (don't leave washing-water standing overnight, and rake the fire) was still being recited by Irish schoolchildren in 1938. That is the strongest case in the series for a belief carried unbroken across a thousand years, and it is a rule about a house, not about a festival.

The apparatus

Each tale gets the seven layers the Pendragon tales have. The original text sits beside an original translation, movement by movement and with notes, alongside a cast, a character web, a Propp story graph, a Greimas desire diagram, a motif index and a storybook retelling. Layer six, the mythograph, is computed from the other five. The original text always comes from a public-domain edition, and the scan it was taken from is linked and kept.

What the series is looking for

Propp's morphology ends with the hero coming home and marrying. The first tale in this series breaks that ending: Nera marries on the far side of the hill, goes back in, and does not return. The working hypothesis of the series is that the northern autumn tales are the tales without a ↓ Return. Their heroes are taken, kept, made to waste away, or visited by the dead who can stay only until cockcrow. Each new tale's story graph tests that hypothesis, and the series will say so if a tale breaks it. After two tales it became the departure is the doorstep. The third tale broke it: Muirchertach returns, and the return kills him. Then it read: the danger is at the door of the house on the festival night, in either direction. The fourth tale took out a word: Táin Bó Regamna has no festival. It now reads: the danger is at the threshold, at night, in either direction, and the house is never the safe place. The fifth tale held, and added a corollary: nobody comes back across it unmarked. Serglige's hero comes home whole only by forgetting. See Analysis.

What it will not do

It will not call anything “pagan” without a source that says so, and it will not present 19th-century folk custom as Iron Age religion. When a modern Halloween belief appears in an old tale, as foot-water does in Echtra Nerai, both ends of the line are dated, and the gap between them is left visible.
