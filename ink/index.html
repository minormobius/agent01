Only ink re-runs the organism — and even that cannot change the verdict above, because reserve decides how far a brush travels and provably never touches the field. The rest repaint the strokes you already have.

what this is

Two populations of Fluoddity organisms are dropped onto one sheet. Each is a swarm steering on a chemical trail it lays down itself — but both write into and sense the same field, so the first hand’s wet marks bend the second hand’s path. That interference is the picture.

Fluoddity keeps a velocity field and renders it as light. This keeps the paths, which is what lets it hold a pen. Each population has a broad-edge nib at its own angle: a stroke running along the edge is a hairline, one running across it is full width, so the thicks and thins come from the organism’s own turning rather than from a filter. Dwelling broadens the mark, a fast drag thins it, and opacity falls as the brush dries out. Pigment composites subtractively onto paper, so where the two hands cross you get a third colour rather than a blown-out white.

And where one hand crosses the other’s fresh ink, the mark bleeds. Each population keeps a decaying map of the pigment it has just laid down; every stroke records how much of the other hand’s wet ink it ran through, and blooms a soft halo under itself in proportion. Roughly the top tenth of strokes are wet enough to show it.

why the duds don’t reach the sheet

Most randomly drawn genomes are boring. So a roll isn’t one draw, it’s up to three, each scored by fluoddity’s own rubric — coverage, motion, spatial coherence, blow-out, read off two snapshots so an organism that looks alive once and then dies is caught. The first candidate to clear the bar gets painted; if it repeats something already on your wall this session, it spends one more candidate looking for something you haven’t seen.

The scoring run is not a preview. It is the painting’s own first 120 steps — same field, same agents, same integration — kept and replayed when the candidate passes. A cheaper probe would be a different substrate, and would certify an organism you never get shown.

the same picture, everywhere

copy link puts the two genomes in the URL, so a shared sheet paints identically on any machine. That needs more care than it sounds like: the simulation is a chaotic feedback loop, so it uses its own sin/cos rather than the platform’s (Math.sin is not specified to the bit, and one bit at step three is a different painting by step four hundred), and genomes are minted already snapped to the grid the URL encodes.
