light in four dimensions

Refraction doesn't care how many dimensions there are. Where a ray meets glass, the ray, the surface's normal and the bent ray all lie in one plane, and in that plane Snell's law holds as always: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Past the critical angle the light reflects instead. A mirror reflects. Glass bends blue more than red, so white light parts into colours.

What changes is the room. A 4D body is bounded by 3D cells, and a 4D prism is a triangle times a square: it bends light only in the plane of its triangle. Turn that plane until it holds w, and the beam goes ana or kata. A wall that fills our space can be crossed by going over it in w. And a ray meets a 3D slice in a single point, so in the slice a beam is a spark, a bright point where it pierces our space (ringed teal if it is heading ana, rose if kata). Push the lever to follow it. The shadow shows every beam whole.

Targets are solids: a ray almost never hits anything thinner than 4D, a flat patch or a point. Light a target by sending the beam through it.

how it's drawn, and checked

Every beam is traced exactly: straight lines, Snell's law in vector form at each glass cell, reflection at mirrors, total internal reflection where it happens. White light is traced as red (650 nm), green (530 nm) and blue (450 nm), with a dense flint glass's spread of index (Cauchy: n = 1.6 + 0.02/λ², so 1.65 for red, 1.70 for blue). The selftest (test/optics.selftest.mjs) checks Snell's law on 2,000 random rays in 4D, the critical angle, the prism's least deviation against 2 asin(n sin 30°) − 60°, that turning a whole level at random changes nothing but its position, that light retraces its path, and that every level is solved at its solution and not at its start. Levels are designed backwards: pieces set solved, the beam traced, targets put where it goes.

Controls are CAD⁴'s: drag to look round, pinch or scroll to zoom, the pad twists your slice into w, the lever moves it, ⊞ squares it up.
