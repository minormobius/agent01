what's calculated

The moonlet is a ball of rock in four dimensions, 100 m in radius, with hills. Its surface is a 3-sphere: a whole three-dimensional world, closed up on itself. Nothing spins it: what holds you down is its own gravity, four's pinned law (the pull falls as 1/r², so orbits close, as ours do), 0.5 m/s² at the surface, a twentieth of Earth's.

You and the bike are 100 kg plus up to 2 kg of nitrogen. Every 1/120 s the page adds up the forces on you, ground or air, and steps you on: gravity, GMm/r² toward the centre; the rocket, 180 N along the nose, burning 0.25 kg/s, so you get lighter and a full tank is 14.2 m/s of Δv (Tsiolkovsky: vₑ ln(m₀/m₁)); the ground, whatever push keeps you on the surface given how it curves under you, solved each step; and the tyres, pedals (300 W), brakes and the sideways force that makes the bike go where it points, never more than μ times the ground's push. Show forces to see the four as arrows.

What falls out: riding round the curve takes v²/R of the pull, so the ground pushes less the faster you go, and the tyres grip less. At 7.07 m/s it pushes nothing and you're in orbit; the pedals get you there only in the limit, closing in over a minute. A hill's crest curves away faster than the sphere, so the ground lets go of you there sooner: over a slalom kicker at 3.6 m/s or more, you fly. Gravity on a slope slows you climbing and speeds you down (the selftest holds energy to a thousandth). Turn harder than the grip and the tyres slide; the grip limiter (like traction control) holds the handlebars to what they can grip; raw lets you slide.

In the air nothing turns you but the reaction wheels, at the rates you command: W S pitch the nose, the stick turns it. Point it and burn. SAS uses the same wheels to keep your wheels toward the ground (never the nose); off, you stay however you were turned. Land on your wheels at under 3 m/s or crash and bounce. The forecast line is where you'll fly with the rocket off, the ✕ where you'll come down. Circularize is an autopilot: it turns the nose to the Δv that makes your orbit circular and burns. Refuel standing on the pad.

the slalom

Ten gates along ring 1, each set 4 m off the course: right, ana, left, kata, round and round. On a three-dimensional ground a gate is a ring of posts round its centre, across the course, and you have to go through the disk inside it. Your slice cuts the ring at two posts, coloured like the kerbs: teal where they stand ana of the gate's centre, pink kata, white when your slice runs through the middle. A gate set ana of the course is invisible until you lean ana toward it; then pink posts appear (you're still kata of its centre: keep leaning ana) and whiten as you line up. Three kickers on the course will throw you if you're fast. Miss a gate: +5 s.

steering through the roads

The moonlet's surface is three-dimensional, so a road on it is a tube, 5 m across, round its centre line: there's room beside it to your right and left, and ana and kata of it too. Your view is a slice, and it cuts across the tube. Its kerbs are coloured by which side of the centre they lie on, against your slice (tailspin's trick): teal where they're ana of it, pink kata, white where your slice runs through the middle. So: teal kerbs, lean kata (Q, or the stick down); pink kerbs, lean ana (E, stick up); white, and the dashed centre line appears, you're on it. Off-centre, the tube looks narrower: at 2.5 m out it's gone. (Ball-lock style turning of your slice changes which way is ana, so set slice to free to steer by colour.)

knots on a three-dimensional world

The roads are two great circles in completely orthogonal planes (ice and violet) and a gold trefoil knot on the torus halfway between them. On the moonlet's surface, a three-dimensional world, they're tied as tight as rope: the two circles are linked once (the Hopf link) and the trefoil winds three times round one and twice round the other. The selftest measures it with Gauss's linking integral: −1.000, −3.000, −2.000.

Yet in the four-dimensional space the moonlet sits in, nothing can be knotted: there's a whole extra direction to lift any piece of a string over any other. A knot needs exactly three dimensions round it. The moonlet's skin has three, so its roads are knotted; the space it floats in has four, so the same curves, seen from there, are just loops. Ride ring 1 and you go round ring 2 once; fly up into orbit and you can leave them all behind.

The two little moons orbit at one height in those same two orthogonal planes. Each is always exactly √2 times its orbit's radius from the other, and they never meet: two orthogonal planes in four dimensions share only their centre point.

how it's drawn

One fragment shader ray-traces your three-dimensional slice (your forward, up and right; the fourth way, w, hidden) of the rock, the roads, the beacons, the moons and your bike, all as four-dimensional shapes: its wheels are rings in the plane of its heading and its up. The sun is a direction in four dimensions, so the light falls on the true 4D surface and shadows are cast through 4D. The overlay projects the forecast and the beacons into your slice: teal where they lie ana of it, pink kata. With slice follows the beacon your across-directions turn slowly about your heading so the next beacon stays in your slice; free, you twist it with the pad or J and L.
