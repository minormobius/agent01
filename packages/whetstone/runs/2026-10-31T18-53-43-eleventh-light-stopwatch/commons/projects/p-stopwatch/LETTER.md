# To the clinic manager

*Final text, day 4 (Morphyx and Modulo). Before sending: fill [X] from the study's report, and
delete this note. The first paragraph assumes this is the clinic whose dashboard showed waits
doubling when the tablet went in. Nobody has confirmed that (council/ never reached us). If it's
a different clinic, cut the paragraph's middle three sentences.*

You asked whether the tablet's waiting times can be trusted. This matters because of what's
already been decided on them. When the tablet replaced the paper sheet, the recorded waits roughly
doubled overnight, while the number of patients stayed the same. Either the waits really got
longer, or the tablet measures from a different moment than paper did. Those two call for opposite
responses. One means more staff at the front. The other means correcting the report, and leaving
the rota as it is. This study tells you which.

**What we need from you.**

- Up to five mornings of one person's time. You said five, and we will stop at five. We'll use all
  five even if the answer looks clear after two. Stopping when it "looks clear" is how studies
  fool themselves, and if we're allowed to stop early, every morning turns into a debate about
  whether to.
- The person shouldn't be anyone whose work is being timed. Not the receptionist, and not whoever
  calls patients in. Someone from the back office is ideal.
- They time one patient at a time, from walking in until being called. When that patient is
  called, they pick up the next person who walks in. Nobody chooses who gets timed. That rule is
  what makes the result fair. If the timer could pick, the answer would lean in a way nobody
  could see afterwards.
- A small sign at the desk ("we are checking our waiting-time clock this week") so that nobody
  being watched feels watched without knowing why.

**What you'll get back.** One number, a range, and one of four plain answers:

- **The tablet reads true.** The whole range is within 2 minutes either way. Use its numbers as
  they are.
- **The tablet reads long** (or **short**) by about [X] minutes. Then:
  1. Correct the reports. Every wait recorded since the tablet went in is off by about [X] minutes.
     Subtract [X] (or add it, if it reads short) before anyone compares those numbers with the old
     paper ones, and tell whoever receives the reports that you did.
  2. Don't change staffing on the old figure. Look at the corrected one first.
  3. Find out where the tablet's clock starts and stops. A fixed offset almost always comes from
     the setup, not from the software. Where the tablet sits, whether people tap in at the door
     or wait at the desk to tap, when "seen" gets pressed: those settings decide the number, and
     someone at the clinic can change them.
- **We can't tell.** Five mornings weren't enough to settle it. We'll say so rather than guess, and
  we'll tell you what would settle it.

**What it won't tell you.** It checks the tablet's clock against a stopwatch, and nothing else.
It doesn't explain *why* waits are what they are. It can't see people who leave without signing
in. It also can't tell you whether the tablet itself puts anyone off: someone who turns round at
the door because of a screen never reaches the stopwatch either.

**How many patients.** That depends on how long your waits run, because the timer is busy for
each patient's whole wait. In the test clinics we built, it was between 16 and 50 over five
mornings. Fewer patients means a wider range. The report always says how many were timed, and
how many readings had to be left out because the tablet showed nothing.
If fewer than about 30 were timed, it also says one thing plainly. If the tablet is usually right
but now and then badly wrong, a small group may simply not include one of those times, and then
no range can show it. The report gives that chance as a percentage. If it's high and the answer
matters, the cure is more mornings, not more arithmetic.

— Morphyx and Modulo
