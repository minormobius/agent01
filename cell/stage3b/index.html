Stage 3b · the genome, running

455 genes, 503 ribosomes

This is Stage 1's engine at the scale of the real cell. Every protein-coding gene of JCVI-syn3A is transcribed and translated as exact random events. The machines that do it are finite and shared: 187 RNA polymerases and 503 ribosomes for the whole genome. A job takes real time, set by the gene's real length, and while a machine is busy no other gene can use it. Watch polymerases crawl along the chromosome, and see what happens when the budget changes.

The machines

An RNA polymerase copies a gene at 20 letters per second, so a 1,000-letter gene keeps it busy for 50 s. A ribosome builds a protein at 10 amino acids per second, so a typical 283-amino-acid protein takes about half a minute. When every ribosome is busy, a new mRNA has to wait.

The events

When a machine starts a job is random, as in Stage 1. When it finishes is set by the length. mRNAs fall apart at a rate set by their length. Proteins are thinned out as the cell grows and divides every 105 minutes. That's about 30 events every second, across the whole genome.

What is fitted, what is not

Each gene's promoter strength and ribosome-binding rate are set so that, on average, the cell holds its measured protein counts and published mRNA levels. Everything else comes out of the simulation and is not fitted: the noise, the bursts, the queues for machines, and every response to a knockout or a change in budget.

Three things to try

Overload one gene

Turn up one long gene 50 times. Its mRNAs soak up ribosomes, the busy-ribosome line climbs, and every other protein slows down. Engineers adding genes to bacteria see this cost as slower growth, called burden.

Halve the ribosomes

Cut the ribosomes from 503 to 250. The same share stays busy, about 70%, so protein output roughly halves. It's the same genome and the same promoters, with less machinery.

Knock out GAPDH

The cell's most abundant protein. Its mRNA is gone within minutes, but 1,300 copies of the protein only dilute away, halving every 105 minutes. Cells feel a knockout late.

Where the numbers come from

Gene positions and lengths come from the genome (NCBI CP016816.2). Protein targets are the measured counts of Breuer et al. 2019. mRNA targets are the averages from Thornburg et al. 2022. Machine counts and speeds, mRNA decay, and protein degradation follow Thornburg et al. 2022: 187 polymerases, 503 ribosomes, 10 amino acids per second, and 3.5 nucleotides of mRNA per second. Undetected proteins are given 10 copies, as in that model.

What is still missing

Energy and raw materials. Here, building a protein costs nothing, and amino acids and nucleotides never run out. Also missing: rRNA and tRNA transcription, regulation, and real division (dilution is continuous). Each of these limits the cell in reality, so this cell is a little too free.

Next layer: 3c, now live

Metabolism underneath. Glycolysis makes the ATP and GTP that every ribosome step costs, and transporters bring in the nucleotides and amino acids. When energy runs low, ribosomes stall, and that's how a metabolic knockout reaches gene expression.
