Stage 3d · growth and division

One cell becomes two

Every earlier stage assumed the cell's size, its ribosome count, and that it would somehow divide every 105 minutes. Here all three come out of the simulation. The cell gets heavier by making protein, builds its own ribosomes from ribosomal proteins, copies its chromosome when enough DnaA has gathered at the origin, and splits once the copy is done and it has doubled. Then we follow one daughter, generation after generation.

Mass and volume

The cell's mass is its protein. Volume grows with it, from about 180 nm in radius at birth to about 225 nm before division. In a bigger cell the same molecules are more spread out, so everything that has to find a partner slows down.

Ribosomes from protein

Ribosome and RNA polymerase counts follow the live levels of their protein subunits. More ribosomes make protein faster, including the proteins for more ribosomes. That compounding is why growth is exponential.

Copying the chromosome

DnaA proteins build a 30-unit filament at the origin, and units can also fall off. When it's complete, two forks run in opposite directions around the circle at 100 letters per second. Each gene's copy number doubles as a fork passes it.

Splitting

Once the copy is finished and the cell has doubled its birth mass, it divides. Every protein, mRNA and busy ribosome goes to one daughter or the other by a coin flip, so sisters are never quite equal.

Four things to try

Knock out dnaA

The DnaA already made may fire the origin once more. After that, daughters never have enough, so the chromosome is never copied again and the cell never divides. It just keeps growing.

Make 4× more DnaA

Initiation fires almost at birth. Division still waits for the cell to double, so the cycle barely changes. Size, not DnaA, sets the pace here.

Slow ribosome production

Halve every ribosomal-protein gene. Fewer ribosomes means slower protein making and slower growth, and the cycles stretch out.

Starve it

Drop the glucose. Growth stops within minutes: the cell holds its size, waiting. Put glucose back and the lineage carries on.

Where the numbers come from

Fork speed (100 letters per second) and the DnaA binding constants (one high-affinity site, two low-affinity sites, a 30-unit filament, binding 100 per mM per second, unbinding 0.55/s) are those of Thornburg et al. Gene positions come from the genome. Metabolism is 3c's network plus two reactions that model defines in its code: the phosphate importer, with 134 mM phosphate in the medium, and its own ATP synthase.

What this layer found

With those two reactions in place, ATP settles at about 3.5 mM and GTP at about 1.6 mM. Those are the published concentrations, and nothing was tuned to reach them. In 3c, GTP sat on a knife edge because the network had no way to bring in phosphate. Glycolysis locked phosphate away in sugar phosphates, a classic glycolytic phosphate trap. The cycle time comes out near the lab's 105 minutes. It isn't an independent prediction, because 3b's rates were calibrated to that growth rate, but it now has to emerge from replication, ribosome building and division working together.

What is our choice

The cell divides when its chromosome is copied and it has doubled its birth mass. Ribosome and polymerase counts follow the median level of their subunit proteins. Promoters are normalised so the population-average cell, with its mix of one- and two-copy genes, keeps 3b's calibration. Metabolite pools are assumed to be made as fast as the cell grows. Volume follows protein mass, and we follow one daughter. There's no space yet: that's Stage 4.
