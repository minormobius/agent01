Stage 3a · the parts list

Every gene in the cell

Stages 1 and 2 used made-up genes. From here on, the cell is real. JCVI-syn3A is a bacterium built in a lab by deleting every gene its ancestor could live without. What's left is the smallest genome of any cell that grows on its own. This page lays out all of it: where each gene sits on the single circular chromosome, what it does, and how many copies of its protein a cell holds, as measured by mass spectrometry. Nothing is simulated yet. This is the parts list the next layers will run.

Outer ring: genes read clockwise. Inner ring: genes read anticlockwise. Spikes: protein copies per cell, on a log scale. Hover a gene, click to open it.

Notice that the right half sits mostly on the outer ring and the left half on the inner. Copying of the chromosome starts at the top and runs both ways round to the bottom, and most genes point the same way it travels, so the machines that copy DNA and the machines that read it rarely meet head-on.

What is measured

Positions come from the genome sequence (NCBI CP016816.2). Protein copies are absolute counts from quantitative mass spectrometry (Breuer et al. 2019). Genes added when syn3A was built, and a few small proteins, were not detected and show no spike.

What is simulated

The mRNA figure in each gene's card is the average from the Luthey-Schulten lab's whole-cell simulations (Thornburg et al. 2022), not a measurement. Most genes average less than one mRNA: at any moment their message usually isn't there at all, which is the bursting from Stage 1 at real scale.

Next layer: 3b

Make these genes run. Every gene is transcribed and translated as exact random events, as in Stage 1, using real gene lengths and a finite pool of ribosomes and RNA polymerases that all 496 genes compete for. The ring above becomes the live view: genes flash as they're read.
