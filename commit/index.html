Both attacks always succeed in the toy. At 256 bits of hash and 128 bits of nonce they still exist, but they take longer than the age of the universe. That gap is the whole escape from the trade-off: real commitments are secure against a bounded opponent, not an unlimited one.

The problem

In 1981 Manuel Blum asked how two people who don't trust each other could flip a fair coin over the telephone. If Alice flips and reports, she can lie. If Bob calls first, Alice hears the call before she reports. His answer was “Coin flipping by telephone” (CRYPTO 1981): Alice commits to a bit, Bob calls, Alice opens, and the coin is the XOR of the two. If either of them chooses at random, the XOR is a fair coin, whatever the other does.

A commitment is a sealed envelope made of arithmetic. It needs two properties. Hiding: Bob learns nothing about the bit from the envelope. Binding: Alice can't open it to a different bit later.

Why you can't have both

Shrink the scheme until it can be counted. A nonce is one of 2ʳ values, a commitment one of 2ᵏ. Count how many nonces send each bit to each value: that gives two histograms, D₀ and D₁, and they are the stage above.

Bob's best guess at the bit from c is right with probability (1 + δ)/2, where δ is the statistical distance:

δ = ½ · Σ꜀ |D₀(c) − D₁(c)| / 2ʳ

Alice can lie about c exactly when both bits reach it. Call p the chance that an honest commitment lands on such a value. Every column where the bars overlap contributes min(D₀, D₁) to both quantities, and those minimums sum to 1 − δ. So for every scheme there is:

p ≥ 1 − δ

Perfect hiding (δ = 0) forces p = 1: every commitment opens both ways. Perfect binding (p = 0) forces δ = 1: Bob reads the bit straight off c. Shift is the first extreme, tag is the second, and hash sits in between, moving as you trade r against k.

The way out

The theorem is about an opponent with unlimited time. Real schemes give up one property against that opponent and keep it against anyone who has to do the work. A SHA-256 commitment with a long nonce is statistically hiding and computationally binding: the double openings exist, but finding one is a 2¹²⁸-step search. Pedersen's commitment (1991) makes the same trade with discrete logarithms in place of a hash.

Checked

The engine, commit.js, is the only copy of the maths, and the page and commit.selftest.mjs both import it: 11,170 checks. They cover SHA-256 against the NIST vectors and node's crypto; commitments that open only one way; Blum's coin fair whenever one side is uniform; p ≥ 1 − δ on 2,960 schemes, including 2,000 random functions; Bob's brute force agreeing with the census; and the mean cost of a double opening matching √(π·2ᵏ) at k = 8, 12 and 16.
