WIRE⁴. A wire in four dimensions is a ball in three directions and long in the fourth. Put it across a stream and, at every place along it, the flow round it is the flow round a sphere in our world: the top picture. Behind a sphere at this speed sits a bubble of slow water turning back on itself.

The second picture is the one our world has no room for: a slice that holds the wire's own length. If nothing varied along the wire it would be the same from bottom to top. It isn't. Above a Reynolds number of about 191 the bubble grows fat and thin by turns along the wire, about every 2.5 diameters. A sphere's wake is stable until 212; this ripple needs the extra direction, so the wire's wake gives way first.

The pulse a wire across a stream that is fastest in the middle: the slice x, w, played in time

In a pipe the stream is fastest on the axis and slower toward the wall, so the wire does not see one speed along its length. This is a first model of that: the wire is the band on the left, the stream's axis runs through the middle of the picture, and the stream falls away above and below it (to rest, 15.7 diameters out; the picture shows the middle 14). There is no wall in it yet. The ripple of the pictures above never appears. The bubble gathers into one bulge on the axis, and in a wide enough stream, past a Reynolds number between 250 and 280, the bulge will not hold still: its tip on the axis draws out into a narrow point and sinks back, once every 30 to 40 flow times. Nothing breaks off and goes downstream; the bubble stays in one piece. The curve under the picture is the bubble's length on the axis through one cycle, as computed; the picture loops it. At 280 the cycle repeats exactly. At 320 it does not, so that one is 45 flow times of the run and jumps where it starts again.

What is not settled: this run is coarse along the wire, so the size of the swing is rough; it keeps the flow symmetric round the stream's axis; and the same wire in a true tube with a wall has so far been computed only in a narrower tube, where the flow stays steady.

The instrument a hot wire, or a hot ribbon, across a 4D pipe: set the flow, read the current

A hot-wire anemometer is a thin wire stretched across a pipe and heated by a current. The stream cools it, a cooler wire has less resistance, and a circuit raises the current until the resistance is back where it was: the current is the reading. This one is computed for a 4D pipe. The top picture is the wire from wall to wall, coloured by how hot each stretch runs, with the stream's speed above it. The curve underneath is the calibration: the current (squared) needed at each flow, drawn from zero so that a flat curve looks flat, with a dot where you are.

Three things to try. Shorten the wire: the cold wall at each end takes the heat, the middle is all that is left warm, and the curve goes flat, so the instrument stops telling one flow from another. Slow the flow: the curve flattens there too, and that one is four-dimensional. Each stretch of this wire loses heat the way a ball does, and a ball loses heat to still fluid at a steady rate that the stream only adds to. A wire in our world has no such floor. Flatten the profile at the same flow rate: the reading drops, because the wire answers to the speed along its own length, most of all where it runs hottest, and not to the flow through the pipe. Read with the wrong profile, the flow rate can be out by half.

Then switch to the ribbon. A ribbon is long in two directions where a wire is long in one, and the stream can only pass round it two ways, as it passes round a wire in our world. So each patch of it loses heat as our hot wire does, with no floor, and at low speed it tells one flow from another several times better than the wire (the dashed curve is the other probe, scaled to start at the same height). It does not do as well as it might: losing little heat to the stream, it loses more of it sideways through its own metal into the rim where it meets the pipe. Widen the pipe, or make the metal a poorer conductor, and the ribbon's curve steepens.

Each stretch of the wire is taken to lose heat as a ball does at the local speed, which matches the full tube computation to about 1% for a metal wire; each patch of the ribbon as a rod between the pipe's two walls, which matches to about 4%. The flattened profiles are stand-ins, not a computed entrance flow, and the ribbon is heated evenly, with no account of how a current would be fed through it.

Up the Reynolds ladder how fast each kind of disturbance grows on the uniform wake

Each line is one way the wake can break its symmetry round the stream's axis: not at all (0), tilting to one side (1), or in two or three lobes. Across is how tightly the disturbance is rippled along the wire; the left edge is no ripple at all, which is all a sphere can do. Up is how fast it grows; below the line it dies. A filled dot marks a disturbance that oscillates as it grows, an open one a steady one. Past 191 the uniform wake is not what a wire would have, so this is what the flow is trying to do, not what it does.
