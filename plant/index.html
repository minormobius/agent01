The summon inspector

A cell in the foam is the intersection of half-spaces, one per neighbouring seed — so a cell's faces are its bisectors, and its face normals are its neighbour directions. Put a seed in the middle and one along each face normal of a Platonic solid, and the foam grows that solid. These are not models; there is no mesh. Everything below is points.

Orange dot the cell's own seed · pale dots its neighbours · blue the dual polyhedron they imply · dashed circle where those seeds would sit if the metric had no grain. Drag to orbit.

Level 1 — the first thing here you can break

Everything above is a primitive. This is a level: a source, a processor, a sink, and a constraint that bites. The verdict underneath is not a description — it is feasible(), the same oracle CI runs, called on the same level the test grades. If it says infeasible here, the gate says infeasible.

Drag it down. The smelter can only run at capacity 51, so the depot’s demand of 50 has almost no slack — somewhere around 50 the whole chain stops being satisfiable, and the verdict tells you which link starved and by how much.

Does that failure feel fair? A puzzle whose margin is 2% is either taut and satisfying or fiddly and mean, and no test can tell the difference. That is the kind of question this project has to ask people rather than machines — the loop that built this can prove the level is satisfiable and cannot tell you whether it is any good.

Level 2 — pick the machine

Level 1 is a slider: ease the ore rate down and watch a label go from green to red. This is not that. Three smelters, one choice each, no dragging toward the answer — you pick one, the factory either runs or it does not.

The ore mine only supplies 55/tick and the depot demands 50 — the smelter's own capacity number is not the only thing that can bottleneck it.

Level 3 — two knobs, either one can break it

Level 1 is one slider. Level 2 is one choice. Both have exactly one thing that can ever be the bottleneck. This is two capacities, set independently — the miner and the smelter each cap the chain on their own, and starving either one starves the depot.

The ore mine supplies far more than either machine can ever use — the depot demands 44, and dragging either capacity below that starves it. Neither knob is safe to ignore.

Level 4 — one source, two sinks, and no favourites

Levels 1-3 are a straight line: one thing to starve, however you get there. This is the loop's first fan-out — one ore source feeding two stockpiles at once, split automatically in proportion to what each one demands (autoSplit(), not a hand-picked ratio). Drag the source down and watch both stockpiles go short together, by different amounts, because the split does not pick a favourite.

stockpileA demands 30, stockpileB demands 70 — 100 total. The shipped rate of 102 clears both by 2%, proportionally split. Drop it below 100 and both stockpiles fall short at once, by the same percentage but not the same number of units.

What to look at

Push anisotropy up. The seeds pull away from the dashed circle, because the foam measures vertical distance differently from horizontal — grade has to stay a meaningful thing to climb. The solid is still exact: every face is still where it should be, to floating point. It is the space that is stretched, not the shape that is wrong.

Now tick naive placement — seeds at unit directions times a common radius, the obvious way to do it. Watch the verdict. Then switch to a cube and tick it again: the cube is fine. Its face normals are axis-aligned and the metric cannot rotate those, so the first solid anyone tries looks perfect and the bug waits in the second one. That is the trap this code was written to survive, and the error reported is measured live rather than quoted.

Why this page exists

This surface is the work product of an agent loop that writes its own tickets and grades itself against gates it also writes. A system like that gets very good at what it can measure and blind to everything else. The correction is people from outside it — which requires there to be something you can open. So: does the squash read as the world having a grain you could learn to use, or as the shapes being broken? There is no test for that, which is exactly why it is being asked.
