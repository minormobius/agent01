Forces between molecules

A classical force field, term by term, worked out under the pinned 1/r: what carries over, what changes, and the new one.

The force field, term by term

A molecular-dynamics program moves atoms by a force field: springs for bonds, springs for the angles between them, a twist term for each bond, charges, and a pair force between atoms that aren't bonded. In our world that pair force is usually Lennard-Jones, 4ε[(σ/R)¹² − (σ/R)⁶]. Each term comes from somewhere in 3D physics. Here is each one worked out again with the force pinned at 1/r. All of it follows from one fact: the pinned potential is the Fourier transform of 4π²/|k|³ in 4D.

So the pair force for the molecular-dynamics view is V(R) = A e^(−βR) − B/R⁵ − C₆/R⁶, plus charges where there are any.

Neutral atoms attract, permanently

A neutral atom's charge, seen in Fourier space, starts at S k²/8, where S = Σ⟨r²⟩ over its electrons. Two such atoms meet through S_A S_B k⁴/64 times 4π²/|k|³, which is proportional to |k|, and |k| transforms in 4D to −3/(4π² R⁵). So any two neutral atoms attract as −3 S_A S_B / 64 R⁵. Nothing fluctuates and nothing is polarized: it's the plain electrostatics of two spherical clouds, the shell theorem's failure again. In 3D the same step gives k⁴·4π/k², which has no reach at all. That's why our neutral spherical atoms don't feel each other until they touch.

Checked against an exact calculation: two model atoms (a nucleus in a Gaussian cloud) whose 4D interaction is integrated directly come within 1.3% of the formula at twenty-four cloud-widths apart, closing in as they separate. Closer in, the exact pull is stronger than the formula (by 14% at eight cloud-widths), so the formula undersells it at contact.

At the distances where our noble-gas atoms sit together, this permanent pull alone is many times deeper than their whole van der Waals well. The 4D noble gases would hardly be gases.

That assumes 4D atoms sit about where ours do. Where they really sit is set by the repulsion's strength A, which needs the overlap of two clouds and isn't computed yet.

Dispersion: still R⁻⁶, a little stronger

Two atoms' electrons jiggle, and each jiggle polarizes the other: London's force. Two dipoles meet through ∂ᵢ∂ⱼ(1/R) = (3R̂ᵢR̂ⱼ − δᵢⱼ)/R³, which is the same in any dimension because the potential is 1/r. So dispersion stays −C₆/R⁶. Its strength comes from the sum of the squares of that tensor's parts: (−2)² along the line between the atoms plus 1 for each direction across it. That's 6 in our world and 7 in 4D, so London's 3/2 becomes 7/4. Checked by solving two coupled 4D oscillators exactly: their zero-point energy drops by 7/4's prediction to one part in ten million.

What C₆ actually is for a 4D atom needs its polarizability α, and the solver can't give one: it keeps every atom spherical, so a field can't push it lopsided. (The usual shortcut, Unsöld's estimate from the orbitals, misses our own neon's α by a factor of 5.6, so we don't use it.) That's open. For element 12, dispersion would only match the permanent R⁻⁵ at neon's spacing if its C₆ were about 85 atomic units, 13 times our neon's 6.4. Short of that, the permanent pull is the stronger of the two at every distance past contact, and further out it wins regardless, since R⁻⁵ outlasts R⁻⁶.

Repulsion and twisting

Repulsion is two clouds overlapping, which costs energy as e^(−βR). A bound orbital fades as e^(−√(2I) r) in any dimension (only the energy decides it), so β ≈ 2√(2I), the same rule as ours: about 2 per bohr for 4D's noble gases. Lennard-Jones's R⁻¹² is a convenience. The molecular-dynamics view should use the exponential. Its strength A is open, as above.

Twisting. Across a bond, each end's other bonds point to the corners of a regular simplex of the space across it: a triangle in 3D, a tetrahedron in 4D. Over 200,000 random twists of the far end, the least energy is the far tetrahedron inverted through the bond (staggered) and the most is the two lined up (eclipsed).

Salt water screens by a power law

In salt water the ions crowd round any charge and cancel its field. In our world the cancelled field fades exponentially, e^(−κr)/r: two ions a few nanometres apart in seawater barely feel each other. That exponential needs the transform to be smooth in k², and 4π²/|k|³ isn't. Screening gives 1/(|k|³/4π² + λ) = 1/λ − |k|³/(4π²λ²) + …, and the |k|³ term leaves a tail of −r⁻⁷, of the opposite sign to the charge. In this universe a dissolved ion is overscreened: past a few screening lengths it looks faintly like its opposite, and the effect fades only as a power.

The tail comes from the Fourier asymptotics of a non-smooth transform, the same machinery that gives the R⁻⁵ above, which the exact two-atom calculation confirms. The salt case itself isn't integrated numerically: its tail is far too small to resolve beside the 1/r it cancels.

What the molecular-dynamics view gets, and what's open

Ready: the bonded terms with 4D's angles and three-way twists, charges at 1/r, the permanent R⁻⁵ with S from the solver, dispersion's form with 7/4, and exponential repulsion with β from the solver. Open: the repulsion's strength A (it needs the overlap of two clouds), the polarizabilities behind C₆ (they need a solver that lets atoms deform), and retardation (in our world dispersion turns to R⁻⁷ beyond a few hundred bohr; through the lens it hasn't been worked out). Also open: hydrogen bonds, and the three-body forces that make our liquids non-additive.
