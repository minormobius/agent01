The force

Why 1/r, what it takes to rewrite Maxwell for it, the vacuum we chose, and what breaks.

Gauss's law leaves no atom

A charge's field lines spread over the sphere round it. In 3D that sphere's area grows as r², so the force falls as 1/r² and the potential as 1/r. In 4D the sphere is a 3-sphere, its size grows as r³, and the potential falls as 1/r². That sounds like a detail. It isn't: an electron's pull toward the nucleus then falls exactly as fast as its own resistance to being squeezed, the centrifugal term. One of them wins at every distance at once. Too weak a pull and nothing binds. Too strong and the electron falls into the nucleus. There's no distance an atom could settle at: no Bohr radius, no atoms, no chemistry. (Ehrenfest saw the classical version in 1917: no stable orbits either.)

So we pin the force: keep the potential at 1/r in four dimensions and see what happens. Everything in ATOM⁴ lives in that world. The rest of this chapter asks what it costs.

Can we rewrite Maxwell?

Pinning 1/r was a choice of potential. Now let's find a field theory that gives it, and look for what breaks. The starting point: in Maxwell's theory a charge's potential is the Fourier transform of 1/k², in any dimension, and that transform is 1/r in 3D and 1/r² in 4D. For 1/r in 4D we need 1/k³. There are five ways to get it, each with a cost, and a sixth, which is the one this universe takes.

Problems every route shares: no shell theorem, no Faraday cage

Our 1/r is special in 3D: it's harmonic (∇²(1/r) = 0 away from the charge). That one fact gives Gauss's law, Newton's shell theorem (a charged sphere acts like a point outside and has no field inside) and the Faraday cage. In 4D, 1/r isn't harmonic: ∇²(1/r) = −1/r³. The flux of the field through a sphere round a charge grows with the sphere's radius, as if empty space were charged. A charged sphere doesn't act like a point, and a hollow conductor doesn't shield its inside: the potential at its centre is 3π/8 = 1.18 times the potential at its wall, so a charge inside is pushed toward the wall.

A small fourth dimension

The way real physics gets 1/r from four dimensions is to make the fourth one small: a circle of circumference ℓ. Close to a charge (r ≪ ℓ) the field spreads in four directions and falls as 1/r². Far away it has filled the circle, can only spread in three, and falls as 1/r. That's how extra dimensions could hide in our world. But it doesn't give us 4D chemistry. Atoms much bigger than ℓ are 3D atoms, and atoms much smaller are back to having no ground state.

The big problem

Coulomb's 1/r isn't a free choice. In 3D, locality, linearity, relativity and positive probabilities together force it. In 4D the same four force 1/r², and then there are no atoms. To keep 4D atoms, something has to go. This universe gives up relativity for electromagnetism: the lens vacuum keeps light exactly as 4D Maxwell has it and keeps every field adding, but its pull reaches across space at once. That's an absolute "now", a preferred frame, set by one new length ℓ. What it does to light is the next chapter. What it does to atoms, through the failed shell theorem, is in the periodic table.
