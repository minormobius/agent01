ATOM⁴

The periodic table of a world with four dimensions of space, computed. A new chemistry, and a way to see why ours is the way it is. As in Lumen⁴, 3D is warm and 4D is ice, every number is computed, and a selftest checks them.

1 · In 4D, Gauss's law leaves no atom

A charge's field lines spread over the sphere round it. In 3D that sphere's area grows as r², so the force falls as 1/r² and the potential as 1/r. In 4D the sphere is a 3-sphere, its size grows as r³, and the potential falls as 1/r². That sounds like a detail. It isn't: an electron's pull toward the nucleus then falls exactly as fast as its own resistance to being squeezed, the centrifugal term. One of them wins at every distance at once. Too weak a pull and nothing binds. Too strong and the electron falls into the nucleus. There's no distance an atom could settle at: no Bohr radius, no atoms, no chemistry. (Ehrenfest saw the classical version in 1917: no stable orbits either.)

So we pin the force: keep the potential at 1/r in four dimensions and see what happens. That's the world of everything below. Section 6 asks what it costs.

2 · Hydrogen, exactly

With the force pinned, the electron's radial equation in 4D is our own, with one change: its angular momentum is shifted by a half, L = l + ½. So 4D hydrogen can be solved in closed form, like ours. Its levels are

3D: E = −Z² / 2n²    4D: E = −Z² / 2(n + ½)²    (in hartrees, 27.2 eV)

The ground state is bound by 6.05 eV, not 13.6, and the atom is twice the size (its average radius is 3 bohr to our 1.5). As in 3D, every level's energy depends only on n, not l. That's the same hidden symmetry that closes planets' orbits, and it survives in any dimension as long as the force is 1/r. But the shells are bigger, because a 4D electron has more ways to point: (l + 1)² orbitals for each l, where we have 2l + 1.

Its spectrum

The light 4D hydrogen gives out. Our Balmer lines (falling to n = 2) run red to violet. Its own fall to n = 2 crowds into the red and orange, ending in yellow at 570 nm, and its Lyman series is out in the ultraviolet.

3 · The periodic table

Beyond hydrogen there's no closed form. Each electron moves in the nucleus's pull and the averaged cloud of all the others, and the cloud depends on the electrons, so we iterate until they agree: Hartree's self-consistent field. For each atom the solver tries the filling the orbital energies suggest, and the neighbouring ones, and keeps the lowest total energy. To know how far to trust it, we ran the same code in 3D first: it gets 34 of our first 36 elements right. The two it misses, chromium and copper, are the famous exceptions, which need an effect it leaves out (section 7).

The 4D table's rows are 2, 10, 10, 28, 28 long where ours are 2, 8, 8, 18, 18: the same doubling, with 4D's bigger shells. Tap any element (on a phone, swipe the table sideways).

4 · Periodic, but with longer periods

The drops just after them are the alkali metals, one electron outside a full shell. The dots are our real measured values: the solver's 3D line has the right shape but sits about a third too low, because it leaves out exchange (section 7). Read the 4D numbers the same way.

One thing runs the other way. Our noble gases hold their electrons less tightly down the column (helium, neon, argon, krypton). 4D's hold them tighter: element 12, 22, 50, 78 each harder to ionize than the last. Section 6 shows why: it's the shell theorem failing. And so 4D atoms don't grow much down a column. The chart below is how far out the outermost electron sits.

5 · What chemistry would be like

Bonds point to the corners of a simplex: a triangle in 2D, a tetrahedron in 3D (109.5°, methane), a 5-cell in 4D (104.5°). These are the angles of the hybrid orbitals of a carbon-like atom. A real 4D molecule's angles would depend on the solver we don't have.

6 · Can we rewrite Maxwell?

Pinning 1/r was a choice of potential. Now let's find a field theory that gives it, and look for what breaks. The starting point: in Maxwell's theory a charge's potential is the Fourier transform of 1/k², in any dimension, and that transform is 1/r in 3D and 1/r² in 4D. For 1/r in 4D we need 1/k³. There are five ways to get it, and each one costs something.

Problems every route shares: no shell theorem, no Faraday cage

Our 1/r is special in 3D: it's harmonic (∇²(1/r) = 0 away from the charge). That one fact gives Gauss's law, Newton's shell theorem (a charged sphere acts like a point outside and has no field inside) and the Faraday cage. In 4D, 1/r isn't harmonic: ∇²(1/r) = −1/r³. The flux of the field through a sphere round a charge grows with the sphere's radius, as if empty space were charged. A charged sphere doesn't act like a point, and a hollow conductor doesn't shield its inside: the potential at its centre is 3π/8 = 1.18 times the potential at its wall, so a charge inside is pushed toward the wall.

And it shows up in the chemistry

This is in every atom in section 3: the solver uses the true sphere-averaged pull, k(s/r)/r, at every radius, where in 3D it would be just 1/max(r, s). And it matters. Outside a shell, k makes the inner electrons' cloud push less than the same charge at the centre would, so inner electrons only partly screen the nucleus. The outer electrons feel more of it, and more as the atom gets heavier. Here are the noble gases, each one's grip on its outermost electron, solved twice: once with the true pull, and once as if the shell theorem held.

The same fact means a neutral 4D atom isn't neutral from outside. Ours, seen from beyond its cloud, has no field at all: the cloud cancels the nucleus exactly. A 4D atom still pulls on a passing electron, with a potential −Σr² / 8s³ (Σr² summed over its electrons), because the cloud never quite cancels. Matter built from such atoms would be stickier than ours in ways this page doesn't attempt to work out.

A small fourth dimension

The way real physics gets 1/r from four dimensions is to make the fourth one small: a circle of circumference ℓ. Close to a charge (r ≪ ℓ) the field spreads in four directions and falls as 1/r². Far away it has filled the circle, can only spread in three, and falls as 1/r. That's how extra dimensions could hide in our world. But it doesn't give us 4D chemistry. Atoms much bigger than ℓ are 3D atoms, and atoms much smaller are back to having no ground state.

And light?

Lumen⁴ worked out 4D light from ordinary 4D Maxwell: three polarizations, light fading as 1/r³, and a wake behind every flash. Lumen⁴ and ATOM⁴ can't both describe the same world unless the force between charges isn't carried by Lumen⁴'s light. With a vacuum whose permittivity grows with wavenumber, light disperses like deep-water waves (ω ∝ √k), and long radio waves outrun short ones without limit. With the covariant 1/k³, light has no particle at all, just a continuum, and its probabilities stop adding to one. With the small dimension, long-wavelength light is our 3D light. That is the big problem: Coulomb's 1/r isn't a free choice. In 3D, locality, linearity, relativity and positive probabilities together force it. In 4D they force 1/r², and atoms can't exist. To keep 4D atoms you have to give up one of those four.

7 · How far to trust it

What's exact: hydrogen in both worlds, the shell sizes, the sphere-averaged kernel k(t), and everything in section 6. What's approximate: Hartree's method treats each electron as moving in the averaged cloud of the others. It leaves out exchange (the Pauli principle's push between electrons of the same spin), so it underbinds, and it averages every open shell into a sphere. That's why it misses chromium and copper, which half-fill and fill their 3d shell by taking an electron from 4s, an exchange effect. Helium's total energy comes out −2.8617 hartree, the exact Hartree–Fock value (the true one is −2.9037). Neon's is −126.4 to Hartree–Fock's −128.5. Ionization energies suffer most, about a third too low across the p block. In 4D the solver finds a few transition metals (elements 64 to 67) giving up their outer s electrons for the d shell, as palladium does in ours. Those are the least trustworthy entries on the page. We also leave out relativity (it matters beyond about Z = 50 here, as with gold's colour in ours) and spin's effects, and treat spin as two states per orbital, as in 3D. A 4D table built with exchange might move a few transition metals' configurations. We expect the main-group skeleton, the period lengths and the noble gases, to stand.
